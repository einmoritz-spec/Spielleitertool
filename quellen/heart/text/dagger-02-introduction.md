#### Introduction

## A Little Backstory,
## As A Treat
Here’s what everyone knows: about a century ago, the aelfir of Spire embarked on the grand folly of the Vermissian – a network of trains connecting the districts and dizzying heights of their vertical city. The stations and the overall design were aelfir work, aelfir ambition. The suffering labour force: mostly drow. The aelfir recruited a cabal of human retro-engineers to tap into the only source of power that could make the whole scheme work – the living Heart beneath the city.

They forged a machine to cut into the Heart. And, as everyone knows (but few dare say aloud), it all went horribly wrong. Magic from the Heart gushed up through the Vermissian network, discordant unreality spilling out and staining even the most exclusive and warded districts of Spire. The attempt agitated (exacerbated? offended?) the Heart too, making it weirder and more perilous. Ever since, the havens and communities on the edge of reality have been under siege, and it gets worse every year.

As everyone knows, the whole Vermissian project was branded a failure. Shut down, sealed off, covered up, buried.

Here’s what only a handful know: it’s not over. Go deep enough, and time becomes strange. Cause no longer precedes effect. Linear time twists and deforms under arcane pressure. Possible futures bleed into uncertain pasts. The incision team are *still in there*, cutting their way through the scab tissue of dead universes, a piece of shrapnel drawn inexorably deeper into the Heart. They’ve cut a secret route into the very core.

Find that route, find that bright machine that cuts infinity. Find a shortcut to destiny.

## The Structure of
## the Adventure
*Dagger in the Heart* is divided into seven chapters. Each one’s got a bunch of landmarks and a few suggested scenes, inviting the characters onwards, deeper and deeper into the Heart.

**1.** **Trainkill:** While exploring the fringes of the Heart, the player characters learn of a buried train rumoured to contain forbidden secrets. **2.****Eastern Express:** The delvers discover what remains of the human sorcerers who helped build the Vermissian – and learn of the stillactive Delving Machine. **3.** **From Above:** Intrigue from the City Above complicates the quest, as different factions sink their talons into the delvers. Using the Map of the Heart discovered in Chapter 2, the delvers set out in pursuit of the Machine. **4.** **The Wormery:** The delvers enter the edges of the Amyrran Zone, where time runs strangely. They reach the secret Haven of the Wormery and learn more about their quarry. **5.** **The Wounded Realm:** Pursuing the Delving Machine brings the player characters deeper into the time-warped region around the Wound – and to their own future sins. **6.** **Edgelands:** Optionally, the delvers return to the City Above to gather tools for the hunt – or settle scores with some of their enemies. **7.** **A Dagger In Your Hand:** The player characters board the Delving Machine and one of them becomes – briefly – king of the City Below, able to claim their truest desire. But who reigns and who falls?

Chapters 1–3 can be mixed in with your own adventures in an ongoing *Heart* campaign; from Chapter 4 onwards, it’s pretty much non-stop until the end.

## Hooking the Player
## Characters
If you’re just starting a *Heart* campaign using this book, the best pitch to give your players is “you’re seeking answers to unutterable questions and looking for your heart’s desire” – the default ‘weirdo pals traipsing into Hell’ setup. More concrete goals like “we seek to kill the Beast” or “we’re going to make our fortune with a trading company” can work; whatever the characters desire will be found within the Heart, and the fastest way to get it is to travel to the core. Play up themes of obsession and desire. Encourage the players to create unbalanced individuals who’ll fall fast and hard.

If you’ve got an ongoing *Heart* campaign, then you know your player characters better than we do – so tell us, how do they get a sketchy rumour about a mysterious buried train?

If most of the characters are strongly connected to a faction from the City Above (the *Forced* or *Pen­* *itent* callings, for example), then consider moving Chapter 3 to the start of the campaign; instead of finding out about the Delving Machine and then getting nabbed by sinister agents from Spire, the player characters are tasked by sinister agents to gather intel about the Delving Machine.

It’s a bit trickier to use campaign setups that tie the player characters to a specific location – such as “you’re all defenders of a particular Haven” or “you’ve all inherited a spooky mansion with a trapdoor in the basement”. This adventure is a hunt, which means lots of wandering and exploration. Options are:

-  Gradually transition from “you’re defending a static base” to “you’re searching for a way to the core” by having some problem or mystery develop over time. Maybe the original owner of the mansion was connected to the Vermissian Incident, or the Haven the characters are protecting needs some exotic resource that can only be found in the core.
-  Connect the location to the overarching story

– maybe it’s part of the Vermissian network, or there’s a Gywnn-Ardasi ghost haunting the place.
-  Rapidly transition by having the Opposition stomp the Haven into the ground, and now it’s a story of vengeance.

### Condensed Statblocks
For minor foes without many special features, we’re using a condensed version of the regular NPC statblock. **Resistance** measures how much stress the foe can take before being defeated; **Protection’s** subtracted from incoming stress. If the foe’s Difficulty is other than **Standard**, that’s noted. Finally, we give the foe’s usual attacks.

### Landmarks from the
### Heart rulebook
Any of the landmarks from Heart can be dropped into this campaign. The various Vermissian stations are especially juicy, as is anywhere connected to the Moon Beneath. Places like Wardstone Nasonov (*Heart*, p. 163) or the Harvest Bazaar (*Heart*, p. 158) work well along the edges of the Amyrran Zone.

## Factions, Not Individuals
Each of the three foes commands an organisation, described as follows.

**Allies & Assets:** A list of the key resources the organisation can draw on.

**Schemes:** Things the organisation might do to improve its position.

Schemes are rated using the same scale as beats (minor/major/zenith). Two successful minor schemes let the bad guys attempt a major scheme; two successful major schemes unlocks the possibility of a zenith scheme. Finish a zenith scheme, and the organisation’s on the verge of victory.

Assume each faction attempts two schemes per chapter of the campaign. Schemes can happen ‘off-screen’, and only show up in the campaign when the characters hear rumours or encounter the fallout from a scheme.

If the delvers take steps to gather information about an organisation (spying, recruiting informants, divinations), then they may get advance warning of a scheme that the organisation’s currently involved in and take action to thwart it. There’s always advance warning of a zenith scheme.

**Countermeasures:** Actions the organisation takes to strike back at the player characters, again organised into minor and major tiers. Assume that the company takes a countermeasure of a particular tier if the characters thwart a corresponding scheme.

*For example, if the delvers stop one of Ptolemy Bay’s* *smuggled weapons shipments (a minor scheme),* *then he retaliates with a minor countermeasure (for* *example, he might arm the delvers’ enemies with* *advanced weapons).*

You can also use countermeasures in response to other actions taken by the player characters, or drop them in as fallout (**Fortune** fallout makes the most sense, but **Supplies** or **Echo** could also work) , or just use them as inspiration for ways to hurt the delvers. Because the thing about bad guys is that they don’t need a reason to hurt the player characters.

### Other Villains

If you’ve already established some recurring bad guys in your campaign, or the player characters’ backstories contain some rival or old enemy, tie them to one of the opposition factions. That bastard aelfir you hate? He’s

Aramos’ beloved nephew. That delver who left you for dead in the bowels of the earth?

Ptolemy Bay hired him.

### Choosing Your Opposition
Bad guys work best as foils for your players and player characters. If your PCs are all gun-heavy combat monsters – Hounds, Vermissian Knights, Cleavers – then Ptolemy Bay offers the most scope for direct confrontations. If your players enjoy intrigue and the backstory of Spire, then Aramos likely works well. The One Who Waits is best suited to groups focussed on mysticism and weirdness.

Another consideration – which bad guy works with your GMing style? You want the players eager and determined to beat the Opposition. If you can do an excellent supercilious rich bastard, then Aramos deserves added consideration.

A third consideration – timing. The One Who Waits works best for shorter games, Bey for mediumlength games, and Aramos for long campaigns.

It may be useful to think of the Opposition factions in game terms. Bay’s a rival player character – he’s got the ambition, the weapons, the tragic backstory, and he tends towards the direct approach. Aramos is more like a gamesmaster, pulling strings, manipulating events, setting up dramatic twists and scenes. The One Who Waits is part of the setting, an incarnation of the Heart’s darkness, horror and twisted desire.

## Ptolemy Bay, the Arms Manufacturer
You’ve got a bullet with his name on it. Or his mark, anyway, an arcane sigil that looks nothing like a P or a B. Bay’s human, but he’s lived in Spire all his life. He claims to have risen from the gutters through hard graft and ingenuity, and now he’s an industrialist and weapons dealer – and a devotee of Azur, the Trade God. He’s connected to various human politicians and factions in the City Above, and his furious drive has made him wealthy and powerful in the surface world – but his interest lies *below*.

Bay’s secret technique is using the fulminous, chaotic magic of the Heart to power his weapons. He bottles chaos to make guns that shoot the wrath of nameless gods. Such weapons can only be constructed *in* the Heart, so Bay has workhouses and factories in Derelictus and beyond, in the semi-stable zone.

These factories never last long, between the dangerous nature of the work and Bay’s prolific use of reality-corroding magic. But there’s always another Haven to turn into a foundry, always another gang of beggars and urchins to be forced into service.

He sells these weapons to… well, that depends on your campaign. If your characters are a bunch of heroic revolutionaries, then Bay supplies the oppressive authorities with specialised guns and bombs. If your characters are veterans of the gnollish wars, then Bay might be illegally supplying the gnolls of Al’Marah in their war with the aelfir; conversely, if your player characters are gnolls, then Bay’s weapons are employed by the aelfir forces.

## The One Who Waits In Ashes
**This is the nature of gods:** they are prisoners of their own essence, their own divine purpose. The Moon Goddess is chained to paths as predictable as any celestial orbit; Incarne the Debt God is as remorseless and rulebound as any tax collector. The gods are without free will; they do only as they must.

**This is the nature of the Heart:** it takes its shape and purpose from those who enter into it. It is nothing without its supplicants and delvers. All that exists here was brought from outside, in thought if not in substance. It is a nightmare of chaos because of the jangled, discordant desires of every delver who entered this place. The Heart cannot conceive of the new; it can copy, and warp, and mock, but not imagine. The Heart is without free will; it does not act, only reacts.

**This is the nature of mortals:** we are scared, mewling scraps of meat and magic. Our nature is fear and lust, we cower and covet. Without our worship, the gods would fade away to mere abstractions; without our desires, the Heart would lie empty and silent. We have free will; we can choose our path through this garden of thorns.

**Know, then, the cult of The One Who**

**Waits In Ashes:** nothing is to be worshipped, nothing desired, nothing done.

#### The Opposition

They hold that the only escape from suffering and fear is to utterly sublimate one’s one thoughts and desires into service of the One. Only when all mortal thought and desire is perfectly aligned with the thoughts and desires of The One Who Waits In Ashes will they become The One Who Arises In Glory, God of the Heart. When there is only one desire, the Heart *must* grant it.

So: a nihilist cult of personality, centred around a mysterious veiled prophet. Cult cells operate by pretending to be a temple of some other god, like the Moon Goddess, and then slowly subvert the locals until they succumb to the influence of The One Who Waits In Ashes. (And if that doesn’t work, then Plan B is ‘kill everybody and everything in the Heart’. If the cult are the only ones left, then they win by default.)

In the City Above, and in Derelictus, the cult is known as the Cult of the Hungry Deep (see The Church of Absolution, *Spire* p. 125). The surface-cult and the cult below no longer share quite the same beliefs – the surface-cult is devoted to the worship of universal decay and despair. The cult below holds that universal decay is a necessary phase: once every consciousness in all the worlds has succumbed to despair and surrendered their will to The One Who Waits In Ashes, when there is only one desire, one hope, there will be redemption.

The cult leader, The One Who Waits In Ashes, is an ambiguous entity. The One’s generally believed to be a veiled woman, but has appeared in other forms. It’s not entirely clear if the One is a person, or a role, or a spirit that possesses cult members, or some other entity altogether. Some even suspect it’s the Heart trying to understand itself. The One dwells deep in the time-warped Amyrran Zone, at the Throne of Ash (p. 107).

### The One’s Secret
If The One’s the big villain of your campaign, then it’s an alternate-timeline incarnation of one of the player characters behind the veil. The alternate character wants the same thing as the player character once did – but is prepared to kill the other delvers and everyone else in the Heart to get it.

So, the question is, *which player character is it*? Don’t answer this right away. Keep the identity of The One in flux at the start of the campaign. Then, over the course of play, look for important decisions made by the player characters – especially ones where there isn’t a consensus across the group. The One Who Waits is one of the player characters who got overruled, or had doubts, or wished things went otherwise. Whittle the number of prospective candidates down until you know which character is The One.

Then, start seeding connections and references to that character in encounters with The One’s minions. Some options:

-  Close associates of the character (family or friends) suffer some random, terrible misfortune and are drawn into the worship of the Dark.
-  A loved one of the character shows up as a ghost bound by The One Who Waits.
-  Magical entropy envelops the character: wherever they go, things rot and decay and collapse as everything falls towards the gravity well of the Heart.
-  Some of the character’s trivial possessions show up as sacred relics in a temple taken over by the Cult.
-  Cultists of the Hungry Deep hint that the singular desire of The One Who Waits is connected to the player characters – and that they are obstacles in the path of transcendence. If they would only give up and succumb to death or numbing despair, the world will be healed.

A key question: What does The One want? Base their goals on those of the player character – is The One willing to burn the world for the thing the character wants? Or is The One’s desire drawn from the player character’s suppressed or denied impulses, something they cannot admit they want?

## Worshippers of the Deep
The scary thing about the Cult of the Hungry Deep is that *anyone* could be a secret cultist. Any non-player character the delvers encounter could turn out to be a secret servant of The One Who Waits In Ashes. By a foulness shall ye know them – there are signs to spot such hidden servitors. Those Chosen by the Hungry Deep are avatars of rot and despair, hollowing out their surroundings. This corruption takes many forms, from flies and rust and fungal growths and skin lesions to institutional failure, bribery, or a loss of ideals and drive. Nothing matters, nothing lasts, everything’s going to be devoured by the dark.

So, take full advantage of the cult’s countermeasures to ‘flip’ NPCs to the service of the cult – but where possible, hint at the corruption before betraying the players. So, if you decide to make a formerly friendly guide into a cultist let someone make a **Discern+Religion** test just before they guide the party into the maw of some beast, to hear them muttering prayers to the Deep under their breath.

As described in *Spire* (p. 125), the cult has a cathedral in Derelictus led by a fallen aelfir, **Mother Falling-** **Ash-On-Snow** (p. 122). The cult’s also corrupting other temples throughout the Heart – in this book, they’ve got their hooks into the churches of Our Glorious Lady at the bridge of Tollembrood (p. 39) and the healing shrine at Avallone (p. 78).

Others serve the Hungry Deep unconsciously and unwittingly, their despairing steps guided by unseen forces. Chief among them is the **Ashen Pilgrim**, an emaciated figure that wanders forever through the endless corridors of the Heart. The Ashen Pilgrim has forgotten its name. Excalibur Carter (p. 60) or Ysella Blake (p. 83) might know it, for the Pilgrim was once one of their comrades, made immortal like them in the depths of the Amyrran Zone.

The Pilgrim cannot die and has had countless aeons of combat experience. Basically, she’s a Terminator looking for meaning – and, having failed to find it anywhere else, is now looking in the entrails of everyone unlucky enough to encounter her.

## Broken Futures
Crawl through pitch-black tunnels where anything might be lurking around the next corner. Clarity measured by the range of your weapon, chaos bubbling under the world and under your skin. The only sure thing is that you will die down here in the Heart.

*Heart* is a game designed for hard-living adventurers burning quick and bright in the dark, a game where the players get to decide on their own destinies through beats, a game where you throw a dozen wild elements into a blender and spit out your own version of the red wet heaven.

In short, *Heart* doesn’t exactly lend itself to planned adventures, but there is a clear structure to the game. No matter who the characters are, no matter where they go or what opposition they face, they’re always seeking the Heart’s core – and most of them are going to die. This campaign builds on that, presenting a possible route into the Heart, obstacles to overcome and perils to encounter en route. Having an ultimate destination and a series of landmarks and plot beats in mind lets you foreshadow encounters and build themes. Don’t think of this as a script; think of it as a map that your players can explore as they are drawn inexorably towards that final journey into the core.

### Navigating the Landmarks
We’ve included sample delves linking some of the landmarks, but these are not the only routes available. The labyrinths of the Heart wind on forever, and there are many ways to get from one place to another. Drop hints and references to nearby landmark. Let the players pick where they’re heading next.

### Entangle The Player Characters
While we’ve pointed out some potential connections, we don’t know your players. Wherever possible, adapt existing non-player characters and locations from this book to your player characters’ needs and backstories. For example, if a character’s backstory involves weird experiments, then those weird experiments took place at the Black Casern (p. 42), or were conducted by Tython Ardasi (p. 69). The more you enmesh your characters in the web of connections, the better – especially when you can link two player characters to the same location or character in different ways. “We both got experimented on in the same lab” is juicy; “hang on, you bastard, *you* were a researcher in the lab where they experimented on *me*” gets downright bloody.

### Broken Time
At the midpoint of the campaign, the player characters enter into a zone of the Heart where time is especially warped. It’s called the Amyrran Zone**1***.* The delvers might take a day to travel from a Haven to a landmark and back again, and find that a year has passed in the Haven while they were gone. Tips for dealing with these time distortions:

**1.** **Keep the player characters together.** Avoid having the party end up on different sides of a temporal discontinuity. Actually, let’s rephrase that. *Be careful* of having the party end up on different sides of a temporal discontinuity. To take the example above, what happens if one of the player characters stays in the Haven and experiences a year of time while only a day passes for the rest? Sometimes, this sort of time jump works perfectly well (“hi guys! You’ve got to meet my new family! I settled down and had a life here while you were away!”), but it can cause problems (“well, I’m going to spend that year chasing down our enemies and exploring the Heart. Why should I wait for the rest of ‘em?”). It’s much less of a headache for the GM if the player characters are all on the same time track.. **2.****Make them unpredictable.** This advice holds for any big magical effect – don’t allow the players to exploit it to the point of dull routine. Clever tricks are great, but when they become rote and start sapping fun from the rest of the game, put an end to the distortion and move on. **3.** **Time isn’t linear:** The player characters may run into future (or past) versions of themselves. However, destiny in the Heart doesn’t run true. An alternate version of a character has taken a different path through life, and while their past may echo that of the player character, there isn’t a one-to-one correspondence. In short – you can’t interrogate your future self to get spoilers about events to come, because their version of history is similar to yours, but different in key respects.
