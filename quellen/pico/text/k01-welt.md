## CORE CONCEPTS {farbe=#3b413f}
1. Everyone is bugs. All characters in PICO are bugs,
and probably pretty cute. That doesn’t mean bad things
don’t happen, but that they’re going to be tiny in scope
for the most part.
2. It’s a huge world. The counterpart to tiny bugs, the
world of PICO is massive - not just in terms of the area
there is to explore, but in relative size. Things that bugs
make are bug-sized, but things from the old world are
sized for humans (or sometimes even bigger).
3. There are no humans left. Is PICO post-apocalyptic?
Most definitely. Cans, boxes, houses, and numerous
small objects have survived. It wasn’t a violent
apocalypse - there’s no nuclear fallout to deal with,
or hideous mutants. Humans are just... gone. Maybe
there’s a skeleton somewhere but, as far as the bugs
are concerned, those are basically just a part of the
landscape.
4. Frogs are scary. Other things are scary too. The
world of PICO is a dangerous one for travellers, but
pretty safe in societies where bug-eating animals are
kept at bay. There should be wonder and danger in
equal measure.
5. Picotitans are the safest way to travel. When
the world seems too big to tackle, get a bigger friend
to help you out. Picotitans are things like cats, foxes,
moles, or rabbits that bugs can befriend and ride,
sometimes making little huts on their back or head to
stay safe. Picotitans won’t fight bugs for you - they’ve
been trained not to, after all - but they might fight other
titanic creatures, or scare away other threats.
6. Cute and weird rule the day. Weird is always good in
a Wild Words game, but here it’s tempered by cute. The
characters, threats, and elements of the world can get
strange, sometimes even scary, but there's an adorable
underpinning to most situations.
7. The aim of the game is to solve tiny problems.
What’s a tiny problem? A village being attacked by a
squirrel, or a farm needing extra help with the harvest,
or rescuing a lost explorer. The hazards and challenges
of PICO shouldn’t revolve around killing, but rather
finding creative ways to solve problems. This doesn’t
mean no violence, but it *does*  mean no cruelty.

## THE MYSTERIOCALYPSE
Where did all the humans go?
Don’t know. I don’t think it matters that much, really.
They were definitely there for a good long while, which
is an impressive achievement for things that were so
staggeringly big and monumentally slow-moving. You’d
have thought they’d get bored at some point and go
away. That might even have been what happened.
But to give a more serious answer, it doesn’t *matter*
where all the humans went, or at least not where *I*  think
they went. The world of PICO is set out in this book, to
an extent, but it’s also a world you’ll be making together
at the table - if ‘where did all the humans go’ is a
question you want to answer as part of your game, feel
free to do just that!
There are some things mentioned later that might give
a few hints. The figure relaxing in front of the Shining
Mountain on page 293 is most definitely a skeleton, for
example... Someone that died while watching TV. And
whatever happened, it only seems to have affected
humans. Maybe that’s a mystery to solve.
But really, the end of all humanity that PICO posits is
just to get the giants out of the way, a chance for the
smaller types to have their moment in the spotlight.
# THE WORLD OF PICO
Let me tell you a story.
There was a night, many, many years ago, when everything ended. Bugs aren’t that great at writing things down,
so nobody really thought to record the particulars of the event, but the effects were lasting; no more pesticides, no
more deforestation, no more fish hooks or bait boxes or flypaper. Pets ran free from newly quiet houses, and roach
motels showed nothing but vacancies.
There’s probably a lesson to be learned somewhere there. Something about the fleeting fragility of existence, a
treatise on impermanence, and what we might leave behind.
But, as stated, bugs aren’t that great at writing things down. They’re even worse at existential musing, as a rule.
So the biggest change that most bugs noticed that night was that it suddenly got a lot quieter, especially near those
black stone ribbons they’d learned not to cross, and the immense above-ground warrens full of beeping machines
and smells so succulent they were almost impossible to resist.
The world hadn’t suddenly become safe - there were still stoats to contend with, and lizards, and snappy little plants.
A bug might still meet their end on the beak of a bird, or (if they were particularly unlucky in the lottery of life) under
the playful claws of a feline deity. But it had become *safer* , and though bugs may not be great at recording history,
they’re pretty damn good at making the best of whatever situation they find themselves in.
So bugs, on the whole, flourished.
And the world opened up to them in return.

## THEMES
PICO may not have answers to every mystery it
presents, but it definitely has a few themes. I’ll list them
here, in no particular order. Let’s start with...
### COMMUNITY
The world of PICO is massive, especially when you’re
playing as a character that’s probably no more than an
inch high. Travelling across a garden might take hours,
and a stroll through the forest might be a weeks-long
endeavour. Going it alone is a good way to get eaten, so
bugs tend to band together whenever they can. There
are lone wolves and hermits, as you might expect, but
they’re the exception - as a rule, the more people you
can rely on, the safer you’ll be. Get out there and make
some chitinous friends!
### CREATIVITY
PICO is built using the Wild Words Engine, which means
(among other things) that the narrative comes first.
You describe what you’re trying to do, or what your
intentions are, and *then*  the rules come into play.
This comes into its own when the bugs have a problem
to solve. It’s not just that there’s no one way to succeed
at something, but that there’s usually no right way - or
every way is right, perhaps. When the bugs come face
to face with a toad barbarian, maybe they pelt it with
stones, or brandish tiny swords. Maybe they run for the
hills, spinning up dust-storms in their wake. Maybe they
cook it a hasty meal to distract it, or baffle it with riddles,
or try to make friends with it, or just sic a nearby fox on
it and watch the ensuing carnage. As a rule, interesting
approaches should garner interesting results.
### ADVENTURE
It comes with the territory. Literally.
There’s a whole world out there to explore, and doing so
is going to come with its fair share of challenges. That
garden we mentioned? Maybe the locals live in fear of
a terrifying feathered beast, the Starling-By-Morning,
and need some help scaring it away. Maybe a slug left
their favourite bottlecap under the gazebo, and is willing
to pay fresh mushrooms to anyone that can go and get
it for them. Perhaps there’s a conflict between two tiny
societies, or an incoming storm that a village needs to
bolster itself against, or a spider that’s spun a map into
their web that they swear leads to an ancient treasure.
As bugs, your job is to explore the world and face the
challenges it throws at you. Even the simplest journey
can be a grand adventure with the right mindset.
## INFLUENCES
Nothing lasts forever, but some things leave one hell of
an impression.
PICO wouldn’t exist if I’d never read the Discworld books,
by Terry Pratchett, which taught me that weird fiction
could take itself less seriously from time to time without
sacrificing heart and meaning.
There’s also very little chance I’d have spent this long
making a game about bugs if I’d never played Hollow
Knight. And who knows, maybe Silksong will be out by
the time this goes to print? *Note: It is, but only just!*
In terms of mechanics, the Wild Words Engine that this
game runs on wouldn’t have been possible without
influence (and occasional nuts-and-bolts course
correction) from the following cavalcade of video games
and TRPGs.
+ Sunless Sea (and the StoryNexus Engine)
+ Heart, the City Beneath (by Rowan, Rook & Deckard)
+ 13th Age (and the Rogue Elements podcast)
+ Blades in the Dark (and the Six Towers Gang)
+ Tunic, Hyperlight Drifter, and the Legend of Zelda
## TONE & CONTENT
PICO is designed to be cute, to be fun, to be reasonably
wholesome and, most importantly, not to be taken too
seriously. Ideally, I would stop this section here and let
you delve deeper into the book with reckless, page-
devouring abandon.
But as we mentioned in the ‘themes’ section, PICO is a
game about adventure and exploration in a world that’s
much bigger than the characters you play as. There’s
potential danger there - the sting of loss, the fear of
death, and the special kind of heartache brought on
by the betrayal of a friend. These aren’t all things that
a bug must experience, but they are things that a bug
might experience.
So before you play, talk to your table. Get together as a
group. Do you want your chitinous camaraderie to have
a darker edge to it, or are you going to immerse yourself
in a warm bath of cute? As long as you’re all on the
same page, you should be good.
Oh, and there’s a snail smoking a cigarette on page
199. He’s not doing it because he’s cool though, so it’s
probably alright.

### 11 {farbe=#494344}
## BEING A BUG
As a human (presumably, anyway), you may have
idly wondered what it would be like to be a bug. Well,
consider this a crash course in everything insectile,
arachnid, and unusually slimy.
### BUGS DON’T DIFFERENTIATE {farbe=#3b413f}
Starting off with a lie probably isn’t the best way to do
things, but it’s what I’m going with. Bugs obviously
*do*  differentiate, as the differences between, say, a
tarantula and a caterpillar are pretty striking. But what
they *don’t*  do is assume character based on physical
form. Scorpions may have a reputation for ferocity, but
most of them are absolute sweethearts. Pillbugs might
seem cute and harmless, but the most famous bounty
hunter in the world is three of them standing on each
other's shoulders wearing a single jacket between them.
Remember the golden rule - just because someone
looks like they might eat me, doesn’t mean they’re
actually going to.
### BUGS MAKE GREAT FRIENDS
Make friends with a bug and you’ll have a companion for
life - small types stick together.
Since the Mysteriocalypse gave the world a bit of
a shake-up, bugs have roamed farther and wider
than ever before. But they rarely do it alone - diverse
adventuring groups are an increasingly common
sight, bugs from different families banding together to
explore, solve problems, and seek out tasty treats.
### BUGS ARE RESILIENT AS ALL HELL
You might think it’s the shells, but you’d be wrong. Well,
partially. According to the leading voices in bug science,
bugs are extremely difficult to kill, or even seriously
harm, because ‘we’re just made that way, better not
question it’. While this may be a failure of the scientific
method, it’s true nonetheless. Bugs can take a lot of
knocks before they get sent down for the count, and
give them a bit of sugar water and they’re liable to
bounce right back up again. Especially bees.
What bugs aren’t is invincible. Bigger threats, especially
those with a taste for scuttling sorts, hit with the kind
of force that even a bug has difficulty withstanding. The
good news is, other bugs can hit back just as hard if the
situation is right, especially if they all work together.
### BUGS ARE INGENIOUS
This one has been true since the first bug flopped out
of the briny sea and decided to give this whole 'air
breathing' thing a go. It's a natural instinct for almost
all bugs to not only adapt to their environment, but
to adapt their environment to them. Some hunt by
weaving elaborate trapdoors, others use their own
moulted shells as natural distractions or ornamentation,
and a whole load of them can look at two disparate bits
of trash and forge them together into a workable tool.
And with the smorgasbord of bits and bobs scattered
around the post-Mysteriocalypse world, that latter lot
have really been given the opportunity to shine.
### BUGS ARE NATURALLY INQUSITIVE {farbe=#3b413f}
Even the most sedate stick insect experiences a brief
flutter of passion at the sight of new vistas, or has to
hide their enthusiasm for a particularly well-told story
of distant shores. There’s something baked into the
average bug brain that drives them to want to learn
more about the world they live in, and the experiences
they can gain from it. It may not be universal, but it’s
common enough to mention it here - the only thing
most bugs love more than being presented with a
problem to solve is the satisfaction in knowing that
they’ve solved it.
### BUGLY NAMES
Each bug has two names - the one they use when
they’re referring to themselves casually, and the one
that describes *exactly*  what they are. *You'll find more*
*about names on page 168, but here's a brief overview...*
CHOSEN NAMES: There aren’t many naming
conventions for a bug to follow outside of whatever
they think sounds cool. You might travel with Lit Von
Chucker, bottlecap golfer extraordinaire, and then sit
down for a picnic dinner with Long Dave.
PSEUDOSCIENTIFIC NAMES: These are created from
multiple small sound segments that snap together
like legally distinct plastic building blocks, each part
referring to the particulars of a bug's anatomy (and
sometimes the things they habitually carry around).
Lit Von Chucker up there is technically a ‘hard-shelled
small-eyed quadrapod’, or ‘Anthotoriki Toxromoteva’ if
you want to get technical.

## THE SIZE OF LIFE
You'll find references to size are pretty common throughout PICO. I mean, it's right there in the name - it's a game
about tiny things! But even in a world where (almost) everything is tiny, *relative*  size still matters; one bug might
struggle to wade through a pile of waist-high dust while their companion steps over it without breaking their stride.
With this in mind, we've given the rest of this page over to a size comparison chart, stacking the kind of bugs you'll
be playing as against various food items to help with your theatre of the mind.
NANO: Somewhere between a granule of sugar and a grain of rice.
This covers everything from ticks to ants, ladybugs to aphids. Nano bugs are small enough that even things
appropriately sized for the scuttling world might be a tad too big for them, but often have the power and gumption
to get by just fine regardless. *Speaking pseudoscientifically, some bugs are so small that a human eye (if there were*
*any of those around) would barely be able to see them, like dust mites and bacteria. They technically fit in the nano*
*category too, but don't tend to interact with buggish society.*
SMALL: Somewhere between the size of a blueberry and a sprout.
This encompasses the average dimensions of a vast majority of bugs, being about the size where one could wield a
bottlecap as a shield and a sewing needle as a sword if they so wished. Bumblebees, wasps, various garden beetles,
and earthworms are all good examples. *If someone is talking about a bug and they don't specify the size, it's usually*
*safe to assume that they're small.*
LARGE: Somewhere between the size of a post-shrinkflation candy bar and a can of off-brand soda.
Now these are *big*  bugs, likely the biggest most of the denizens of the world of PICO would ever hope (or want) to
see. Heftier butterflies and very hungry caterpillars weigh in at the smaller end of this category, while tarantulas,
centipedes, and the occasional hercules beetle occupy the upper end. *Still tiny compared to a cat or a dog, but*
*giants in the scuttling world; large sized bugs sometimes have trouble moving around in settlements that aren't*
*made to accommodate them.*
HUGE: Maybe the size of a packet of bacon, or a carton of twelve eggs.
Usually reserved for the non-scuttling denizens of the world of PICO, like toads,
stoats, and pangolins. *But are there huge bugs out there too? Most*
*definitely, but they're usually the product of weird nuclear*
*stuff, or from learning to be predator rather than prey*
*where beasts are concerned.*
TITAN: Even bigger than a roast chicken.
Not actually pictured on this page because it*literally*
*would not fit* , the titan size is almost exclusively
reserved for larger beasts (like cats and foxes).
*There are rumours that bugs were once this*
*size, but... Well, let's hope those days are*
*long past for the good of everyone involved.*

## ADVENTURING BANDS & REASONS TO ROAM
As we mentioned before, bugs tend to be naturally inquisitive sorts. And, with a world so wide and vast and strange
spreading out in all directions, can you really blame them?
So for every few bugs that decide to settle down and carve out a place to call their own, there's another driven by the
inescapable urge to *wander* . For this second sort, the horizon isn't just a distant vista, it's an invitation - a challenge.
Just how far can you go, and what might you see when you get there? What tales will you take home when you're
done, if you ever head home at all?
This feeling, this deep and beautiful wanderlust, is the drive behind the formation of adventuring bands: groups
of bugs pulled together by the joys of travel, and forged into better versions of themselves by the challenges and
opportunities that their journeys provide. And for some of these bands, life really is that simple - you follow the road,
or head off of the beaten path, and turn your life into the kind of story that a bard would sing about.
... Or, at least, that's how things would be in an ideal world.
So, for a more balanced view, let's take a look at a few of the other reasons bugs head out into the uncertain dangers
of the post-Mysteriocalypse, and what they may hope to achieve while they're out there - some reasons to roam.
*The reasons on the right are only a handful of common examples - who really knows why any bug does anything?*

### GETTING INTO SCRAPES
That doesn’t mean it’s going to be easy! The problems
may be tiny, but so are the folk trying to solve them.
There’s danger out there in the big wild world, the
kind that’s best faced with friends at your side and a
hastily-thrown-together plan in your brainbox. Some
encounters are physical, some social... and others
are just plain weird. But they can all be solved if you
push the right buttons, talk to the right people, and
sometimes knock a few heads together if you really
have to.
### LEARNING STUFF {farbe=#3b413f}
They say a change is as good as a rest, and while that
has very little to do with learning, I felt obligated to
shoehorn that bit of wisdom in there somewhere. Bugs
are small, but their dreams and ambitions tend to err on
the bigger side of things - learning how to achieve their
goals, and how to help others do the same, is the butter
to adventuring’s bread.
### VISITING NEW PLACES {farbe=#3b413f}
But where do you find these problems, these scrapes,
these folk from which to learn? New places, of course!
Travel broadens the mind (a quote that actually fits this
section, much better than that last one I used), and it
also brings with it a festival of challenges to face and
obstacles to overcome. For the average adventurer, a
day where you find a new place, solve some problems of
both the tiny and bigger variety, and get a little reward
at the end? That’s the best kind of day you can have.
# GOING ON ADVENTURES
*"One more step, Mr. Bofro, and I'll be the farthest from... Oh bugger, I've lost count."*
The winding road. The sun-kissed horizon. The smell of an unfamiliar dish. Adventuring is, as most bugs would tell
you, definitely *where it’s at* . But what actually *is*  an adventure, when you get right down to it?
Well, it's a chance to explore the world! To see things you haven’t seen before, to do things you wouldn’t normally
do. To take risks, and to face challenges. To learn, and to overcome tough situations alongside the rest of your
adventuring band. To make friends, and maybe even enemies. *Below you’ll find a little guidance on what your bugs*
*will likely end up doing as they traverse the world of PICO in an adventuring band, starting with...*
### SOLVING TINY PROBLEMS {farbe=#3b413f}
A band of punk locusts that keep eating the nearby
crops before they can bloom. A mole tunneling under
the foundations of an insect metropolis. A lost grub that
sniffles out a request for help.
These are tiny problems, the kinds of thing a roving
party of boisterous bugs are just the right sorts to solve.
Everywhere you travel you’ll find new tiny problems to
solve, and when you solve enough of them, you might
end up...
### SOLVING BIGGER PROBLEMS
You see, the average person just wants to live a life that
they can enjoy. Bugs want that even more than most.
The core loop of PICO focuses on just that - bringing a
measure of harmony back to places that have gone just
a little bit wrong. Most bigger problems are really a slew
of tiny problems bundled together, and once enough
of those little issues are solved, you’ll find that life
improves for locals and travelling bugs alike.

### 19 {farbe=#494344}
## ... AND THE CHANGING TIMES
Some habits may die hard, but change is the name of the game when considering the chitinous future.
Old world bugs pretty much kept themselves to themselves. Oh, they mingled, sure, and sometimes even cooperated,
but most had a hive, nest, or mound to go home to that was filled with family and very little else. Part of it was
practical, as comfortable living conditions for an ant are little more than a series of doorframe-related head trauma
opportunities for a praying mantis. But some of it was good old-fashioned self-preservation, and a somewhat
unhealthy lack of trust.
But in the bright age of friendship and camaraderie that typifies most of the modern bugly world, those insular
generational instincts have been cleaned away like so many cobwebs (sorry, spiders). Now the majority of bugs
are born in diverse scuttling societies, settlements made with the teamwork of many different clades and families
working together.
### BIG SHADE
A spiralling city that climbs up and around the outside
of an oak tree out in the true wilds, in the shadow of
the branches almost every moment of every day. Big
Shade is known for explosive and sudden local festivals,
signalled to start whenever the movement of clouds,
leaves, and branches gives them more than a few
seconds of direct sunlight - it's not unusual for a Big
Shade festival to start with zero preparation and last
less than a minute (though it's impressive how much
dancing and drinking can be done during that time).
### RIVERBOOF
A bit of a mistake, this one was. The bugs that founded it
were particularly short-sighted, and the 'river' they took
as a source of great travelling potential was actually
a large pond with a continual whirlpool in the centre.
The water runs, but it won't take you anywhere other

### CRANIOPOLIS

### LILY'S LOOK
Built on the sloping expanse of an old megastructure's

ceded some slates to the bugs in the early days, but

### THE BRASS SECTION
Heard for a hundred strides in all directions, the

### GRASP
Not a place for the faint of heart, the entire village of

## SETTLEMENTS OF PARTICULAR NOTE
Bugs get everywhere, but they don't *stay*  everywhere. For bugs to make a place their home, there usually needs to be
something special about it - an existing defensive structure, a useful natural resource, or even something as simple
as an incredible view. Here are a few settlements that have sprung up in the years since the Mysteriocalypse, the
kind that bugs would pass rumours of around a campfire as the night draws in.

### TO LEARN

While this doesn't sound quite as fun as the other options, it's definitely a good idea. Preparing before a long journey
might come in the form of trading away older bits and bobs for fresh new ones, kitting yourself out with special
tools that will help on the road ahead, or hiring a picotitan to make longer, more difficult journeys significantly easier.

## FROGS
And toads. Maybe even axolotls, too.
Amphibious and hungry, even the friendliest-looking
frog should be treated with caution. It's a food chain
thing, you see - bugs ARE delicious, and frogs ARE

## MICE
Now this is a curious one. Some mice are intellectual

## DOGS
Ah, the boundless innocence of canine life. To dogs,

## RACCOONS
Back in the pre-Mysteriocalypse times, raccoons had

## BATS

# NOT-BUGS
*“Variety's the very spice of life. And cinnamon of death, if you ask me. Can't stand the stuff.”*
With so many different kinds of bugs in the world, you'd be forgiven for assuming that they're the default. It's a
veritable world of bugs, after all - surely there's not space for anything else?
Well, hypothetical ruminator, that's where you'd be wrong.
While the characters in the world of PICO are bugs, and most of the NPCs that players will engage with will likely be

The rest of this page is given over to a brief overview of some of the 'big players' in the world of PICO that aren't bugs.
None of them are inherently evil - this isn't that kind of game - but some of them *are*  inherently dangerous, just due
to the essential reality of a bug's size or habits (and possibly their tastiness).

# CATS
*Of course they get a special section of their own.*
Cats deserve a special mention here, as the apex predators and undisputed overlords of the world of PICO. There
are bigger beasts, and there are faster beasts, and there are even smarter beasts, but there's nothing that's bigger,
faster, and smarter than a cat.
Cats are important enough to get a few subheadings, so we'll throw those down below.
General advice? Don't underestimate them.
Specific advice? *Run* .rthy of worship, but cats are not, in fact, divine.
### CATS GO WHERE THEY WILL
Think you're safe underground, or in a tree? Guess
again. Cats roam the world of PICO without any sense
of bugly boundary or border - the entirety of the post-
Mysteriocalypse is their territory, and only complex
deals made with other cats stay their travelling paws.
### CATS KNOW EVERYTHING
Okay, they don't know *literally*  everything. But cats can
remember the time before the Mysteriocalypse, even
if they hardly ever speak about it to anyone except for
other cats, and they hold answers to mysteries that
bugs haven't even thought about yet. If you need a
question answered, they say, ask a cat. And bring a gift.
A really impressive gift.
### CATS HAVE 99 LIVES
This one hasn't been completely proven yet, but
students of the feline complexities are very, very sure
that no cat has ever died. How does this work? No idea.
But it's definitely true that cats rarely seem to get hurt
by anything and, even if they do, they bounce back up
to full strength in a week or two.
### CATS ARE MADE OF MAGIC
It's the only explanation when you really think about it.
Their bodies *look*  like that of any other beast, with tails
and whiskers and some rather worryingly sharp claws,
but they *act*  like some sort of furry liquid. Cats turn up
in the strangest of places too, often relaxing in spaces
they shouldn't be able to fit into at heights that would
induce vertigo in all but the bravest of birds. And if
you've ever tried to keep a cat out of a particular space
you'll know that, somehow, they will always end up
exactly where you don't want them to be.
### CATS DON'T EAT BUGS... USUALLY
But don't breathe that insectile sigh of relief just yet.
Scuttling types might not be on the menu for the
average cat in terms of food, but they often fall into
the category of 'living toy' instead, which can be just as
dangerous.
### CATS HAVE ODD ATTENTION SPANS
Sometimes so long that they can watch a mousehole
from sunup to sundown, and at other times so short
they probably wouldn't even make it through this
sentence. Whether something can hold a cat's attention
very much relies on an intersection of interest and
opportunity, with a dash of influence from whether
there's a good patch of sunlight to lounge in.
### CATS ARE NOT GODS
So, this last one is to reign you in a bit after all of that
majesty. Cats are as mundane as bugs when you
get right down to it - they eat, they sleep, and they
sometimes fall off of things unexpectedly. For all of
their impressive traits and hidden secrets, they're not
infallible - they can be tricked, they can be fought, and
they can even sometimes be beaten (even if it is only
temporarily). If socialized with bugs early on, they can
even be befriended!

### 26 {farbe=#494344}
# PICOTITANS
The world of bugs is a pretty friendly one, all told. There are fights, scuffles, disagreements, all those usual things, but
most bugs get along with each other as long as they're not actively trying to eat one another.
The world of beasts? Not quite so much. Fights are more frequent, territory is wider-ranging and more aggressively
defended, and the weakest are often left to fend for themselves.
That last bit is important - what's weak for a larger beast is still extremely strong in bugly terms, and over the
uncounted years since the Mysteriocalypse groups of bugs have taken to giving these abandoned beasts a home,
teaching them that bugs are friends first and food a distant second, and using them as titanic mounts.
These picotitans, as they're called, range in size from ferret to fox, but no matter how large or small they may be to
other beasts, they're a formidable ally for any set of insect adventurers.
## HERE KITTY KITTY
It takes skill to earn an abandoned animal's trust before it flees, crushes, or eats you, but bugs are known for
their skill in the art of convincing others. Or at least, they like to think they are. In actuality, what the initial steps
of training a picotitan require more than anything else is food, specifically the kind of food that looks a lot more
appetizing than the one handing it over.
But it's not always that easy. Sometimes a beast just doesn't understand enough about tiny sorts to realize
that they're trying to help, and a little strategy - or straight-up trickery - is required.
## BEASTLY DRAWBACKS
Once you have a picotitan on side, one of the things that a lot of would-be beast
trainers forget is that it needs somewhere to live. This is less of a problem
for nomadic sorts, who move through the world on a whim, but anyone
that wants a place to hang their little hat is liable to find their house
mistakenly crushed by a playful titan within the first week.
Larger communities of bugs often have a 'stable' of sorts just
for this purpose, or are built near larger structures or natural
features left over from the pre-mystery times that trained
beasts can call their own.
## BEASTLY BENEFITS
But when all that's taken care of? Well, you're golden - a picotitan,
as long as you keep it fed and happy, can do anything from transport
messages to carry an entire castle around on its back, allow the
crossing of otherwise impassable terrain to coming to the
defence of their tiny friends in a tough situation with a wilder
beast.
The one thing they'll never do, however, is harm a bug - it's an
essential part of the process, convincing these massive beasts
not to snack on those around them, so asking it to gobble
up other bugs you don't like (or other bug-sized things) is
dangerously confusing, and best avoided.
