### 29 {farbe=#494344}
## TOLD BY WILD WORDS
PICO runs on a version of the Wild Words Engine,
tinkered with just enough that the basic principles are
the same but that it also fits a massive world of tiny
adventurers.
But what do you need to play a game of PICO? We'll get
that out of the way first, with a quick list below...
### A WEAVER (GM)
The Weaver (or, if you want to get technical, Weaver
of Very Engaging Stories Involving Bugs) is PICO's
GM figure. Why call it a Weaver, rather than just a GM?
Because bugs have no masters, but they do tend to
get caught up in the strands of stories bigger than
themselves. One person at the table takes the role of
Weaver, playing NPCs, presenting the world, and helping
to guide the story when it needs it.
### SOME BUGLY CHARACTERS (PLAYERS)
Everyone at the table is a player, when you get down to
it, but some are more player-y than others. Characters
in PICO are all bugs of various kinds, and it's these little
bugs that drive the story forward.
### SOME SHEETS (CHARACTER & ROAMING)
At the very least, every player at the table with a
character needs a Character Sheet - a way to record
information about their bug, to see what skills and
special parts they have, and to track how they develop.
You'll also need a Roaming Sheet - this is a place to
record the important features of your picotitan (if you
have access to one), and also information on the wider
world around you. The Roaming Sheet is shared by all
players, so you only need one for the table. *Though if*
*you explore large swathes of the world, or manage to*
*befriend multiple picotitans, you might actually need*
*more than one roaming sheet in the end. One's a good*
*start, let's just say that.*
### SOME DICE (D6s)
Every player at the table, and the Weaver, needs a
bundle of d6s. Six of them is a good number, but you
might want to bring a couple more just in case one rolls
under a chair or something.
It happens.

### 30 {farbe=#494344}
## JOURNEYS
The most structured of all - journeys let bugs move from place to place, encapsulating long treks, camps, and
scouting expeditions, as well as the hazards and mysteries found and faced along the way.
*Example: This beach? It's just as lonely as it first seemed, no point in hanging around for too long. The bugs are*
*going to make their way inland as night falls, hoping to reach the nearest bugly settlement in time for a good hearty*
*supper. The Weaver works out roughly how many strides the journey will take, and then puts a track down for them to*
*fill with their movement (as well as a ticking clock to measure the passing of time) - a classic journey.*
# STRUCTURE
A game of PICO follows a particular structure... Sort of. Play is broken up into three distinct types - scenes,
montages, and journeys - which each have their own rules, and can happen in pretty much any order. Two scenes
in a row? No problem! A journey, with a montage in the middle, and then another journey? Absolutely fine.
## THE SPOTLIGHT
As you might have guessed, the spotlight is a game term for when the attention of the table is on you. When the
Weaver describes a location, they have the spotlight. If a player breaks in to ask a question, the spotlight swings to
them and their bug. A raving badger charges in? That's probably a moment worthy of the spotlight, yeah.
In scenes, the spotlight swings wherever the action dictates. If things get really dramatic or hectic, the Weaver might
start tracking the spotlight to ensure everyone gets a fair shake at play (see *Who Goes Next?*  on the next page).
In montages, every bug gets the spotlight once (to do their task), and some might get it more than once (usually
because they have an ability that lets them use multiple tasks, or if whatever they were doing needs a bit of a
narrative follow-up before the conversation continues).
In journeys, the spotlight will mostly be on the Weaver as they describe the world and bring encounters into play,
but bugs will also have their moments as they make Lookout Rolls, take important decisions, and natter about
their days.
## SCENES
Scenes are pretty freeform. Scuttle around, get into
trouble, have a good time or a bit of a scrap. The
spotlight (more on that in a second) moves from player
to player freely as they take actions and reactions that
affect the world, and the Weaver will slide in now and
then to describe things, embody NPCs, ask questions,
or ask for particular rolls.
*Example: A band of bugs are exploring a curious*
*sandcastle on a lonely beach, taking their time to*
*explore each salt-scented room and picking their way*
*through hazardous collapsed areas. The moment-to-*
*moment decisions they make matter, so the Weaver*
*handles this experience with a scene.*
## MONTAGES
These are a little more structured. In a montage, every
bug gets the chance to do a single 'task', a long action
condensed down into a sentence or two of description
(and maybe a roll of the dice). This helps longer, slower
periods of in-game time pass more quickly out here in
the real world.
*Example: That same band of bugs are getting ready*
*to leave the sandcastle, having bested (and then*
*befriended) the titanic crab in its deepest chambers.*
*But before they go, each of them has something they*
*want to do; grabbing a particular type of resource,*
*maybe nursing some wounds. The tasks matter here,*
*but not the moment-to-moment play, so it's a montage.*

### 31 {farbe=#494344}
## THE CONVERSATION
It might seem silly, pointing out how important the
conversation is - I mean, you can't really play a group-
focused TRPG without one. But the conversation is
*particularly*  important in a narrative-focused game like
PICO - it's where players and Weaver get together to ask
questions, take actions, make decisions, and move the
spotlight from person to person so everyone gets some
time and attention.
Even when the spotlight isn't being explicitly tracked,
the conversation should help decide who goes next.
Someone's been a bit quiet, or not had their time to
shine in a while? Anyone at the table can help bring
them back into the story, not just the Weaver. PICO is, at
its heart, a team game, and you're all there to have fun.
It's also important to bring a couple of safety tools into
the conversation, especially early on. Mute topics that
you don't want to turn up, and make sure that everyone
is on board with the intended tone of the game - you'll
find more about that on page 193.
## WHO GOES NEXT?
In most situations, the swing of the spotlight is obvious.
One bug throws something down a deep well, and
the spotlight swings to the Weaver to describe the
satisfying splash it makes when it hits the water. Then
somebody else does something, and the spotlight
swings to them. Easy enough, right?
But in situations where time is of the essence, or where
everyone should have a fair amount of time centre-
stage, the Weaver can make a Spotlight Track on a
bit of scrap paper to see who has had the spotlight for
actions (tracked with an A) or reactions (tracked with an
R), which helps determine who should probably have it
next. Here's an example...
Darius: ARA
Celia: RRA
Uther: AR
In the above example, Darius and Celia have both had
the spotlight three times in the scene. Uther has only
had it twice, so the Weaver might prompt them to take
an action or challenge them with a reaction. If Uther
wants to hang back a bit, Celia should probably get
it next (as she's taken two reactions, which means
she's had a bit less agency than Darius with his two
actions). As long as these tracks stay *vaguely*  balanced,
everyone should get a piece of the spotlight pie!

# TRACKS
A track is a set of boxes (technically circles, but I'm not
splitting hairs here) linked together in a line. Quick, there
goes one now!
Tracks are used in a whole lot of different ways in PICO,
but the most common use is to measure steps along
the path to something happening. Because of the
'something', most tracks come with a name attached to
them, like the one below.
Think of a track as somewhere between reminder, book-
keeping aid, and abstract story. The one above tells you
that croaklins are approaching (thanks to the name),
and that five things are probably going to happen before
they get here (thanks to the number of boxes).
# o-o-o-o-o
## MARKS
Marking a track is as easy as putting a diagonal line
through one of the boxes. *Here's our five-box track, but*
*now with a single mark...*
But what does that mean? Usually that whatever the
name of track hints at is one step closer to happening.
In the case of the track above, every mark represents
the croaklins drawing ever closer.
Tracks can be marked in a variety of ways; through
successful actions, passing time, failures and missteps,
sometimes even at the Weaver's whim. But here's
something that's always true...
When a track is fully marked, *something*  happens.
*Example: The noise of Jin's shout reverberates through*
*the valley, and the Weaver marks the final box on their*
*track. Moments later, the first croaklin head appears!*

## BURNS & CLEARS
If a mark is a simple line, a burn is cross - a second line
that crosses the first, forming an x shape. *Here's a track*
*with two marks and one burn...*
Think of a burn as some kind of *super mark* , an indelible
change that's impossible (or at least really difficult)
to clear. Why is this important? Because some tracks
don't just count up! Marks can be cleared, erased from
a track to set progress back. And how do marks get
cleared? It all depends on the track!
*Example: The bugs are trying to win the affections of a*
*wily raccoon, and the Weaver sets them a track to fill to*
*represent their progress. Giving gifts and doing favours*
*mark the track, but offending or angering the raccoon*
*clears a mark.*
This is where burns come in handy, representing
progress that's probably not going to reverse.
*Example: One of the bugs gives the raccoon a*
*discarded mitten, his favourite objet d'art. The raccoon*
*is so happy with this gift that the Weaver burns a box*
*instead of marking it, to represent a positive feeling*
*toward the bugs that the raccoon will never forget.*
## BREAKS
A break in a track can be represented by a special
vertical line in between two boxes. *Here's an example of*
*a four-box track with a break halfway through.*
Breaks are a limiter on how many boxes can be filled
at once. While most tracks will be marked one box at a
time, some (such as tracks representing damage) might
take a whack of a few marks at once. If those marks
would go past a break, they stop instead - any extra
marks to be made are ignored.
Breaks can also be used to represent changes in the
world, or a hazard, even before their track is filled. Maybe
a hermit crab crawls about the place quite happily until
its track is marked up to a break, then retreats into its
shell while the rest of the track is being marked.
### - TRACKS EXAMPLE -
Weaver:	Squirrels are famously patient, but their king
won't wait forever. I'm starting a track, called
*The Hunt for the Acorn*  - it's got three boxes,
then a break, then three more.
Layla:
Let me guess - if the track gets fully marked,
the squirrels are going to come after us?
Weaver:	Oh no, that would be terrible! Actually, when
we reach the break, the squirrels are going to
come after you. When the track is fully marked,
that's when the squirrel king loses his patience
entirely and tries to track you down himself.
Bicco:
Ah... That sounds less than perfect. How do we
mark it? Or rather, how do we avoid marking it,
as this seems like the kind of track we want to
keep as clear as possible?
Weaver:	Speed is key here. I'll mark the Hunt for the
Acorn track at dawn each day, so you've
essentially got six days to get things done,
three of those without hulking monsters on
your tail.
Dunf:
Hey, that's actually pretty generous! I'm pretty
sure we can find that acorn in six days, right
folks? Maybe even three?
Layla:
And *I'm*  pretty sure we should get going! Come
on gang, it's journey time. To the Hogwild!

### TO MARK OR NOT TO MARK?
Remember, not every track is one you're going to
want to fill! A track representing the damage your bug
takes, for example, or the arrival of a rampaging titan,
is probably one you're going to want to leave as empty
as possible! *Tracks you want to avoid filling might be*
*cleared with a successful action, rather than marked.*
### OPEN AND HIDDEN TRACKS
What, you thought the Weaver was just going to tell you
everything, every time?
Most tracks in PICO are open, out in front of everyone
for the whole table to see. But some can be hidden,
where only the Weaver can see how many boxes and
marks there are. If you ever see the Weaver make a little
pencil mark and smile to themselves, there's probably a
hidden track.

# ACTIONS & DICE
*“Not only does god play dice, but he sometimes throws*
*them where they cannot be seen. Ever had a massive*
*cube just drop out of the sky near you? It'll give you*
*pause for thought, I tell you that.”*
Picture the scene: you're out on an adventure, days
away from home, and you come across a sleeping
scorpion. It's big enough that it fills almost the entire
path, and scorpions are notoriously light sleepers -
whatever you do here, you're going to risk waking it up.
You could try to sneak past, you could double back a
little and find another way to your destination, you could
wake it on purpose and try to make friends, or you could
charge in for a fight and attempt to take it off guard.
Whatever you decide, you're going to be doing what
PICO refers to as an action - a moment where the story
brings an element of skill, choice, or randomness into
play. And how do you deal with this?
By rolling some dice.
## WHEN DO YOU ROLL?
Not all actions require a roll. Going to open your own
front door under normal circumstances? Yeah, you can
probably manage that without reaching for the dice.
As a handy set of guidelines, you should roll the dice
when an action you're attempting is...
### DIFFICULT {farbe=#008a99}
*The door has been barricaded from the outside, and you*
*have to push it open with great force.*
If an action requires a lot of effort, so much that you
may or may not succeed, roll the dice to find out what
happens.
### DANGEROUS {farbe=#9f2320}
*The door has been rigged with an exploding shell -*
*somebody really doesn't like you.*
If an action's outcome might involve harm or serious
consequences, roll the dice to find out whether you
suffer them or not (and how bad they are if you do).
### DRAMATIC {farbe=#979f32}
*The door isn't the problem, it's the adoring crowd*
*waiting outside that you need to impress. Make a scene!*
If an action involves a big dramatic moment, either in-
world or as part of the game's narrative, roll the dice to
see how you fare. Stories need drama, after all!
## HOW DO YOU ROLL?
Once you've determined that a roll is required, it's time
to grab dice. PICO uses a d6 dice pool system - you
roll a number of d6s, and then you read off the highest
number as your result (and doubles matter, too).
But how many dice are you rolling?
That depends on the situation you're in and how you've
built your bug. Almost all rolls in PICO follow the same
basic structure:
### Skill + Aspect + Advantage
... Meaning that the maximum number of dice you'll
ever have in your pool is six.
The box below breaks down the three factors behind
your dice pool, but if you're ever not sure whether you
need to roll or not, or how many dice to use, check with
the Weaver. And in some instances, the Weaver will just
straight up ask you to roll!
### SKILL DICE   [up to 3d6]
### ASPECT DICE   [up to 1d6]
### ADVANTAGE DICE   [up to 2d6]
Skill dice come from the skills your bug has access to. All
bugs have a number of special skills, which can give a
bonus of 1d6, 2d6, or 3d6.
All bugs have a number of aspects, representing special
body parts and unique tools. You get a bonus of 1d6 if
you have an aspect that suits the situation.
Advantage dice are more situational. You can gain a
maximum of 2d6 advantage on your roll if you...
	» Have the environment, a POI, or a discovery working
in your favour, which can give a bonus of 1d6.
	» Have help from another nearby bug (though if things
go wrong, they'll face consequences just like you),
which can give a bonus of 1d6.
	» Use some of your bits or bobs, odds and ends you
pick up on your travels, for a bonus of 1d6. Using bits
and bobs as part of an action *might*  consume them
if a roll goes well, but will *almost*  *definitely*  consume
them if a roll goes poorly. *See page 160 for more*
*guidance on using bits and bobs.*

## READING RESULTS
So you've rolled your dice - what happens next?
There are two important takeaways from your rolled
dice. There's the result, which is the highest number
you see, and there's the twist, which you earn from
having doubles of *any*  of the numbers you see.
*Example: Celia rolls 4d6, and gets 1, 3, 4, and 6. Her*
*result is 6, and she doesn't have a twist.*
*Example: Cedrico rolls 3d6, and gets 1, 1, and 4. His*
*result is 4, but he also gets a twist from the double 1.*
Different results have different names and effects
which can vary based on the type of roll you're making -
we're focusing just on Action Rolls for now, covered by
the table to the right, but there are other kinds of rolls
you'll be making too! *You'll learn more about those later.*
When you've worked out your result, announce it to
the table! That's when the conversation takes over
again, and the game continues based on the result
you've just rolled.
### AN EMPTY POOL
If you ever want (or need) to roll when you have no way
of getting dice for the pool, roll a single d6 and count
triumphs as conflicts. *This might happen when you're*
*trying something that's way out of your comfort zone,*
*but even then you should look to scrape up some kind*
*of advantage to help out.*
## ACTION ROLL RESULTS
### TRIUMPH
Everything goes according to plan! If you're
affecting a track, this lets you mark or clear
a box (whichever makes more sense for a
good outcome in your current situation).
### CONFLICT
It's still a success, but it comes at a cost. If
you're affecting a track, you *might*  be able to
mark or clear it, but there's probably going
to be a drawback of some kind to deal with
as well.
### DISASTER
Things go about as badly as they possibly
could. If you're affecting a track, you don't
mark or clear a box, and there will definitely
be some kind of drawback to deal with.
### TWIST
Something unexpected happens, in addition
to the usual result! This extra outcome can
be suggested by anybody at the table, but
the Weaver has the final say. *See page 37 for*
*more information on twists.*

## ACTIONS VS. REACTIONS
Most of the time, an action is something you choose to do just because you want to. You're in control, and you choose
what you're doing and how you're going about it.
But, sometimes, the Weaver will ask you to make a reaction - they throw the spotlight in your direction, usually
accompanied by some kind of immediate danger, challenge, or choice, and you have to work out what you're going to
do in response. You're still in control of how you're going to get something done, but the 'what' is a little more limited
based on the context around the reaction.
*Example: Rexi is engaged in a rumble with an angry crow, the two of them trading blows as they scamper and*
*thrash among the dense branches of a bramble bush. The crow leaps forward, beak open, for an attack, so the*
*Weaver asks Rexi to make a Reaction Roll. The context of this roll is that Rexi's in danger, and should probably get out*
*of the way or find some way to blunt the crow's attack. Whatever he chooses to do for his reaction should address*
*this situation in some way - maybe dodging away, trying to block the beak with his tail, nipping behind a thorny*
*branch, or leaping right at the crow with a counterattack. All are applicable to the situation, and that's what really*
*matters.*
### APPROPRIATE REACTIONS & WILD CARDS
Mechanically, reactions work just like actions - you decide what approach you're taking, build
your dice pool, roll, and read your results off. But unlike actions, reactions aren't voluntary;
you *are*  in the spotlight, and if you don't act accordingly something bad *will*  happen.
But maybe that's alright! The spotlight is on you for a reaction, so if you see an
opportunity to get something done that doesn't quite match the situation, you can
still attempt it. It just, you know... might turn out pretty badly for you, no matter
how well you roll.
*Example: In the above situation, the Weaver expects Rexi to try to defend*
*himself in some way, or at least get a bit of damage in while the crow attacks,*
*but he doesn't have to. Rexi might use this reaction to steal a feather from*
*the crow as it savagely pecks at him, gaining a resource that might help him*
*later on down the line (if he survives). But, because this reaction doesn't*
*do anything to protect him from the incoming attack, he'll take negative*
*consequences from it as if he'd rolled a disaster, even if the rest of the*
*reaction goes well in terms of stealing that feather.*
## LUCK ROLLS
A special roll that the Weaver might call for from time to
time, when you make a Luck Roll you simply roll a single
d6. Instead of having to check a pre-made results table,
the Weaver will tell you exactly what the result means
for that particular situation (probably before the roll is
made, but you never know). How exciting!
*Example: Tambori and Felkin are facing an angry cat*
*that's about to lash out. The Weaver asks them to make*
*a Luck Roll, telling them that the higher the result, the*
*better. Tambori rolls a 3, and Felkin a 5 - Felkin's higher*
*result keeps her safe in this situation, and the cat*
*springs at the unlucky Tambori instead.*

### 37 {farbe=#494344}
# TWISTS
When a player rolls doubles, they've almost always
got the option of choosing a twist - an unexpected
happening suggested by any other player at the
table, and decided upon by the Weaver. These can be
positive, negative, useful, harmful - it all depends on the
situation and the creativity of the players!
### NARRATIVE VS. MECHANICAL
Twists are narrative by default, suggestions of what
happens in the story of the game. There can be a
mechanical side too, but that's up to the Weaver to add
if they feel it's right for that particular moment. This
helps regulate the power twists can have on both the
wider story and the immediate situation.
*Example: Jenkins, a spiderish sort, rolls a twist while*
*they're climbing a crumbling garden wall. One player*
*at the table suggests they find a useful seed lodged*
*in a crack, another that Jenkins manages to spot*
*something interesting in the distance due to their*
*height. The Weaver has two decisions to make here -*
*which twist feels right for the situation, and whether*
*either of them has an immediate mechanical impact.*
*If the Weaver goes with the first suggestion, they*
*might reward Jenkins with a new bit, Suspicious Seed.*
*If they go with the second suggestion, they'll only*
*give information that might lead to a later benefit - a*
*completely narrative outcome, but still a potentially*
*useful one.*
### POWER!
Twists can be game-changing, but they shouldn't
be game-breaking. Noticing a new weakness in a foe
mid-battle would be a great twist, but defeating that
foe instantly would be far too strong. As a general rule,
a twist should offer nothing more powerful than a
good triumph could achieve.
### SOME GO-TO TWISTS
When suggesting a twist, the possibilities are literally
endless (or rather simply bound by the creativity of
the table), but there are a few tried-and-true twist
outcomes that work for almost all situations.
	» A bit or bob is found, or lost.
	» A piece of hidden information is gained.
	» A useful thing is spotted, or suddenly removed.
	» Unexpected aid (or danger) arrives.
	» The character achieves something that would usually
take another Action Roll.
### - ROLLS & TWISTS EXAMPLE -
Weaver:	The outskirts of the Hogwild aren't too dense,
but it's going to be hard travel once you're
under the boughs. Is there anything you want
to do to prepare, or are you going to head right
in?
Layla:
I think surveying the immediate locale wouldn't
be the worst idea. Is there anything tall I can
climb nearby that'll get me a good look into the
forest, higher than the undergrowth?
Weaver:	There's an old pre-M signpost nearby - it's
broken, but still pretty tall. Looks like it won't be
too hard a climb, either.
Layla:
Then I'll head up there. I've got Climb, and I
think my limbs aspect helps out here... That's a
3, a 5, a 6, and a 6! A triumph with a twist!
Weaver:	You make it up the signpost no problem,
getting a commanding view of the first section
of the forest. And there's a path, hidden by
bushes, that you'll easily be able to find now
that you've seen it from this height.
Layla:
Result! But what about the twist?
Weaver:	Let's throw it over to the table. Anyone?
Dunf:
Ooh, maybe the path has some old lanterns
strung along it, meaning it'll be bright even
under the canopy.
Layla:
I love that! Can we go for that?
Weaver:	We definitely can - I'll stick that in my notes as
well, so I don't forget once the trees close in.
### A TWIST, OR...?
The results table for Action Rolls on page 35 lists a twist
as the only outcome of doubles, but there's actually a
lot of other stuff you might do instead if the situation
is right. Some specific actions in a game of PICO have
alternative options you can choose from when you roll
doubles, but you can almost always default back to a
simple twist if you want to.
*Example: Yasmin is in a rumble, using her candy axe*
*to attack a beastmouse that got a little too bitey for*
*her liking. She rolls a double as part of her attack and*
*because of the situation, she can either take a twist or*
*treat the roll as a critical (as described on page 58).*

# CUT
It's all so easy, you're thinking to yourself - I've got loads of dice to roll!
And yes, that's true... But when times get tough, you might not have as many *results*  as you expect.
Cut is how PICO measures increasing difficulty, a method of lowering the chances of you getting perfect results
from your rolls. When you cut, you remove a number of results from the dice you've just rolled, starting with the
highest number. That 6? it's gone now. Maybe that 5, too!
*Example: Clarendo is about to roll with 5 dice, but the Weaver informs him he has a Tough Cut of 2. He still rolls all 5*
*dice, getting 2, 3, 5, 5, and 6, but then that Tough Cut comes into play - he removes the two highest dice, the 6 and*
*one of the 5s. Not only has his triumph dropped to a conflict, he's lost his twist! Tough break, man.*
## TYPES OF CUT
Cut is applied when *something*  makes an action harder to excel in than usual, and there are a few different
*somethings*  you should know about. These are the five types of cut, and we'll describe each of them below...
### TOUGH CUT
The most common type, applied by the Weaver when an action is just tougher to pull off cleanly.
Tough Cut might be applied thanks to poor positioning, illness, unfamiliarity, stubbornness...*It's the*
*most general type of cut, and can be hard to avoid or reduce without changing your approach.*
### ARMOUR CUT
Usually only encountered during a frenetic combat or serious scuffle, this is applied by the Weaver
if you're using an action against a heavily armoured or well-defended target. *You might remove*
*Armour Cut by denying a target of defences, or finding a hidden weak point.*
### SPEED CUT
This is applied by the Weaver when you're trying to do something faster than usual, or targeting
a really swift creature as it zips by. *You might remove Speed Cut by immobilizing a target, or by*

# IMPACT
Impact is a measure of how effective an action is, both
in terms of how it affects the world around you and in
how many boxes are marked on a track. *The Weaver*
*usually determines impact based on how effective an*
*action you're about to take is likely to be.*
There are four levels of impact an action can have in
PICO; no impact, low impact, normal impact, and giga
impact. This might sound like a lot, but don't worry;
if you play to your bug's strengths and make good
decisions, only two will turn up in regular play.
The boxes below cover the narrative and mechanical
implications of each level of impact, and each is
illustrated with an example of Gnash, our little bug
pictured to the right, using an action to open a door.
## NORMAL IMPACT
That's more like it! When affecting a track you can mark the usual one box, and any additional effect you have on the
world is likely both useful and noticeable.
*Example: Gnash doesn't much care for doors. Others might push it open gently, or even just open it up like a regular*
*ol' person and saunter through, but Gnash chooses to barrel through with a holler; it's just the kind of bug he is.*
## GIGA IMPACT
If you have giga impact, then all bets are off! Your effects are titanic and, if you're affecting a track, you mark *every*
box up until either the end of the track, or the next track break. Giga impact solves problems quickly!
*Example: With this level of impact, Gnash can pretty much do what he wants where this door is concerned. He might*
*knock it right off of its hinges, break it into pieces, or even smash through the middle, leaving a bug-shaped hole in*
*his wake. It's a true demonstration of peak bugly power!*
## NO IMPACT
A complete non-starter. If the Weaver tells you that an
action you're taking is going to have no impact at all,
that's a sign for you to re-think your approach - you
won't mark any boxes on tracks at all, and any other
effect you might on the world will barely be perceptible
and change absolutely nothing.
*Example: Gnash pushes against the 'door' to no avail,*
*not realising that it's actually a wall.*
## LOW IMPACT
Not exactly an impressive display. Your action is
successful, but it only has a very small effect on the
situation. If you're affecting a track, mark one box fewer
than usual (normally meaning you mark zero, but your
action might still have other effects).
*Example: Gnash manages to open the door... but it's*
*torturously slow, or it sticks and scrapes repeatedly.*
*Either he's too weak, or the door is just too heavy.*

## THE BASICS OF IMPACT
Let's get this on the page right now, to prevent any
confusion (and to hopefully speed things up): if the
Weaver or a special rule for your bug hasn't told you
otherwise, the impact of any action you take is going
to be NORMAL.
Why? Because wrangling over the impact of *every*
action at the table would get dull fast, especially in a
lighthearted game like PICO. Instead, you should check
your probable impact with the Weaver only if you're
doing something that you think might be outside
your capabilities. Lifting a pebble? You probably have
normal impact. Lifting a boulder? Low impact, or even
no impact, might be more appropriate. *Basically, only*
*check when you're in doubt.*
As a quick rule of thumb...
	» Low Impact represents roughly the amount of effort
a little grubling could put into something.
	» Normal Impact is about right for the average bug
doing something they're pretty confident in.
	» Giga Impact is usually reserved for things that are
massive and impressive, like cats and other titans.
### A SLIDING SCALE
A quick reminder here that 'low impact' is not
synonymous with 'failure', nor 'giga impact' with
'success'. - that's all in the dice. A triumph on a low
impact action to grab the attention of a crowd, for
example, might have you attract one or two useful
individuals. In contrast, a disaster with giga impact
might have you at the centre of attention for everyone
there, but for all the wrong reasons. Impact augments
your Action Roll results, it doesn't replace them.

## SO, WHAT'S MY IMPACT?
It happens to the best of us - we've got this brilliant plan we're about to put into action and the Weaver leans in to go
"just so you know, you're *probably*  going to have lower impact there than you think..."
Don't think of this as an impasse though, and definitely not as a punishment. In an ideal world, that's the Weaver
pointing out that you might want to change what you're doing, or that there may be something you haven't
considered. If your impact is going to be higher or lower than normal, the Weaver should (almost) always tell
you before you roll. This gives you time to consider other actions, or even alter your approach entirely.
*Example: Rexi is a tough-talking, leather-jacket-wearing scorpion, currently trying to intimidate a group of mice*
*that stole a grub's bike. He wants to use his Swagger skill, but he's massively outnumbered - low impact, the Weaver*
*tells him. But Rexi's player has an idea - if they used their Sizzle skill, they have an aspect chosen at character*
*creation that lets them treat Sizzle as Gigasizzle when dealing with foes that have the 'Beastly' keyword. These mice*
*are as Beastly as they come, so this seems like a perfect opportunity to splash a bit of venom around and instill fear*
*into some furry bike thieves. It might even scatter some of them, leaving them wide open for a future swagger!*
Of course, sometimes you might not *want*  to change what you're doing. In those cases you can either roll with lower
impact or ask the Weaver if there's anything you can do to increase it - who knows, you might be able to cut a deal.
### WHAT INCREASES YOUR IMPACT?
Increasing your impact is pretty tough - fictionally,
there's a reason bugs don't wander around doing most
daily tasks with the speed, strength, or effectiveness
of cats! But 'tough' is not the same as 'impossible', so if
you're angling for increased impact we recommend...
	» Asking the Weaver: They *might*  offer you some kind
of deal, along the lines of 'if you do this riskier thing or
use a resource, you can bump your impact up by one
step'. This is pretty situational though (and it slows
the game down), so try not to rely on it too often.
	» Using Certain Aspects: Your bug's aspects (detailed
in full on page 104) are the building blocks of your
character, and each comes with special rules. Some
aspects allow you to increase your impact in a certain
situation, or when using a particular skill.
	» Choosing the Right Approach: Using your bug's
best skills helps with dice rolls, but high impact is
more about whether what you're doing suits the
situation. The perfect action may be rewarded
with increased impact from the Weaver, even if you
haven't asked!
	» Using Combos and Finishers: Found on page 46,
these are teamwork-style moves that lead up to
an action with increased impact. They don't come
without a cost, though...
	» Targeting Weaknesses: When facing a hazard, using
a skill they're weak to increases your impact by one
step (a great way of getting reliable giga impact).
### WHAT DECREASES YOUR IMPACT?
Unfortunately easier than increasing your impact, these
are things the Weaver will be taking into account when
you describe your action - and you should keep them in
mind too! When your impact is lower than you'd hoped,
you might be...
	» Overreaching: Maybe what you're trying to do is
just a *bit*  too ambitious. There's nothing wrong with
shooting for the stars, but you are still a tiny bug at
the end of the day!
	» An Unhelpful Environment: Navigating a field
during a lovely sunny day? No problem. Navigating
that same field during a thunderstorm, at night?
Good luck. Bad environmental conditions might
cause cut (as detailed on page 38), but they can just
as easily lower your impact.
	» Choosing a Less-Than-Optimal Approach: We
don't want to say that your ideas are terrible, but
there's a reason people don't often try to break rocks
with a blancmange.
	» Targeting a Resistance (or Immunity): Most
hazards have resistances, which drop your impact
by one step (the exact inverse of a weakness). Some
hazards also have immunities, resulting in even a
perfect shot having no impact at all! Learn about
your foes and use the knowledge to your advantage.
	» Hitting an Unexpected Drawback: Sometimes a
surprise wrench gets thrown in the works. *See the*
*next page for info on 'secret variables'.*

# TRUTHS
The Mysteriocalypse definitely had some serious effects on the world of PICO, but nobody can quite explain what
they *were* . It put bugs at the top of the pecking order (thematically, at least - realistically, birds are still the masters
of pecking), and it for sure removed a particularly common species of two-legged titans that loved building
unexplainable things, but the how and the why are lost in... Well, in mystery.
But there are some things that a bug can grasp, if they put their minds to it: these are Truths, unusual facts about
the world of PICO that travellers will discover as they explore, learn, and strive for answers.
## ESTABLISHING TRUTHS
Truths are established by players pursuing their bug's mystery, a question decided during character creation and
then chipped away at through the process of making first small, then grand, discoveries. When a grand discovery
is made, the Weaver records it on the Roaming Sheet (page 172), as these truths affect more than just the character
that introduced them - they're now a fact of the world! *More on these discoveries can be found on page 164, so for*
*now we'll just give an...*
*Example: Ryan is a bearded fly, part of a band of bugs wandering the vast,*
*gloomy interior of the Shadow House. He has a mystery to solve, 'How can*
*glass be so strong, yet so clear?', which is pretty appropriate for a fly*
*when you think about it. During his adventure he makes several small*
*discoveries; he realises that glass dulls sound (but doesn't stop light),*
*that it makes a satisying clinking noise when other glass hits it, and*
*that if a sound is high-pitched enough, it can make glass shatter*
*completely. With these three small discoveries under his belt,*
*he takes the spotlight to make a grand discovery - that glass*
*is, in fact, solidified music. The Weaver records this grand*
*discovery on the Roaming Sheet as a new Truth of the post-*
*Mysteriocalypse, and Ryan gets to choose a new mystery to*
*figure out in the future.*
### CHANGING THE WORLD, ONE
### TRUTH AT A TIME
When a truth is recorded on the Roaming
Sheet it becomes a fact of the world of
PICO, something both Weaver and players
can draw on through the rest of a game or
campaign as they play. See the following page for how these
truths can be used, but also heed this warning first...
Truths are *powerful* .
Quite apart from the mechanical benefits we're about
to explain, a truth can affect the direction of a story
and the flavour of the world. It's good practice to have
a group discuss a grand dicovery, and the truth it will
impose on the story moving forward, before making
it official.
And one final pointer - truths don't have to make sense to
us, but they *do*  need to make sense to a bug.

bugs might travel through. That means truths can be called upon as an environmental advantage in the right
situation, if it makes sense for the action. *Advantage is detailed on page 34.*
*Example: Joachim, a bug in Ryan's group, is trying to scare an overly curious bird pecking on a nearby window.*

normal twist, the effects aren't decided by the other players at the table, and this doesn't need to be part of any kind
of roll - instead, the player that called on the truth decides on the effect (though they still need to get the okay
from the Weaver, as with any twist decision). *Twists are detailed on page 37.*

*bird's own actions. If that doesn't give it a scare, nothing will!*

# COMBOS & FINISHERS
Sometimes the odds are against you; you're tiny, and the world is a big, big place. But with a little teamwork and a lot
of effort, your band of bugs might be able to pull off a combo - a sequence of linked actions that lead to an extremely
impressive outcome, a finisher.
When the Weaver sets a track that the bugs have to fill in order to achieve something big (such as taking down a
vicious hazard, or achieving an important and immediate goal), they may also set a smaller, 3-box Combo Track. The
setting of a combo track is always a Weaver decision.
## FILLING THE COMBO TRACK
When taking actions with a Combo Track in play, a bug can burn a box on one of their aspects as they perform an
action. This represents the bug putting in extra effort to contribute to a combo, and the aspect is burned whether
the action is successful or not. *It's always the player's choice whether to burn in order to add to a combo.*
But if this action *is*  successful - a conflict or triumph - it marks a box on the Combo Track in addition to any usual
effects (even if it doesn't mark any other tracks). The combo is in full swing, and a finisher is just around the corner!

## PICK YOUR MOMENT

wouldn't normally mark their track, but can still fill a
Combo Track and unlock an instance of useful giga
