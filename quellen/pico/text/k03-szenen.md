## SETTING THE SCENE
Scenes are made for freeform play - you can do pretty
much anything in a scene (or at least attempt pretty
much anything, you're still going to have to rely on the
dice if your actions get difficult, dangerous, or dramatic).
Scenes should start with the Weaver as they literally 'set
the scene', making it clear...
	» WHERE the scene takes place
	» WHEN the scene takes place
	» WHO is in the scene
	» WHAT is happening before anybody acts
	» WHY the scene is happening
This might sound like a lot to remember, but it doesn't all
have to be delivered at once. Questions from the players
can help flesh out some of these points, and if one of
them gets missed accidentally - eh, it happens. It's not
the end of the world.
Really, all you're aiming for is for everyone at the table to
have a clear idea of what's going on, which in turn gives
them a good basis for thinking about the things their
own bug might do.
*Example: As the sun begins to set, the streets of*
*craniopolis get a little quieter. There are still bugs out*
*and about, but they move faster, spend more time in*
*the shadow of awnings and close to tin walls. It's easy*
*to see why - every now and then, a shrill chirp from*
*high above hints at the presence of bats in the rafters.*
*It's after a particularly close-sounding chirp that you*
*hear another bug hissing at you from a nearby saloon*
*doorway - 'Hey, travellers, this way'.*
The example above is only a few sentences, but it hits
the where, the when, the who, and the what. The why
will likely become apparent when the group interacts
with the NPC that's just been introduced.
But players could ask questions too, before they make
any decisions. Questions like 'What kind of bug is this
hissing guy?', or 'Just how far up are those rafters?', or
'Remind me, do bats eat bugs?'. It helps keep everyone
on the same page, metaphorically speaking.

# EXPLORING
*“Exploration is really the essence of the insect spirit.”*
Did we mention how big the world is? I think we might
have, a couple of times...
There are two sorts of exploration that are important
here. For heading out into the world, going to new
places, and travelling through unfamiliar terrain, you're
going to want the Journeys chapter (starting on page
83). But right here we're thinking smaller-scale -
learning more about the place you're actually in, and
using it to your advantage.
## POINTS OF INTEREST
One of the easiest ways to define a location is to think
about what's there that is interesting, either to your bug
as a character or to you as a player. These are points of
interest, sometimes abbreviated to POIs.
When the Weaver sets the scene, listen out for points of
interest that you might be able to use as you play.
*Example: The Weaver describes a scene that starts*
*in the forest. As she does, she tells the group about a*
*thicket of bracken nearby, about some weird-smelling*
*flowers, and about a beehive up in the branches high*
*above. These three things can all be considered points*
*of interest.*
### ADDING NEW POIs {farbe=#3b413f}
But it's not just up to the Weaver to populate a scene!
Before anybody starts taking actions or making choices,
players can ask questions about things that might be
important to them. A player whose bug loves to swim
might ask about any water in the area, and one who
is playing a tiny gourmet might ask if there are any
amazing smells wafting around.
*Example: Jace's bug is a digger by nature, and loves*
*burrowing underground when they get a chance. Jace*
*asks the Weaver what the forest floor is like, and she*
*adds that it's mostly soil and moss, but there are a few*
*jagged stones sticking out in some places. Suddenly,*
*new POIs! Those stones might be useful for hiding*
*behind, the moss could be collected and kept, and the*
*soil floor sounds perfect for Jace's bug to dig down into.*
### ADDING EVEN MORE POIs {farbe=#3b413f}
And don't think that all of this stuff has to happen right
at the start of a scene - these questions can be asked at
any time, really.
The only guideline we'll give here is an important one...
Not every scene will have POIs that work for you.
If they did, the game would get boring pretty quickly.
Always feel free to ask, but the answer from time to time
might just be 'no'.
## THE THEATRE OF THE MIND
If you've been playing tabletop games for a while, you're likely familiar with this term. PICO is a game where almost all
of the 'action' takes place within the theatre of the mind, a fancy way of saying 'you imagine stuff happening as you
talk about it'. Locations don't have their own little maps, you don't have miniatures (although we admit that would be
disgustingly cute), and the fiction of what's going on is only constrained by a combination of rules and story.
When you use the theatre of the mind, you have licence to be a little flexible with what is and isn't in a scene. Those
points of interest? Maybe there's a cool one that you 'missed' talking about, because it would make the immediate
play more interesting. If that's the case, ask the Weaver if you can stick it in a scene. Need a sudden NPC to do X? Ask
the Weaver if there's anyone around that can, conveniently, do X.
When using the theatre of the mind, everything you say is a flag to both the Weaver and the other players about what
you understand, what you assume, what you're thinking, and where you might be going with it.
It's a collaborative story, at the end of the day. There's power in leaning into that.
### USING POIs {farbe=#3b413f}
Points of interest aren't just useful for setting a scene, they can have mechanical benefits too! Once a POI
is established, it can be used for environmental advantage when making an Action Roll, or as something to
investigate when gathering bits and bobs (more on those very soon). *Points of interest might be dangerous too, or*
*make some actions more difficult by adding cut or reducing impact. Find out by playing!*

## MOVING AROUND {farbe=#3b413f}
Once you've established your POIs and know what the
scene looks like in terms of the theatre of the mind, it's
time to move! Take actions, do stuff, hop on things, hop
*off*  things. Those points of interest are there, you may as
well clamber all over them.
Exploring a scene can take the form of careful study,
but it's more likely that you're going to wander about
the place and make a general nuisance of yourself.
It's a great way to learn more about your environment,
and learning is key! And you want to do something
difficult, dangerous, or dramatic, that's when you
make an Action Roll!
*Example: As the group starts moving around, Jace*
*takes an Action Roll to have his bug try to burrow down*
*under one of the sharp stones - just how deep do they*
*go, and what might be below them?*

# GATHERING
*“A penny saved is a penny that you have to work out*
*how to roll home. And that's not as easy as it looks,*
*believe me. What are you even doing with a penny,*
*anyway? What, you want some kind of really expensive*
*dining table?”*
Almost every scene will have opportunities to grab
useful (or useless) stuff to take with you, as long as you
have the space to hold it. These things come in the
form of bits and bobs - you'll get a full run-down on
how these work, and how many you can carry, on page
160, but for now all you need to know is that bits are
biological oddities and bobs are artificial scrap.
## GATHER ROLL RESULTS
### TRIUMPH
You get exactly what you want! Put the bit or
bob you were going for into one of your carry
slots, or give it to one of your other group
members for them to hold in one of theirs.
### CONFLICT
You get what you want, but it's not as nice
as you were expecting OR there's some
downside to acquiring it. Depending on the
situation, you might gather a bit or bob with
a negative tag, or take some damage (or
some other consequence) while collecting
your prize.
### DISASTER
Your hopes are dashed - either the bit or bob
you were trying to collect is unobtainable,
it's completely unfit for purpose, or you take
some damage when trying to acquite it and
*still*  don't end up with it in your carry slots.
### TWIST or ADD TAG
You can treat a twist while gathering as
normal, where something unexpected
happens. Alternatively, you can add a
positive tag of your choice to the bit or
bob you acquired, with the approval of
the Weaver.

## GETTING BITS & BOBS {farbe=#3b413f}
See that flower over there? I bet it's got some really cool
pollen inside of it. But how to get it out?
Just like when you move around, grabbing something
from your surroundings is as easy as announcing what
you're doing to the group, and then describing it in the
fiction of the game. Easy, right? Well... Sometimes.
### TO ROLL, OR NOT?
If there's no element of randomness or challenge to
gathering a bit or bob, you just *get it* . No rolls required -
stick it straight into a carry slot and continue with the
scene. *You'll find information on carry slots, and how*
*many bits and bobs you can hold, on page 161.*
But If the act of gathering would be difficult,
dangerous, or dramatic, you might have to roll for it.
This is where actions come in, coupled with your skills
(the skills Hunt and Rummage are particularly useful
here). The table on the previous page shows a special
set of dice results you can use when taking an action to
gather, too!
*Example: Sasha sees a penny half-buried in the mud of*
*a riverbank. She loves shiny things in general, and she*
*has carry slots free, so she decides to try to take it with*
*her. The Weaver asks her to roll, pointing out that the*
*penny is pretty firmly stuck in the mud. Sasha does, and*
*gets a conflict with a twist. The Weaver describes that in*
*the course of pulling the penny out, she alerts a nearby*
*snake on the other side of the river, that slips under*
*the water and heads in her direction. But she does get*
*the penny, and with the twist she adds a positive tag,*
*Golden, deciding that this penny is made out of pure*
*gold. She adds the Golden Penny to one of her carry*
*slots, but now she and her friends have to deal with the*
*approaching snake.*

### DANGEROUS TALK {farbe=#3b413f}
Not every bug you meet is going to be friendly, and that
goes triple for beasts. Saying the wrong thing, bringing
up the wrong topic, or being rude - either accidentally
or on purpose - might lead to a scuffle - maybe even a
full-blown scrap! When you're interacting verbally with
something hostile, you should probably roll the dice to
see how the conversation goes.
*Example: A band of piratical caterpillars are hanging*
*out at the bar, their long bodies blocking every stool.*
*Asif wants to take a seat and grab a relaxing drink, but*
*he's going to have to ask them to move first. He looks at*
*his friending skills and chooses Yap to help his action,*
*hoping to be a sparkling enough wit for them to want*
*him around and make some room so he can sit. But if it*
*goes wrong, he may well just be their next target!*
### DIFFICULT TALK {farbe=#3b413f}
Difficulty in interaction might come from the topics of
conversation, but it might also be thanks to what you
hope to get out of an exchange. Trying to persuade
an NPC to act in your favour, to do something out of
character for them, or to act against their best interests,
is going to be *hard* .
You can also roll to try to determine whether someone's
telling the truth or not, though you'll have to work in
some interrogative questions into your speech to take
advantage of this type of interaction. *Expect some*
*Tough Cut in situation like these, especially if the*
*individual you're talking to is particularly wily.*
*Example: Herve and his gang of friends are on the*
*trail of a wily fox, a titan that's stolen one of the tin*
*cans that their home settlement uses as a kitchen.*
*As far as they can tell, the fox has crossed a nearby*
*river controlled by zealously mercantile spiders, who'll*
*take other bugs back and forth in their silky barges -*
*but only for an exorbitant price. With no bits or bobs*
*valuable enough to pay the toll, Herve uses his Dazzle*
*skill to try to persuade one of the spiders that letting*
*them cross for free now will bring riches in the long*
*run, but it's going to be a hard sell.*
### DRAMATIC TALK {farbe=#3b413f}
Sometimes the dice are used for an interaction not
because it's dangerous, or difficult, but because it's a big
pivotal point in a story - the outcome being uncertain
adds a spice of the unexpected to proceedings.
*Example: Lucy is bargaining for the release of an old*
*stag beetle smith, a long-time prisoner of one of the*
*squirrel kings. She's in the good graces of the court, so*
*there's no immediate danger to her, and the king's quite*
*fond of her already - but think about the ramifications if*
*it got out that they were letting prisoners go. Lucy rolls*
*the dice, but uses the Think skill to try to find a good*
*logical way to argue the squirrel king around to her*
*position.*

## HOW TO MAKE FRIENDS {farbe=#3b413f}
There's a whole cateogry of skills devoted to interacting
with NPCs, known as the friending skills. *You'll learn*
*more about them on page 149, but we'll do a quick*
*run-down of how each might help you during a social*
*interaction here too...*
	» Yap: For chatting, gathering information, and putting
bugs and beasts at ease.
	» Swagger: For making yourself seem tough or cool,
often by playing up your physicality or presence.
	» Dazzle: For showing off and grabbing the attention
of crowds, and for confusing others with clever
wordplay.
	» Scare: For inspiring *fear* ! Like Swagger, your physical
presence can work in your favour here, but alliances
built on fear don't tend to last.
	» Care: While primarily used for healing, making people
feel valuable and safe is a great way of instilling
positive feelings in them.
	» Tame: For interacting with wilder bugs and larger
beasts, those that might not share your ability to
speak, but can still be soothed or reasoned with.
But don't think that it's only these skills that can
come in useful! You might be able to endear yourself
to an individual by sharing a passion with, or by
demonstrating knowledge or understanding of
something they hold dear.
*Example: Heinrik is trying to forge an alliance with a*
*curious bird, so uses his own skill at flying to establish*
*some common ground.*
### ... AND INFLUENCE PEOPLE {farbe=#3b413f}
Wouldn't it be nice if every problem could be solved
with a lovely, calm chat? Unfortunately for the bugs
of the Mysteriocalypse, things haven't changed *that*
much; sometimes the best way to get what you want
is through throwing a bit of intimidation into the mix,
maybe even making some threats.
Friending skills can still come in useful here (like
Scare), but don't be afraid to try other skills too -
absentmindedly crushing a pebble between your claws
with the Bash skill during a conversation is going to lend
some weight to fighting words, even if you're not the
most eloquent speaker.
### WHAT, NO SPECIAL ROLL TABLE? {farbe=#3b413f}
Not this time! NPC interactions should be as varied as
the NPCs themselves, and the classic triumph / conflict
/ disaster / twist spread is easy to adapt to whatever
outcome a conversation or chance meeting might have.
You don't need special rules to make a meeting special,
just a bit of creativity at the table!

## MORE THAN JUST A FIGHT
Don't get us wrong here, the rumble rules definitely cover a bites-and-blades style combat. But, in a game of PICO,
violence is entirely optional, and these rules can *also*  be used to represent a variety of encounters with the dangers
of the world. A rumble might take the form of...
	» A scuffle between bugs as they compete for a delicious prize, or a bit of useful
territory.
	» A tense exchange of words and gestures, the kind of argument where
something's got to give.
	» A deadly encounter with a beastly threat, where losing probably
means becoming someone's next meal.
	» A frantic chase through the undergrowth, with a pursuing party
drawing ever closer.
	» A contest of strength or skill, where prizes are decided in advance
and winning is more a matter of honour than survival.
	» An all-out brawl in a tin-can bar, where bugs drunk on
pollenmead throw tiny chairs at one another.
	» A match of cap-golf, or any of the other bugly sports popular
among the residents of the post-Mysteriocalypse.
	» A nighttime ambush on weary travellers, stealthy froglins
rushing in to sieze what they can and scarper.
... Or any combination of the above!
### THE HALLMARKS OF A RUMBLE
In a rumble, you're usually working directly to defeat
an opponent in some way. That means there will
usually be at least a few of the following elements...
	» A Heart Track to fill, as explained on page 57.
	» Opportunities to specifically attack and defend,
with special rules on page 58.
	» A lot of damage being thrown around, which can be
enhanced or mitigated with weaknesses and
resistances as shown on page 60.
# RUMBLING
*“This mustn't register on an emotional level. First, distract target, then block his grabby tongue. Counter with wing to*
*left cheek. Discombobulate. Dazed, he'll attempt a wild hay-maker - employ shell block, and body shot. Capacity to*
*lick all my nice food, neutralized.”*
Bugs don't fight - that would be so *uncouth* ! But from time to time they do love a rumble, where mandibles snap,
toothpick-blades slice, harsh words are unleashed, and both shells and pride are often dented.
That's right, rumbles are the bugly equivalent of a combat scene! The next few pages delve into forms a rumble might
take, the special rules both Weaver and players can use during this type of scene, and how to leverage your skills and
aspects to take hazards on head to head!

## TRACKS IN A RUMBLE
Tracks are just as useful in a rumble as they are in other modes of play, as an excellent visual reminder of a scene's
current goal. The usual progress tracks as described on page 32 can still be used (and are great for things like
changing environmental conditions, the time taken for reinforcements to show up, or the amount of distance left to
cover in a chase or race), but there are three types of track that are particularly useful in a rumble: Spotlight Tracks,
Combo Tracks, and Heart Tracks. *Not every rumble will use these tracks, so don't feel obligated!*
### SPOTLIGHT TRACKS
PICO doesn't have an initiative system beyond 'whoever
is doing something should probably get some spotlight
next', but with actions and reactions coming thick and
fast in a rumble, it can help everyone if the Weaver
keeps a Spotlight Track - a way of recording who has
acted and reacted, and how many times.
As described on page 31, a Spotlight Track is as simple
as putting down the name of each bug (or player) and
then putting either an A or an R next to their name
each time they get the spotlight, representing actions
or reactions. If a player hasn't had the chance to take
actions recently, the track will make that clear, and
the Weaver (or another player) can use the narrative to
swing the spotlight round to them. *If there's a hazard*
*involved in a scene, or NPCs, you may or may not add*
*them to the Spotlight Track according to the group's*
*tastes, or the amount of bookkeeping the Weaver wants*
*to do.*
### HEART TRACKS
Though most tracks are represented with linked circles
(because they're easy to draw at the table), some tracks
within this book look a little different. A little... heartier?
### ♥♥•♥♥♥♥
Heart Tracks work exactly the same way as any other
track, they just look a little different (with a heart
representing a box and a little dot representing a break).
Why? Because they're there as a visual indicator that
this particular track is *perfect*  for soaking up damage
during a rumble (though, like all tracks, they can be
marked in other ways). *You don't have to represent*
*these tracks using hearts at the table, though,*
*especially if you find them harder or slower to draw. And*
*some Weavers might not even have the Heart Track of a*
*hazard visible, especially if the threat is unknown to the*
*bugs in the band!*
You'll find Heart Tracks on your character's aspects (on
page 104) and, if you're the Weaver, on the statblocks for
hazards (starting on page 201).

Particularly useful if the bugs are facing a really tough
hazard, a Combo Track (as described fully on page 46)
lets characters burn some of their own boxes as they
act to set up a high-damage finishing move. Teamwork
is even more important in a rumble than in most scenes,
so when the Weaver puts a Combo Track down you
should try to get some use out of it!

## ATTACKING AND DEFENDING
Any action used in a rumble has the potential to mark tracks and affect the world, just like it would in any other scene.
Bugs that aren't combat-focused can still be just as useful as they normally would be, it just takes a bit of quick
thinking and some creative uses of their existing skills!
*Example: Spidal isn't a fan of scuffles, but when danger comes knocking he's determined to hold his own. His frying*
*pan can be used as a Bashing weapon, his Cook skill is great for throwing salt or spices into the eyes of opponents,*
*and even his Care skill might come in handy as he distracts combatants with the delicious smells of a hearty meal...*
*long enough for one of his allies to sink tiny fangs into them.*
But, for the fightier bugs among you, there *are*  two special types of action (with unique results tables) that are
perfectly suited to rougher situations - attacks and defends.
The results of an Attack or Defend Roll are tuned with combat in mind, all about taking or avoiding the damage and
effects of dangerous moves. You don't need to specifically call out that you're attacking or defending (though we
suppose you could if you wanted to, it would certainly give a very anime-fight-scene vibe to proceedings). Instead,
it's usually on the Weaver to determine whether the normal rules for an action, or the special rules for an attack or
defend, suit the situation better.
## ACTIONS TO ATTACK {farbe=#9f2320}
An attack is usually an action specifically intended to
harm a target in some way, or at least to make their life
somehow worse. The rumble skills Bash, Bite, Slash, and
Sizzle are all prime candidates to help with a roll here,
as are any aspects that might make you stronger, more
menacing, or particularly spiky. *The rumble skills get a*
*full explanation on page 151.*
But as we mentioned above, these scenes don't expect
the more pacifist bugs to stand aside and let others
have all the fun. Any skill can be used to attack, and
many foes will actually have specific weaknesses to
non-rumble skills, increasing the amount of marks
made on a Heart Track!
*Example: Froglins are weak to both the Tame, because*
*they're so rarely shown kindness, and Yap, because*
*they can't stand seeing their friends get insulted or*
*made fun of (by anyone that isn't them).*
The damage an attack deals is always equal to the
action's impact (so a normal-impact attack would
deal one mark of damage if successful, whereas a
giga-impact attack would deal enough marks to fill the
target's Heart Track completely, or at least until the next
track break), and the damage type is the same as the
skill used for the roll. *We'll give some extra rules for*
*damage, and how it's affected by a target's weaknesses*
*or resistances, on page 60.*
## ATTACK ROLL RESULTS
### TRIUMPH
Your strike lands true! You deal damage to
your target, and hit them with an effect.
### CONFLICT
You hit, but it's either a glancing blow or
something with additional complications.
Choose one of the following...
	» You deal damage OR an effect,
OR
	» You deal a mark of damage AND an
effect, but you're also hit with damage
or an effect from your target.
### DISASTER
That did not go as planned. Your attack
misses completely, letting your target
take the opportunity to hit you with both
damage AND an effect of their own.
### TWIST or CRITICAL
You can treat a twist while rumbling as
normal, where something unexpected
happens. Alternatively, you can choose
to deal an extra mark of damage to your
target, which gets applied right after the first
instance of damage (if you're dealing giga
damage and hit a track break, this additional
mark is made *after*  the break).

## REACTIONS TO DEFEND {farbe=#185d7e}
Defending is a great use of your time if some nasty hazard has lined up a shot against you. In fact, the Weaver will
throw the spotlight your way whenever you become the target of an attack from a foe, specifically to let you
use a reaction to defend yourself!
There are a couple of skills that are absolute classics when it comes to defending, in the shape of Dash (for getting
out of the way quickly), Dance (for elegantly evading), and Shield (for weathering an incoming blow as best you can).
But, just like with attacks, any skill can help you make a Defend Roll if it fits the situation!

Hazards don't treat impact in the same way that bugs do, so sometimes you'll have some truly *terrifying*  amounts of

### 60 {farbe=#494344}
## DEALING DAMAGE
When you're engaged with a hazard or hostile force of some kind, damage comes from any action that brings the
rumble closer to a conclusion. This usually comes in the form of attacks, where bugs try to deal damage directly to
an opponent in the form of marks on their Heart Track, but also covers...
	» Actions that make an opponent less likely, or less able, to keep rumbling.
	» Actions that trap an opponent, or that let the player characters get closer to escaping.
	» Actions that confuse or demoralize an opponent, making it more likely that they'll give up.
### MAKING THE MOST OF YOUR DAMAGE
Impact is important here. Heart Tracks in a rumble are like any other track in this regard - the more impact your
action has, the more boxes you're going to affect. Giga impact tends to end rumbles pretty quickly against all but the
most titanic foes!
The quality of your rolls matters massively too. Both triumph and conflict results are a reliable way of getting some
damage in (though conflicts also usually involve you taking some damage yourself), and avoiding cut where possible
makes these results more probable.
### ADDING EFFECTS
When you have the opportunity to deal damage, you'll almost always have the choice of trading it for an effect
(or adding an effect on top of the damage you deal). While there are a few specific effects that have pre-defined
outcomes, most will be freeform - an extra bit of narrative weight to your action that changes something about you,
your opponent, or the rumble as a whole. Don't discount effects just because they may not mark tracks directly; PICO
is a narrative first game for a reason! *Effects, both specific and freeform, are discussed in full on page 64.*
### WEAKNESSES & RESISTANCES
You can't have a damage type system without some
weaknesses to exploit, or resistances to avoid! Many
hazards will have at least one weakness and one
resistance on their statblock, and the rules for these are
pretty simple...
	» If you hit a target with a damage type they're
weak to, increase your impact by one step (from
no to low, low to normal, or normal to giga).
	» If you hit a target with a damage type they're
resistant to, decrease your impact by one step
(from low to no impact, or from normal to low, or from
giga to normal).
### IMMUNITIES
Some hazards even have immunities, types of damage
that just have no effect on them whatsoever. In these
cases it doesn't matter what your impact is, or how well
you roll - you can't mark their track with a damage type
they're immune to. You can still use effects though,
so keep this in mind. *Hazard statblocks are found in*
*chapter 10, starting on page 200.*
### ACTIONS, SKILLS, & DAMAGE TYPES
The damage an attack deals always has a type, usually
the same type as the skill that was used for the
Action Roll (so a roll with the Sizzle skill would naturally
cause Sizzle Damage).
There are ways to alter this though, if it makes more
sense for the fiction of the game. A clever use of a bug's
aspects, any bits and bobs they have on hand, or the
environment they're in (including any established POIs)
might all make another damage type more appropriate
than the one that matches their skill. *The final example*
*on the next page gives a scenario where the skill used*
*and the type of damage it deals may not match up.*
But what does this really mean?
That there's no such thing as being useless in a
rumble.
Even the gentlest bug can achieve something in an
out-and-out fight through clever use of their skills,
resources, and the world around them. Yeah, maybe
combat-themed bugs might still get the biggest,
splashiest moments, but it's always a team effort.

## EFFECTS
Whenever you take an action, you're probably looking to have an effect on the world in some way. And in a rumble,
when you're rolling to attack or defend, that effect is handled separately from the damage you deal, take, or avoid.
An effect can be anything from grabbing someone to knocking them over, jumping to a new position to whispering in
someone's ear. They don't deal or avoid any additional damage, but they can have a big impact on the narrative of
a scene, and might change how an enemy reacts or what opportunities your allied bugs have.
*Example: Jojo body slams an approaching wheely-grub with a triumph, dealing a mark of damage with his Bash*
*skill and gaining the opportunity to inflict an effect. He uses it to push the wheely-bug off course, smashing it into*
*a wall and stopping its incessant zooming - the Weaver declares that the next bug that attacks it may not take the*
*usual cut imposed for the wheely-grub being so fast, but it'll recover soon enough!*
## FREEFORM EFFECTS {farbe=#ee7bae}
Most effects in PICO are going to be freeform, where you describe what you want to do and the Weaver comes up
with an associated narrative or mechanical outcome to match it (whatever fits the situation). An effect might deny
a hazard cut, temporarily remove one of their moves, change your location on the battlefield, create a new POI... the
possibilities are pretty much endless! *There's additional guidance for the Weaver on how they might mechanize*
*effects on page 198.*
## SPECIFIC EFFECTS {farbe=#008a99}
Some effects happen often enough that they get a special name, and may even be referenced by certain rules. These
effects, in fact!
### PUSH & PULL
Pushing a target sends it away from you, and pulling it
brings it towards you. It's as simple as that!
*Example: Javier rolls a conflict for his attack against*
*a nearby beastmouse, and decides to go for an effect*
*rather than a mark of damage. Why would he do this?*
*Because the beastmouse is standing on the edge of*
*a convenient cliff! The push might send it right over or*
*leave it dangling, needing help to get back up.*
### STUN
Another easy one, but without the flexibility of pushing
and pulling. When a character stuns a hazard, it can't
take the spotlight next (usually meaning that an ally
can leap in with an immediate follow-up action). This
is the same in reverse, with a stunned character not
being able to take the spotlight next (for an action or
reaction). Why is this bad? Because if you can't react
to something, that's an auto-disaster!
*Example: A beastmouse stuns Javier, a blow from a*
*tiny club sending him reeling. Another mouse follows up*
*with a wicked bite, which the stunned Javier takes the*
*full force of, as if he rolled a disaster.*
### GRAB
A grab entangles two individuals until the grabber lets
go (which can be done at any time) or the grabbed
breaks free (which will usually require a roll to achieve,
or to stop from happening).
There are few hard-and-fast rules for a grab - this
isn't that kind of game - but there are a few important
conventions you should follow, based on the relative
sizes and positions of those involved in a grab. Bigger
individuals can easily move or throw smaller ones,
and smaller individuals can ride or climb around on
bigger ones (but usually only if they're the grabber).
*Example: Javier decides to grab a hulking beastmouse*
*chieftain, and leaps onto the creature's back. From here*
*he plans to cover the beastmouse's eyes so that his*
*allies can get some good hits in - but he'll have to hang*
*on tight!*
### SWALLOW
Only available to hazards, and even then quite rarely,
this allows them to gulp down a bug and keep them
trapped until their teammates help them escape... Or
until the bug finds a way back out!

## DEATH & DEFEAT!
PICO is a pretty upbeat game, but it's not without its darker moments, and it's definitely not without consequences
for unfortunate events.
What 'winning' looks like in a rumble depends entirely on the nature of the scene - what exactly *does*  happen when
that track is finally fully marked? If it's a chase, someone will probably get caught; if it's a sporting event, there will
likely be a losing team... And if it's a real, proper scrap, then someone's going to end up hurt.
Use the Safety Tools on page 193 to determine how much of this kind of stuff you want to show, and what level of
detail the table is okay with in terms of description.
But what if the worst does come to pass, and for one of the player characters? When every heart they have is burned,
or when they're in a narratively deadly situation? Well...
### DEATH, AVOIDED
Death in PICO is a player choice.
You *can*  die, but the mechanics won't force you to. Instead, if you fully burn all of your aspects, you can curl up
into a ball and hide for the rest of the scene. Then at the start of the next scene you completely clear a single
heart for free and can act normally again (though you're still extremely banged up).
This is the standard way of things, but we encourage you to deal with death (and avoiding it) in a way that suits your
table. They're your characters, after all, and it's your story - I just wrote this book. You might consider...
	» Having a character curl up and hide when all of their aspects are marked, rather than burned.
	» Having each character have one 'lucky escape', and then suffering
permanent death the next time they're in the same position.
	» Ruling that fully burning an aspect removes it from that bug forever, so
death isn't a concern, but loss of power and progress is.

## TIPS, TRICKS, & GENERAL ADVICE
When tiny fists start flying and beastly fangs are bared, the situation may seem overwhelming at first. If that's the
case, keep the following tips and tricks in mind...
## SO... ARE THESE RULES JUST FOR RUMBLES?
Well, it's funny you should ask that!
No.
While attacking, defending, taking damage, and inflicting effects work really well as a baseline for rumbles, they can
also be thrown into any situation where they would be appropriate. Want to test your strength at a bugly carnival
as part of a montage? The Weaver might ask you to treat it as an attack! A choice on a journey leads to worrying
consequences? Well, that's probably going to deal some damage to one of your aspects.
And, similarly, not every action you take in a rumble is going to be to attack or defend, or will have the opportunity of
marking a track.
Think of all these special rumble rules as useful options for simulating dangerous, high-tension situations.
*The rules for other types of scenes, like the aforementioned montages and journeys, begin just a page-flip away.*
### TOO MUCH CUT? LOWER IT!
Some of the hazards in this book have pretty hefty
defences of their own, including the ability to impose
multiple kinds of cut at once (such as a swift, iron-
plated beast imposing both Speed and Armour Cut on
actions taken to damage it).
But as bugs, you have *multiple*  options open to you
where lowering cut is concerned, and the Weaver has a
couple of their own as well. You could...
Use effects to put a target into a state where its cut
won't apply. That iron-plated beast? It's going to have
a weak point somewhere, so use an effect to find it and
then future attacks to focus on it.
Change your approach when you need to. Bugly heads
are famously bad at smashing through brick walls, but a
clever approach can deny an opponent certain kinds of
cut even without an effect in play. If you're not sure, ask
the Weaver for some cryptic advice!
Choose aspects carefully when progressing your
character! Many of the aspects that make your bug

### CREATE AND USE POIs
Points of interest are there to make scenes more
interactive, and to give players the chance to have their
bug do things that wouldn't usually be possible. They're
an excellent tactical tool, and clever use of a POI can
change the course of a rumble completely.
### FOCUS ON A HAZARD'S WEAKNESSES
Once you've found a weakness, exploit it for a steady
source of giga damage for as long as it lasts. Some
aspects allow you to detect weaknesses in others, but
common sense goes a long way too!
### WORK AS A TEAM
The obvious application of teamwork in a rumble is to
fill Combo Tracks (page 46) and get some big, solid hits
in, but it goes far beyond that. Set up opportunities for
your fellow bugs, and work to get them out of trouble
where you can. Keeping a united front in a rumble can
overcome just about anything.
### USE GIGA IMPACT!
Even a single instance of giga impact can dramatically
change the course of a rumble and, in some cases,
end it in an instant. It's not always easy to achieve, but
with Combo Tracks, weaknesses, points of interest,
special aspects, and clever approaches, your bugs
are *far*  stronger than they might first appear. Just
because you're small, doesn't mean you're not mighty!
