### 83 {farbe=#494344}
# GET OUT &
# SEE THE WORLD
A journey is a special type of scene that lets bugs
travel from one location to another. You don't need to
use a journey if the band are just walking down the road,
or going from one bugly house to a nearby park - that
kind of tiny jaunt can happen completely off camera,
smoothed over with a single line of description from the
Weaver.
No, journeys are there for the big ones - the dangerous
road from settlement to settlement, the search for a
dungeon in the distant, dense forest, or the hunt for a
predator the bugs have sworn to bring to justice.
## JOURNEY RULES
Like montages, journeys run on different rules than
typical moment-to-moment play. Time and Distance are
suddenly important (though are still measured loosely,
so as not to burden the Weaver with too much book-
keeping). Picotitans also have a huge impact on play
here, with even the newest picotitan speeding progress
through a journey significantly. *You'll find a full chapter*
*on picotitans starting on page 171.*
## ROLES
There are certain times during a journey that players will
be asked to decide which bug is taking on a specific role,
which usually requires making a decision that will affect
the entire band.
We recommend assigning roles as a group to whoever
is happy to take them in that moment. You could also
leave it up to the Weaver to decide, or have a system of
'I did it last time so I'm choosing you to do it this time'.
Whatever works for your table is fine, but every now and
then a player's bug will have a benefit or two, or a skill,
that make them more suited to a particular role.
And if you've got strengths, why not play to them?
## LIGHT & WEATHER
With the time-based mechanics that journeys
introduce, light level and current weather come into
play (and may persist into other scenes taken as part of,
or after, a journey). *You'll find rules on light and weather*
*at the end of this chapter.*

### 84 {farbe=#494344}
# THE BASICS OF TRAVEL
*The fear of being far from home, balanced with the excitement of the new? That's just bread and butter to a bug.*
Bugs band together in small groups when they travel, setting off from one place and crossing the big, dangerous
world with a specific destination in mind (usually, anyway - there's value to wandering about the place aimlessly from
time to time).
Some of them do it on foot, covering huge distances at a quick march and stopping to camp when they're tired,
grilling tasty bits over an open fire as they tell stories with their friends. Others take the faster route, buying or raising
a picotitan to travel on - a huge but helpful creature that's strong enough to carry a little bug house on its back, the
height of mobile luxury.
*Travel works pretty much the same way whether you're using a picotitan or*
*not, in terms of mechanics. But, as a rule, travelling with a titan is faster*
*and safer than going it alone.*
## WHY GO ON JOURNEYS?
For some bugs, staying within the safe walls of a settlement
is the most sensible course of action - they're not adventuring
types, after all. They want a quiet life with the comfort of home,
and maybe a tiny mug of cocoa at the end of each day.
But those aren't the kind of bugs that PICO helps you tell
stories about.
The bugs you're playing as are adventuresome types - they
might still be afraid of the wider world, but they're
willing to face that fear and get out there, to see
sights and smell smells that others could only
dream of.
They're the *cool*  ones, essentially.
As an adventuresome bug, you might undertake a
journey to...
	» Discover a new place
	» Escape the routine
	» Aid your home community
	» Solve a grand mystery
	» Discover an exciting new thing to eat
	» Challenge yourself
	» Help those in need
	» Find cool stuff
	» Bond with your picotitan
Journeys in PICO usually need a destination - a physical place you're
heading to - but sometimes, venturing out on a journey with nothing but
a nebulous goal in mind is fine. I guess you'll just keep going until
you find what you want!

# TIME & DISTANCE
These are the two most important components of a journey in PICO - the distance you cover, and the time it takes
to cover it. The Weaver keeps a watch over both of these, but you should probably know how they work as a player
too - they are tracked out on the table for everyone to see (usually, anyway).
## STRIDES
Distance is measured in strides, an arcane length that's difficult to precisely judge for the average bug, but works
well when you're guessing, giving vague directions, and telling tall tales.
At the start of any journey, the Weaver will set a Journey Track, a set of linked boxes representing distance. One box
on this track is the length of a stride, and when bugs get a wiggle on and make progress, these boxes are marked.
When all boxes are marked, the journey is over - you've covered enough strides to reach your destination!
The Weaver decides the length of a Journey Track by choosing how many boxes to add - the longer the track, the
more strides you'll have to cover, the further away your destination is! Because they're tracked out in the open, most
of the time the Weaver should also give them a name (usually based on where you're journeying to).

## TICKS
Time is measured in ticks, which would be wonderfully accurate if bugs carried pocket watches around.
They don't.
Each day in PICO is split up into eight ticks, four of them taking place during the day and four of them taking
place during the night. At the start of a journey, once they've set the journey track, the Weaver will also set a ticking
clock - a physical representation of passing time shown by a half-circle above the Journey Track, and another half-
circle below. The segments of these half-circles are marked clockwise as time passes, one for each tick, just
like the face of a very scribbled-upon clock.
Every in-game sunrise, the marks on the ticking clock are erased and a new day begins. Some benefits and special
abilities are stronger during light or darkness, and some things can only happen once per day!

## THE LEADER
When you're chosen as the leader, the power is in your
hands! It's your job to decide on the travel actions your
band of bugs takes throughout the journey, setting the
pace and deciding when it's time to rest. *A group might*
*decide that a journey has one leader for the duration, or*
*that the leader changes every time they make camp, or*
*even that the leader changes after the current leader*
*chooses a single travel action, so everyone gets a turn*
*at the head of the band. We recommend discussing*
*how you're choosing the leader, and how often that role*
*changes hands, before you play.*
### ONCE YOU HAVE A LEADER...
Then they can choose a travel action, from the list on
the right.
Each travel action takes a certain amount of time
(measured in ticks) and covers a certain amount of
distance (measured in strides).
	» For every stride it covers, mark a box on the
Journey Track.
	» For every tick it takes, mark the ticking clock.
Some of these travel actions are focused on movement,
getting you to your destination as quickly, or as
safely, as possible. Others are based around rest
and relaxation, offering you a bit of a break from the
constant movement and letting time slip by.
### EVERYBODY ELSE
So what does everybody else do while the leader is
setting the pace? Well, they're all on the lookout -
keeping their eyes open (if they have eyes, of course) for
any interesting things the band might encounter during
a particular leg of the journey. *We'll learn more about*
*Lookout Rolls, and surprise, on pages 88 to 91.*
# TICKING CLOCKS & MAKING STRIDES
*Ah, the open road.*
Once the Weaver has set your Journey Track and ticking clock, it's time to start making some progress!
Your band of bugs, whether astride a picotitan or moving on foot, makes progress as a group - heading off solo in a
world of predators is hardly the wisest idea, after all! But groups can get a little messy when everyone wants to take
charge, so PICO requires the group to assign a special role at the beginning of a journey - the leader.
## TRAVEL ACTIONS
### RAMBLE {farbe=#008a99}
Your group advances at a gentle pace, taking in the
surroundings as you do.
Lookout Rolls: Yes
Surprise!: No
Distance Covered: 1 stride
Time Taken: 1 tick
### MARCH {farbe=#008a99}
You move swiftly and seriously, making good progress
in a surprisingly short amount of time.
Lookout Rolls: Yes
Surprise!: Yes
Distance Covered: 2 strides
Time Taken: 1 tick
### WAIT {farbe=#008a99}
You stay in one place, and you stay alert.
Lookout Rolls: Yes
Surprise!: No
Distance Covered: None
Time Taken: 1 tick
### CAMP {farbe=#008a99}
Your group takes a breather and sets up camp, starting
a montage (details found starting page 69). All bugs
can spend a task as normal during this montage, or any
number of bugs can choose to spend their task being
*on the lookout* . If there are no bugs on the lookout, you'll
get surprised by any visitors or events. If there are bugs
on the lookout, you won't be surprised.
Lookout Rolls: Maybe
Surprise!: Maybe
Distance Covered: None
Time Taken: 2 ticks

### 87 {farbe=#494344}
### MAKING PROGRESS
Rambling and marching both allow your little band of
bugs to make headway on a journey, trading some time
and effort for marks on the Progress Track.
But which should you use? That depends very much on
the situation, and the experience you want from your
journey.
Rambling is the safest way to make progress, but it isn't
exactly fast. You'll be covering one stride at a time; great
for spotting oddities and opportunities along the way,
not so great if you're on a time crunch of some kind.
The biggest benefit of rambling is the ability to choose
how (or even if) you engage with what you encounter, a
reward for taking it slow.
Marching is the opposite of rambling, in that you
move with speed but pay for it with an increase in
danger. Covering two strides at a time gets you to your
destination faster, and also means you'll have fewer
encounters along the way. Unfortunately, the pace you
keep means that some of those encounters you'll likely
blunder right into the middle of, as you'll have no time to
prepare in advance or skirt round the edge of whatever
is happening. *This is covered more on page 91, under*
*the Surprise! heading - but you'll need to learn about*
*Lookout Rolls first, on the next page.*
### - TRAVEL ACTIONS EXAMPLE -

objective in the distance, the door to the

setting the Journey Track at 8 boxes.
Layla:

tomorrow's dawn. Who's going to lead us here?
Dunf:
Ooh, not me! I want to keep my eyes on the
surroundings, I like it here.
Swift:
I'll take the lead if nobody objects.

Swift:
There's a temptation to march - we do want to

all - but we'll start off a little slower. Ramble, I
think, in the interests of not being surprised.
Weaver:	Ramble it is! I'm marking one stride on the
Journey Track and a segment of the ticking
clock. Everybody else, get your dice ready for
some Lookout Rolls!
## TRAVEL BY TITAN {farbe=#008a99}
Journeys are where Picotitans *shine* .
If your band of bugs has access to a friendly picotitan, crossing vast distances is both quicker and safer. The
creature itself can move faster across terrain than even the swiftest bugs (even if that's simply due to a disparity in
size and stride-length), and if you've got a titan-den built onto their back that gives you a solid, safe place to camp
out when it's time to rest or sleep. Some picotitans even have special benefits that make travel even easier!
We'll save most of the specifics for the Picotitan chapter, starting on page 171, but there are two rules that are
important for right now...
### PICOTITANS ARE FAST!
Riding a picotitan lets bugs cover ground at an
impressive clip. When choosing a Ramble or March
travel action, the leader can decide whether your band
rides the picotitan or not. If you do, you can...
	» Increase the amount of strides a Ramble covers to 2
	» Increase the amount of strides a March covers to 4
What does this mean? Journeys go quicker, and with
fewer interruptions - simple as!
### TITAN-DENS ARE SAFE!
What better to ensure a good rest than a soft bed on the
back of a friendly titan? When choosing a Wait or Camp
travel action, the leader can decide whether your band
uses a picotitan's titan-den. If you do, you can...
	» Ensure that you can't be surprised by an event or
hazard, as your picotitan will notice and warn you
What does this mean? Basically that you don't have
to have a bug assigned as a lookout to prevent being
surprised.

## ON THE LOOKOUT
What, you thought the leader was going to get all the fun?
When you're rambling, marching, or waiting during a journey, every bug that isn't the leader is on the lookout. This
means they're keeping their eyes open for anything that might be useful, interesting, or potentially dangerous - it's
how you find friends, foes, and happenstances while journeying through the world of PICO. Mechanically, bugs
that are on the lookout each get to make a Lookout Roll when the leader chooses the ramble, march, or wait travel
actions for the group.
*Example: Kale, Inigo, and Melody are three bugs journeying through the rough terrain of the Hogwild. Kale takes the*
*position of leader, so he makes the decisions as to whether they ramble, march, wait, or camp. Inigo and Melody are*
*then on the lookout as they all travel, so it's up to them to keep watch as they move. When Kale chooses to move*
*them into a march so they get to their destination a little quicker, Inigo and Melody both make a Lookout Roll to see*
*what they encounter along the way.*
Lookout Rolls are automatically taken whenever a band of bugs rambles, marches, or waits. If you're taking the
camp action on a journey, a bug can spend a task to go on the lookout (which allows them to make a roll), but it's
not mandatory. Think of going on the lookout while camping as going on watch, letting others busy themselves with
personal stuff while you keep your eyes open for danger.

## LOOKOUT ROLLS
Each bug that goes on the lookout gets to roll 2d6 - the
Lookout Roll. The results of this roll determine what
that bug notices as their band moves or rests.
	» If the results of this roll are two different
numbers, you haven't spotted anything. Not
anything worth talking about, anyway - you may see
some cute scenery or drifting clouds, but nothing
worth more than some idle chatter. But...
	» If the results of this roll are doubles, of any value,
you've spotted something interesting! You can
check the table on the right to find out exactly what
you've managed to see, but it'll likely lead to an
encounter or choice (you'll find details on page 90).
Remember, Lookout Rolls are always taken by every
other bug when a leader chooses the ramble, march,
or wait travel actions, but are optional during a camp
action (requiring a camper to spend their montage task).
### SPECIAL SENSES
Some bugs are better at Lookout Rolls than others,
rolling additional die or gleaning extra information with
certain results. A few can even take additional actions
when they're on the lookout, like snaffling down a bit
for some healing without it distracting them from
their surroundings. But remember, even if your bug is
particularly adept at going on the lookout, you can still
take the leader position from time to time for a bit of a
change. Niches are good, but so is personal choice!

### 90 {farbe=#494344}
## ENCOUNTERS & CHOICES
After you've made your Lookout Rolls, it's the Weaver's job to tie those results into the fiction of the game. Some steps
of a journey will pass without incident, others might require a choice to be made, and the most involved will have
the Weaver presenting the bugs with an encounter of some kind. *We'll deal with the Weaver's portion of journeys on*
*page 92 - don't worry, we haven't forgotten you.*
If part of a journey passes without incident - with no doubles on the Lookout Roll, and with no additional elements
added by the Weaver - then you simply mark whatever needs marking on the Journey Track and ticking clock, then
take another travel action if you've still got some distance to go. But life is rarely that simple, especially when doubles
are rolled...
### CHOICES
Sometimes a lookout result will prompt the Weaver to give your group a choice. This might affect a bug, the entire
group of bugs, or the wider narrative, and might be decided by everybody at the table or a single individual (whatever
seems to work for the moment). A choice on a journey might look something like this...
*Example: Luther managed a double 6 on his Lookout Roll, which means he spotted 'an unexpected landmark'.*
*The Weaver sees this as a good opportunity to give Luther a personal choice to make - either the landmark reveals*
*something troubling (but useful to know) about the area they're travelling through, or destroying the landmark will*
*give the band a high-value chunk of resources that they might use to improve their picotitan. This essentially gives*
*Luther's player the chance to either take a guaranteed benefit (with a frisson of associated antisocial behaviour), or*
*a more nebulous bit of information that might help them down the line.*
Some choices might be between two beneficial states, others two negative outcomes. Some might even be a
choice of instantaneous or future reward. The important bit with these choices is that they're made in place of an
encounter - whatever's been spotted shouldn't be investigated further after the choice has been made.
### ENCOUNTERS
More complex than choices, an encounter is essentially a small scene that plays out before the next travel action can
be chosen. This might involve Action Rolls, acquiring resources, overcoming challenges, or even facing hazards - an
encounter can be anything related to what's been spotted (or what additional elements the Weaver might add). Let's
wind time back a little and look at that same example, but this time as an encounter rather than a choice...
*Example: Luther managed a double 6 on his Lookout Roll, which means he spotted 'an unexpected landmark'.*
*The Weaver sees this as a good opportunity to give the group an encounter to navigate - the landmark spotted, a*
*miniature castle made out of tin cans and wire, is ineffectually guarded by a sleeping termite in an ill-fitting hat. The*
*group might choose to ramble on by, to wake the termite with questions, to storm the castle and hope for riches*
*inside, or any one of a hundred other approaches they think of. The Weaver has presented the situation, and it's up to*
*them to decide how they act and how much narrative weight they give what they've found.*
Encounters are open-ended by nature, and usually contain potential benefits and drawbacks. They might pass by
quickly or, if the bugs really get stuck in to a situation, take up a decent chunk of the session.
### CHOICE / ENCOUNTER THEORY
What's this, a sidebar on game design? Not quite - more of a pointer on how best to use choices and encounters
within a session, for both Weavers and bugs. Choices on a journey are a bit like montages in scope - they condense
what might otherwise be an encounter down into a few sentences of back and forth, which is great for keeping
the pace of a game high when that's needed. Encounters are more like scenes, where a lot can be accomplished
through creativity, but longer exchanges might take up quite a bit of real-life time. The Weaver can use choices and
encounters to help control the flow of a journey, and may even tailor which is offered due to the group's interest in
certain elements of the world or whether they have a time-sensitive goal.

## DO YOU ENGAGE?
An important question, this one. If the Weaver presents
you with an encounter, you've got an important decision
to make - or, to be more accurate, you *might*  have an
important decision to make.
When rambling, waiting, or camping with someone
on the lookout, the bugs will often have the chance to
decide how they're engaging with a spotted element,
or even if they engage at all! They've seen it from a
distance, after all - they've got time to prepare, and
maybe to avoid it entirely.
But when marching, or camping without a lookout,
any new elements introduced will be a surprise - the
bugs have either blundered straight into something or
they have an unexpected visitor at the campsite, and
they're forced to engage.
### SURPRISE! {farbe=#83946a}
When bugs have no time to prepare, or are caught off
guard, they're briefly in a state known as surprise.
Some hazards have special benefits that only activate
when they have the element of surprise on their side.
It works both ways too though! If the bugs are prepared,
and can choose how they engage with a spotted
element, they might choose to take the element of
surprise themselves with a dramatic entrance!
Having the element of surprise may increase your
impact, give you extra dice on an Action Roll, or allow
you to achieve something that wouldn't normally be
possible when there are eyes on you. But surprise only
lasts for a single action, so make the most of it.

## THE WEAVER'S ROLE
Once the Lookout Roll results are in, the spotlight passes to the Weaver. They have two jobs to do each time a travel
action is chosen and lookout results come in, the first being optional (adding special results) and the second being
mandatory (describing progress). We'll tackle how to do both of these right now!
### ADDING SPECIAL RESULTS
Lookout Rolls are great at telling you what bugs have spotted, but that's not the whole story. The world of PICO is
a living, breathing one (sometimes literally), with a whole lot more going on than even the finest bugly eyes or the
quiveriest of antennae could ever hope to capture.
To represent this, the Weaver can add one or more special results during each section of travel, things that are
happening in the world (and that will likely affect the band as they make progress) but that the lookouts may not have
spotted on their first sweep. These results can be added purely at random or in secret, semi-randomly according to
the local likelihood, or without any hint of randomness with the aim of telling an unfolding story in mind.
### ADDING SECRET AND RANDOM RESULTS
This is especially useful if the band of travelling bugs
is a small one (in terms of number - we kind of assume
they're small in terms of size). If there are only one or
two bugs making Lookout Rolls then doubles will turn
up less frequently, and a few additional results help
keep journeys feeling less empty.
To add secret random results, simply roll a d6 secretly
(if you have a GM screen, this is the perfect time to use
it) and treat whatever the result is as a double of that
number, then check the lookout results table as normal.
*Example: A bugly duo makes their way across the*
*blasted plains of the Skyglass Dome. With only one*
*leader and one lookout, they won't get doubles that*
*frequently, but the Weaver rolls an additional random*
*d6 for each of their ramble and march actions, giving*
*them more potential encounters to engage with. They*
*might not always notice what's happening nearby, but*
*it sure notices them!*
### ADDING LOCAL LIKELIHOOD RESULTS
Some areas are just more likely to contain particular
encounters - beasts out in the wild, NPCs and looming
hazards in the night-time streets of a dangerous
bugtropolis.
If you want to spur more lookout results that suit the
area, you can choose and add a single 'floating number',
from 1 to 6, that is added to all Lookout Rolls. This
floating number can be counted as part of a double if it
matches any of the naturally rolled results.
*Example: Onio's band are travelling through the deep*
*woods, picking their way between drifts of fallen*
*autumn leaves. The Weaver decides that the area*
*they're travelling through is a veritable treasure box of*
*potential resources (even if they are mostly hidden by*
*the leaves), so adds a floating 5 to all of the Lookout*
*Rolls made in the locale. This considerably increases*
*the likelihood of a double 5, making the 'Trove of Stuff'*
*lookout result far easier to obtain.*
### ADDING UNFOLDING STORY RESULTS
Particularly useful for groups that are less keen on randomness and have a strong narrative drive, this is really just a
dressed-up way of letting the Weaver decide exactly what each stage of a journey brings without having to rely on
the dice - excellent for when there's a particular story that they want to tell.
What's important to remember here is that the Weaver's chosen story-based encounters don't *replace*  lookout rolls
entirely, they're instead *flavoured*  by them.
*Example: Lyra and her companions have been on the trail of a horde of beastmice, barbarian rodents rampaging*
*through a series of small insect communities. While on a journey to try to catch up with them, Lookout Rolls take*
*place as normal, but the Weaver also has a few pre-made encounters, landmarks, and choices they want to throw*
*into the mix. These help the bugs gather information about the beastmice as they travel, and keep the flow of*
*narrative consistent and the current goal of the party at the forefront of play.*

### DESCRIBING PROGRESS
Once the Weaver knows what's been spotted (through a combination of Lookout Rolls and any secret results they've
added), it's their job to describe the group's progress. This is partly informed by the environment the bugs are moving
through - maybe a thicket in the middle of a forest, or a dried-up riverbed - and partly by those recently rolled
results. This gives the Weaver a chance to introduce any new situations or challenges that a travelling band might
face, combining the mechanical outcomes of dice rolls, the fiction of the world, and a hefty dose of upcoming player
choice (whether the bugs engage with what they find, which is dealt with on the next page).
If you're new to GMing, that might sound intense. Don't worry too much - we'll break it down for you below.
	» If nothing was spotted during a round of Lookout Rolls, the Weaver has free reign to introduce whatever they like
- or to say that a segment of the journey has passed without incident.
	» If one lookout spotted something, the Weaver can describe the element in more detail and the players decide
whether they want to engage with what they've found, or pass on by. Engaging may require anything from a
simple decision to an entire scene being played out before the journey continues.
	» If multiple lookouts spotted something, the Weaver combines the results into a single element - the bugs then
make a choice as to whether they engage with it or not (duplicate results might mean multiple bugs spotted the
same thing, or that there are multiple instances of that thing nearby).
*Example: The Weaver describes how the band are passing through*
*the hollow of an ancient, fallen tree. One of the bugs on lookout*
*rolls a 6, to spot something interesting, and then a 3 on*
*the lookout results table - a change in the weather. The*
*Weaver describes how the bugs can see the leaves*
*above them begin to rustle, then fall; there's a gale*
*building up, but they're safe for now within the*
*wooden battlements of the fallen tree. Once*
*they go outside, though...*

### 94 {farbe=#494344}
# WEATHER
*“Clams crossed by storms, a storm for every clam. Sorry, calm. Damn. Messed that one up.”*
From wind to rain, from heat to cold, the weather of the world wreaks havoc on tiny creatures. When raindrops are the
size of your head, finding shelter becomes not just a matter of comfort, but a matter of survival.
## DECIDING THE WEATHER {farbe=#3b413f}
Three different forces can change the weather in the world of PICO...
	» The Results of a Lookout Roll: A lookout roll of double 3s signals a change in whatever weather the bugs are
currently experiencing, but it might be one they can avoid by taking shelter (especially if they're not surprised).
*Remember, the lookout result might change the weather for the better or for the worse, but there are some*
*special abilities within this book that give players a little more control.*
	» Entering a Microclimate: Some of the realms within chapter 11 of this book have special rules where weather is
concerned (and for light and darkness, which we'll tackle on the next page). *A place directly beneath a sprinkler*
*system may always be rainy, for example.*
	» Through the Will of the Weaver: Sometimes, the heavens just change. The Weaver can, at any point, bring a new
weather type into play (or end an existing one).*They might do this to add a layer of challenge to an otherwise*
*standard encounter, to ratchet up the tension, or to throw in some thunder-and-lightning based drama... Or*
*simply because after a few days of rain, everyone at the table is up for some sunny skies.*
### WEATHER AND MOOD {farbe=#3b413f}
A bug's mood (page 156) is often affected by the weather - whenever a new weather system comes into play, the
Weaver may hit every bug in the band with a positive or negative mood pull. The direction of the pull isn't hard-coded
though - a rainstorm might usually pull moods to the negative, but if it comes after days of scorching heatwave it
might be a positive-pulling relief!

The heavens open and the rains pour down, while the

## WEATHER SYSTEMS {farbe=#3b413f}
When a new type of weather comes into play, it usually brings some special rules with it. Each weather system has a
few different rules that a Weaver might use (except for Calm Skies, as you'll see below) to represent the mechanical
effects it might have on scenes, montages, and journeys. These are split into positive and negative effects (+ and -),
and some bugs are lucky enough to avoid the negatives of various weather types altogether.
So when the skies change, a band of bugs might have to contend with...
### CALM SKIES
The sun shines, clouds move, and all is right with the world. Calm Skies is the default position for weather, with no
special rules for bugs or Weaver to follow.

### 96 {farbe=#494344}
# DAYLIGHT & DARKNESS
*“It was written I should be loyal to the nightmare of my choice. That's bats, for me. Scary buggers, bats.”*
Some bugs wake up at the crack of dawn, pulling back curtains (if they have them) to witness a wash of gold
and orange spreading over the huge world around them. Night birds retreat, the air gets warmer, and numerous
dangerous creatures head to bed.
But bathing the world in glorious light is a stressful job, so for about half of every day the sun takes a little break from
shining and goes and hides somewhere out of sight. Come on - it's earned it. And some bugs *prefer*  that period of
darkness; it's more dangerous, yes, but it's also somehow simultaneously more serene.
All this is to say that the world of the many-legged doesn't stop for night or day, it merely marks their passing. Both
come with their rewards, their challenges, and their dangers... and that's what these pages are all about.
## LIGHT IS IMPORTANT (DARKNESS IS TOO)
Every scene or montage your bug takes part in will happen either in daylight or darkness and, when you head out
travelling on a journey, you'll probably experience both multiple times. There are no inherent rules to remember that
distinguish between daylight and darkness, but there are many cases where an aspect, a location, or a hazard might
have rules that change depending on the level of light. A particular stretch of path might be safe by day, but overrun
by foxes at night. A draconic starling might only strike at midday, screaming down at prey from the cover of the sun
overhead. And a bug that seems sleepy and torpid from dawn to dusk might suddenly come alive at night, gaining
extra dice on their rolls and having their mood shoot up in delight.

## GOING UNDERGROUND
There are some places where the rhythms of the world
fade away, places deep beneath the surface that
have never felt the touch of light save what's shed by
luminescent mushrooms and fast-burning matches.
When bugs head into a place that has a roof or ceiling
that cuts off the sky and no strong light source, they're
assumed to be in darkness constantly - even if it's
daytime up above.
This is great for some bugs, but *terrible*  for others
(especially those used to swanning around on the
surface world). Luckily, there are a few ways around it...
	» Finding an area with luminous mushrooms might
allow a bug to count themselves as under daylight for
a short time, if the mushrooms are bright enough.
	» Lighting a match or torch can provide a brilliant burst
of brightness at the right time, and definitely counts
as daylight until it fizzles out. Remember, it might
not look impressive to you or I but, to a bug, that's an
incandescent flare they're holding.
	» Finally, some bugs can make their own light! Some
have shells that shine like a beacon, others have
weird glowing glands, or leave trails of iridescent
slime... it takes all sorts to make a world, and it takes
*these*  particular sorts to let their friends bask in false
daylight for a bit when necessary.
## EYES & VISION
I bet you thought we'd get to this one sooner! Well,
there's a reason that we haven't. For ease of use, all
bugs are assumed to see just well enough in the dark
that it doesn't make rolls or actions any more difficult to
pull off.
This is a baseline though - just like any other part of
the world, personal choices trump general rules. Some
bugs might elect to take a set of eyes that's particularly
powerful at night, giving them increased visual acuity
in darkness, but that leaves them constantly dazzled
during the day. Another might go eyeless, ignoring
light-based effects entirely. A particular predatory
beast's coat might even let it blend in under the cover of
darkness, or turn light to a hypnotic surge...
Basically, if you want daylight and darkness to affect
your bug on a personal level, reflect (hah!) that when
you choose your aspects.
