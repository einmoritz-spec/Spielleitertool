# BUYING BASICS
This chapter contains the first half of the instructions
for how to build a character for the world of PICO, a
tiny bug with big dreams - the part where you get, and
spend, Nectar.
If this is your first time making a bug for a game
of PICO...
	» Skim through this chapter AND the next chapter, so
that you understand the whole process.
	» Check with the Weaver to see how much Nectar you
have to spend.
	» Spend your Nectar on purchasing aspects,
augments, and skills from this chapter.
	» Decide on the other features of your bug in the next
chapter, starting on page 153 (those ones don't
require you to spend Nectar).
If you've made bugs for PICO before, grab whatever
you want from wherever you want in any particular
order - attack this character creation buffet as you like!
Aspects, augments, and skills are in this chapter, and
everything else starts in the next chapter on page 153.
### TWO WHOLE CHAPTERS?
PICO's character creation system requires a mixture of
spending Nectar and making choices. All of the Nectar
spending is in this chapter, all of the other choices are in
the next chapter.
### SIZE
All bugs are SMALL by default, but there are a few
aspects that allow you to alter your bug's size on page
130. We don't recommend new players use these, as
they may have some unintended effects on the fictive
side of the game, but hey - we can't really stop you!

# HOW TO MAKE A BUG (part 1)
*“The leg bone's connected to the... Oh wait, what's a bone? We've messed that up already.”*
This is where you get to let some of that creativity shine - a chance to build a completely unique bug, one that's just
for you. We can *almost*  promise that no other player in the world will have a bug that's exactly like yours, which seems
appropriate given the vast number of weird little bugs in our own world, and the inherent strangeness of the post-
Mysteriocalypse world.
There are eight steps to making a bug, which may or may not sound like a lot, depending on how good you are at
counting. This chapter covers the first four steps; where you get your Nectar, then spend it on aspects, augments,
and skills. In fact, to break it down in a more useful way...
## STEP ONE: GET YOUR NECTAR {farbe=#eba400}
Nectar is essentially a character creation resource, which can be earned during play for
advancement but is also given out in bulk by the Weaver before you make your bug. The more Nectar
you have to spend, the more choices you can make and the more powerful your bug will likely end up
being. Larva bugs begin with 25 Nectar, Imago bugs begin with 50 Nectar.
*You'll get your starting Nectar on page 102.*
## STEP TWO: PURCHASE YOUR ASPECTS {farbe=#eba400}
Aspects are parts, gear, and buddies that define your bug, which can be purchased with Nectar.
Each comes with a name, a track, and some sort of special rule that makes you unique. These aren't
the only things you're going to be spending Nectar on, so don't go crazy here! A single aspect costs
3 Nectar.
*You'll find an overview of aspects on page 104, and grouped sets of aspects to purchase with Nectar*
*starting on page 108.*
## STEP THREE: PURCHASE YOUR AUGMENTS {farbe=#eba400}
Aspects can be improved by adding augments, extra little rules that help make them more distinct.
Each group of aspects comes with some suggested augments, but you can really add them on from
anywhere as long as you can justify the fiction of it. There are also special augments that can help
theme your bug in a certain direction! Each aspect can have a maximum of two augments, and
each augment costs 2 Nectar.
*You'll find an overview of augments on page 106, and then a set of augments that suit each aspect*
*group starting on page 109.*
## STEP FOUR: PURCHASE YOUR SKILLS {farbe=#eba400}
The last thing you'll be spending Nectar on, skills give you general competencies that let you perform
different types of actions. Each of your skills has a rank, starting at zero, and can be raised to rank
one or rank two by spending Nectar. It costs 1 Nectar to take a skill from rank 0 to rank 1, and 2
Nectar to take a skill from rank 1 to rank 2 (it also costs 3 Nectar to take a skill from rank 2 to
rank 3, but that's disallowed for starting characters).
*You'll find an overview of how to choose and use skills on page 144, then a list of skills divided by*
*category starting on page 148.*

### 102 {farbe=#494344}
# NECTAR
*"... They who control the Nectar control the Picoverse. The Nectar must flow."*
Nectar is a delicious, golden liquid that fills bugs with exactly the right kind of energy they need to develop - to gain
new aspects, to augment existing aspects, and to boost their skills. It's a character creation and advancement
resource, essentially, but one that's actually tangible in the world of PICO. We'll delve in to how you might acquire
Nectar on the next page, but for now we're concerned with your...
## STARTING NECTAR
Before making a character for a game or campaign of PICO, your Weaver will hand out a certain amount of Nectar
to each player. This starting Nectar allows you to build the basics of your bug. You can spend this starting Nectar
as you like, but we recommend about 50% of it should go towards skills and about 50% towards aspects and
augments.
This starting Nectar also defines the game's power level - the more Nectar a bug has to spend, the more advanced
they are, the more cool stuff they can do, and the more of the world of PICO they've likely already seen. The Weaver
can technically give out any amount of Nectar for players to build their bugs, but we suggest choosing from one of
the two options below...
## LARVA (25 Nectar)
With 25 Nectar, a bug will likely have two or three aspects (with a couple of augments for good measure) and a spread
of rank 1 and 2 skills. This is perfect for players new to the world of PICO and those planning longer-term games, as
it keeps starting rules complexity low (while still giving a good amount of options to choose from) and gives plenty
of room for growth. *Bugs starting at 25 Nectar are usually new to the world and probably know very little about it*
*(though there's nothing stopping you from playing a more experienced traveller that just happens to not have found*
*much Nectar along the way).*
## IMAGO (50 Nectar)
With 50 Nectar you can build a much more competent individual, a rugged bugly adventurer with four or five aspects
(and one or two augments on each) and a wider spread of skills, with many at rank 2 - and maybe even some at rank
3, if the Weaver and the group are in agreement. This might suit experienced players that want a more powerful start,
or a one-shot experience for those who have quite a few hours of PICO under their belt already and just want to try
a new kind of bug. *Bugs starting at 50 Nectar have usually travelled a bit, been on a few adventures, and know how*
*the world of PICO operates (though there's nothing stopping you from playing a young bug who just happened to*
*hatch rally, really strong).*
## SPENDING YOUR STARTING NECTAR
We'll remind you of these costs again in the future, but for the sake of completeness you'll be spending Nectar on...
	» Aspects, at 3 Nectar each.
	» Augments, at 2 Nectar each.
	» Skills, with a rank 1 skill costing 1 Nectar, an upgrade from rank 1 to rank 2 costing 2 Nectar, and an upgrade from
rank 2 to rank 3 costing 3 Nectar.

## FINDING MORE NECTAR
Nectar is the sweetest, most delicious, most brain-
tingling substance in the world of PICO. Going far
beyond the sweetness of pollen or even cola, nectar
is a golden liquid produced by flowers when they
witness something truly spectacular. Impress a flower
with a heroic deed and they may just disgorge Nectar,
releasing a glob of the stuff to be eagerly snaffled up by
nearby bugs for later use.
Your starting Nectar gives you enough to build a
character before the game begins (or during a session
zero, see page 192), but Nectar is addictive stuff - bugs
can't help but seek it out once they get a taste. So
where might you find more?
Nectar found during play is essentially a gift from the
Weaver, a reward for besting a challenge or completing
a session. It can't be gathered or scrounged up - it's far
too powerful for that - but might be given out...
	» After the band of bugs defeat a difficult foe
	» After the close of an important arc of the story
	» After a particularly daring or complex plan works
	» After surviving a tough situation
When Nectar is gained, all bugs gain the same amount.
You *could*  run a game with varying power levels from
bug to bug, and Nectar handed out in different amounts
to different characters, but 'rules as written' everybody
gets the same amounts at the same time. 1, 2, or 3
globs of Nectar per session is usually perfect.
### CONSTANT GROWTH
If it suits your group, the Weaver might also hand out
Nectar at the end of every completed session. This
ensures bugs will be constantly growing and changing
as they adventure (as long as they moult to take
advantage of it).
If you're going to do this, we recommend not giving
out more than 1 or 2 Nectar at the end of each
session if you're ALSO giving it as mid-session
rewards, and no more than 3 or 4 if you're only giving
Nectar at the end of each session.
### MOULTING
Once a game begins, Nectar can be used by spending
a task during a montage and moulting, shedding your
old bugly skin and emerging stronger. *You'll find more*
*details on moulting on page 80.*

# ASPECTS & AUGMENTS
*"Brevity is."*
The combination of aspects and augments are what makes your bug unique. Ideally, you should spend about half
of your starting Nectar on your aspects and augments - maybe a little more if you want to keep your skill list on
the minimalistic side. There's also a hard limit of six aspects per bug, but you won't get there for a while!
## ASPECTS
Your aspects are the body parts, carried gear, and little companions that make you *you* . Aspects can be used again
and again without ever being lost, but they do each have a track - when that track is fully marked, you don't get the
benefits of an aspect again until at least one of the boxes has been cleared.
Aspects are used to...
	» Absorb Damage: Their tracks absorb any damage you take in the form of marks, and some even have breaks to be
able to better absorb giga damage. If you ever take more damage than a track can absorb, start converting
some of those marks to burns until all the damage has been absorbed. An aspect's track has its boxes in the
shape of hearts, just to remind you that they're there to represent some kind of insect health.
	» Gain Benefits (and Drawbacks): These are special rules that break the general rules of the game, often in your
favour (but not always). Some aspects have benefits and drawbacks that are always active. Other have ones that
only activate in certain situations, or ones where you need to mark (or burn) an aspect's track to have them come
into play, or meet a certain condition. Unless an aspect explicitly tells you something special needs to happen
before its benefit or drawback can be used, assume that it always applies.
	» Help You With Rolls: Aspects also have a narrative component that lets you grab a bonus 1d6 when you're making
a roll where the aspect might be relevant. A roll to turn aside damage will definitely be helped if you have a shell
of some kind, for example, and you might even describe how you're using some piece of gear or helpful buddy to
absorb an incoming blow. You can only get a bonus of 1d6 from aspects on any roll though, even if more than
one of your aspects might help out.
	» Give You a Pseudoscientific Name: Some aspects can also help you name your bug, giving you some insight into
how absent pre-Mysteriocalypse scholars might have talked about your diminutive form. You'll learn more about
names on page 168.

### ASPECT TYPES
Aspects are split into three types - parts, gear, and
buddies. *You'll find an example of each kind of aspect*
*on the right.*
Part aspects are literally parts of your own bugly body
that are particularly distinctive - they don't describe
every facet of you, but they do describe the bits that are
worth paying attention to.
Gear aspects are pieces of the world that you've
grabbed and kept until they're as much a part of you as
an arm or a leg. Bits and bobs are used, eaten, or lost,
but gear is forever.
Buddy aspects are little friends (little even in terms of
bugs, usually) that travel with you and help you out in a
pinch.

### SPECIFIC OVER GENERAL
Many aspects have benefits that can change how
you play the game, and some that even change how
fundamental parts of the game work. In these cases,
your aspect's benefits and drawbacks always trump
the general rules laid out in this book.
For example, the general rule for healing burns on
your aspects is that you can do it in a settlement by
using a task. But the Straw Hose aspect (shown below)
specifies that you can remove burns on its track by
spending a task near a source of water, and that being
in this situation removes all burns at once.
### STRAW HOSE  ♥♥♥
#### GEAR
Filled with water, which can be spurted out droplet by
droplet or in a wash of extreme force.
Benefit: Burn to drench an area instantly, putting
out fires and washing away dirt. Whenever you take
a montage near a source of water (such as a tap or a
pond), clear all burns on this aspect's track.
The specifics here override the general rules on burns,
and how many you can clear at once. They also change
the way certain montages work for this aspect's owner.
### AUTONOMOUS
Buddy aspects have fewer hearts on their track than
most, but they all have an unwritten special rule - they
have minds of their own, and can often wander
around the environment of their own
accord. They're still a part of your
character though, at the end of
the day - just keep it in mind for
roleplay and clever actions.

## AUGMENTS
Aspects start off pretty good, but we're aiming for *amazing* . That's where augments come in.
Augments are special extras that can be added to an aspect, making its Heart Track longer and adding new benefits
(and maybe drawbacks). *Each augment also has an additional bit of pseudoscientific name for your bug, but we'll*
*deal with that later.*
Each aspect you purchase can have a maximum of two augments attached to it, and once they're on, they're there
to stay! The augments add extra hearts (and sometimes breaks) to the aspect's track, and their benefit is pinned
under the existing benefit from the aspect.
### GROUPED BY ASPECT
The augments in this book are all grouped by the kind of aspect they work best on, so you'll find augments like
'Venomous' and 'Crushing' in the Chompers section. But don't feel held back by this - any augment can be added to
any aspect, even if it comes from a different section. Want a venomous backpack? Be our guest.
Here's an example of an augment, on the left, and that same augment added to one of the example aspects from the
previous page, on the right.

## OVERVIEW
Something about your mouthparts is special - they
draw the eye, or give you abilities one wouldn't normally
expect. When you take a set of Chompers as an aspect,
you're flagging to the rest of the table that you want
food, or biting, to be a big deal for your character.
*If you don't end up choosing an aspect from this page,*
*that doesn't mean you don't have a mouth! A bug*
*with no distinctive Chompers might have anything*
*from a little set of nibbling mouthparts to a big, goofy*
*smile - but whatever you have, it won't give you any*
*mechanical bonuses unless represented by an aspect.*

	» Because food is important to you
	» Because you want to eat something specific
	» Because biting things is fun
	» Because you want to look scary

and what made it so gosh-darn impressive?

# SKILLS
*Gold (gold), always believe in your skills...*
Once you've chosen your aspects and augments, the rest of your starting Nectar should be spent on skills - useful
areas of competence that help your bug interact with the weirdness of the world around them.
There are 24 skills in a game of PICO, and for a starting character each of these begins at rank 0. A rank 0 skill is...
well, it doesn't do much for you, let's just say that.
But when you raise a skill's rank, *that's*  when it becomes important enough to stick on the character sheet. When
you're taking an action and have a related skill that's at least rank 1, you add dice to your pool equal to that
skill's rank (so a rank 1 skill adds 1d6, a rank 2 skill 2d6, and a rank 3 skill 3d6). You can only rely on one skill for a given
action, but the skills themselves are pretty flexible - if you can think of a narratively interesting way that skill would
help, it's probably good to go! In fact, your skills should help you decide what kind of actions your bug will take. Play
to the skills you have and you'll rarely go wrong, even if it means approaching a situation a little differently than the
others in your party.
*Example: Jace is trying to climb a wall, and has the*
*Climb skill at rank 2. When he makes an Action*
*Roll to climb, he adds the 2d6 granted by*
*his Climb skill - simple, right? Julien also*
*needs to get over the wall, but doesn't*
*have ranks in the Climb skill. What*
*Julien does have, though, is ranks in*
*Dig - they describe how their bug burrows*
*into the soil and stone of the wall and worms their way*
*up that way. Ryan doesn't have ranks in Climb or Dig, but*
*does have a particularly useful skill for these situations:*
*Fly! Even though each of them is tackling the same*
*challenge (a wall that needs bypassing), their skills let them*
*approach it in a completely different way.*
## PURCHASING SKILL RANKS

easy enough to remember.
	» Increase a skill from rank 0 to rank 1: 1 Nectar
	» Increase a skill from rank 1 to rank 2: 2 Nectar
	» Increase a skill from rank 2 to rank 3: 3 Nectar
As a general guideline, spending about half
of your starting Nectar on skills should give
you a good spread of useful competencies...
As long as you make sure to take them from
different categories.

### COVER YOUR BASES
The skills of PICO are split into categories for a reason
- bugs are generally more effective in an adventure if
they focus on at least one skill from each category,
as a good baseline. That doesn't mean a character can't
dip into multiple skills from the same category (a rough,
brawling bug will likely have multiple rumbling skills, for
example), but it does mean that a good spread will help
out in a variety of situations.

## CHOOSING YOUR SKILLS
The skills in PICO cover all of the common activities a bug might find themselves engaged in during an adventure.
Some of them are quite niche - Swim is probably only useful if there's water around, or something enough *like*  water
that you won't mind taking a dip in it. Others are broader - Yap covers a whole range of speech related activities, from
arguing to telling jokes to talking with your mouth full.
So how do you choose which skills are right for you?
Look at Your Aspects (and Augments): There's a reason we had you pick these first - many aspects and augments
relate to a specific category of skills, or sometimes even a single lone skill. If you've got an aspect that lets you treat
Dig as Gigadig, probably best to buy at least one rank in the Dig skill so you can use it.
Think About Cool Stuff You Want To Do: When you close your eyes and imagine your bug, what are they doing?
Reading peacefully by the fire, knocking back pollenwine in a tiny bar, pirouetting through a battlefield with a tiny
sword in hand? Pick skills that relate to the things you want to do once the game begins.
Think About Team Composition: Unless you're playing solo, you're going to have a band of other bugs around you.
The best insect adventurers work well together, focusing on different skills and approaches so that no matter the
situation, there's always a solution at hand. If you're building characters together, maybe have a discussion about the
kind of skills other people are taking.
Dabble In Each Category: You can focus wherever you like, and there's no hard rule saying you need to have at least
one each of the moving, friending, finding, and rumbling skills... But it does tend to make life easier, and gives you a
much higher chance of having something you're at least *passable*  at in every scenario you'll find yourself in.
## USING YOUR SKILLS
As previously mentioned, skills are an important component of most Action Rolls (offering a number of dice equal
to their rank if related to the action you're taking). But using a skill to get some dice doesn't just give your action a
mechanical boost, it gives it *narrative flavour!*
This flavour is a starting point for you to build upon with your description. Okay, you're using that particular skill, but
HOW are you using it? Trying to defend yourself against a swooping bird with Shield might have you curling up into a
ball, protected by your shell. You might be raising a tool aloft instead, putting it between you and the bird. You might
be hunkering down under cover, or flattening yourself out over the ground to make it harder to get scooped up. It's all
shielding, at the end of the day, and *how*  you describe your actions can have as much impact on the situation as the
specific skill you're using.
### SKILLS ARE FLEXIBLE
If you're performing an action and a skill might be useful, but you're not sure, ask the Weaver! PICO wants you to
use skills in weird and interesting ways. Attack a giant beast with Dazzle, distract approaching froglins with Yap, use
Scare to tell a spooky campfire story. Don't worry if it's a stretch - if it sounds even *vaguely*  possible, it probably is!
You *can*  make an Action Roll without a skill, but that should be reserved for really desperate situations where you're
completely outside of your comfort zone.

## SKILLS AS DAMAGE
Skills in PICO also act as 'damage types' when you're
affecting a track of some kind (be that the health tracks
of a hazard you're facing or a Weaver-set track for a
specific situation, like a race or argument).
An action that marks a track usually affects one box,
but some tracks might be affected more, or less,
by certain skills. This might come in the form of one
mark more than usual, or even one mark less. It might
also be divorced from the concept of marking, and be
represented by reading triumph results as conflicts, or
by ignoring cut. There are a lot of variations out there to
contend with! Here's an example...
*Dunf is trying to Bash a heron as it attacks him, but the*
*heron's feathers give it resistance to Bash Damage. His*
*attack hits home, and the heron reacts by staggering*
*back a little, but no boxes on the heron's track are*
*marked - Dunf's usual one mark he would make is*
*lowered by the heron's resistance, to zero.*
In essence, you can think of any track - whether it's for a
hazard, a situation, or an event - as having the potential
to be weak or resistant to certain skills. Some might
even be immune to a skill completely, with that skill
never making a mark under any circumstance!
## GIGASKILLS
Every now and then you'll be able to treat a skill as a
gigaskill, representing you being so effective with that
skill in a particular moment that your power approaches
that of a cat rather than the bug you are.
Gigaskills don't just mark one box when they affect a
track - they mark every box, up to either the next break
or the end of the track. They ignore weakness and
resistance too (though not immunity, keep that in
mind). Here's an example...
*Dunf, thanks to perfect conditions and a well-chosen*
*aspect, can treat his Bash as Gigabash for a brief*
*moment. The heron trying to gobble him up rears back*
*to strike... And he slams into it with a Gigabash, an*
*entirely unexpected level of power. The heron's track is*
*six boxes long, and every single one of those is marked*
*by Dunf's prodigious attack. The heron staggers back,*
*crosses its eyes, and slumps down in a heap.*
