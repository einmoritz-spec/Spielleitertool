### 154 {farbe=#494344}
# HOW TO MAKE A BUG (part 2)
*“Did I make six choices, or only five?”*
It's time to finish up this bugly design process! By this point you should have spent all of your Nectar on aspects,
augments, and skills (unless you wanted to keep some of it back for in-game use, you little hoarder). That means all
you have left to do is make a few more choices about your little adventurer, and then you're ready to play!
This chapter covers the last four steps of making your bug, where you choose your moods, your bits & bobs, your
starting mystery and, finally, your name. In fact, if we're going to go into a bit more detail...
## STEP FIVE: CHOOSE YOUR MOODS {farbe=#eba400}
Every bug has three moods, one of which is shared with all other bugs (the neutral 'ok' mood) and
two of which are chosen by you. Try to choose moods not only based on the mechanics they offer,
but on what you think would be fun to roleplay from time to time. You don't spend Nectar on these,
you just choose one positive and one negative mood.
*You'll find a list of moods to choose from on page 159.*
## STEP SIX: CHOOSE YOUR BITS & BOBS {farbe=#eba400}
Bits and bobs are useful stuff your bug has picked up on their travels, with bits being biological and
bobs being artificial. These are gained and lost easily through play, so don't worry about making big
dramatic choices here. This section also contains info on chunks, huge versions of bits and bobs that
are necessary building materials for a picotitan structure... But you don't start with any of those, and
you couldn't carry them even if you did! All bugs begin with 1 bit and 1 bob (although you could
take 2 bits and no bobs if you prefer, or vice versa).
*You'll find a list of bits and bobs to choose from on page 163.*
## STEP SEVEN: CHOOSE YOUR MYSTERY {farbe=#eba400}
Every bug is naturally inclined toward solving mysteries as they travel, weird questions that can be
answered to better understand the vast world of PICO. Like bits and bobs, these will change over
the course of an adventure, but all bugs begin play with a single mystery to solve to get them going.
Choose 1 mystery that interests your bug.
*You'll find a list of potential mysteries on page 167.*
## STEP EIGHT: NAME YOUR BUG! {farbe=#eba400}
Every bug has two names, one a personal choice and one a pseudoscientific descriptor. For your
personal choice name, choose whatever you like. Your scientific descriptor is taken from the
combination of aspects and augments you chose earlier.
*There's guidance on bug names, both pseudoscientific and common, on page 168.*

# MOODS
*"Life is a rollercoaster; you've just got to ride it."*
In PICO, mood is important. Every bug has three of them, one positive, one negative, and one that's sort of neutral.
Positive and negative moods are unique to each bug, but that middle mood is the same on every character sheet.
Knowing your mood helps you roleplay your bug, but it has some mechanical weight too - each positive and negative
mood has a benefit or drawback associated with it, giving you some extra power or making life just that little bit more
difficult.
## THE MOOD TRACK
Found on your character sheet, the mood track is represented by a simple set of five boxes. The mood track only
ever has one mark on it, an X representing a bug's current mood. This X moves to the right if a bug's mood is pulled
positive, to the left if it's pulled negative. Moods change over the course of a game session depending on both the
narrative and your bug's own chosen aspects - if it helps, think of it as a tug of war between the best and worst sides
of a bug's nature!
Bugs begin the game with their mood at neutral, which is always represented by the middle three boxes of the
track (the ones linked together and marked 'OK'). Their positive mood is added to the box on the far right, their
negative to the box on the far left. *We've got an example mood track below, in the black box - the more colourful*
*boxes above and below should help explain what each part of the mood track means, and how to use it...*
#### [GRUMPY]
#### [DETERMINED]
#### [OK]
## POSITIVE MOOD
When the mood marker is in the rightmost box of your
mood track, your bug is blessed with the effects of a
positive mood. Positive moods have nothing more to
them than a beneficial bonus, so there's no harm in
aiming for a good time while out adventuring! *This bug's*
*positive mood is Determined, which lets their player re-*

## NEGATIVE MOOD
When the mood marker is in the leftmost box of your

benefit and a drawback, so they have to be handled
carefully if you want to get the most out of them. *This*
*bug's negative mood is Grumpy, which makes friending*

## CHANGING MOODS
A bug's three moods might be chosen at character
creation, but it's during the game itself that the mood
marker starts moving and shaking!
When a bug's mood is affected by their actions or
environment, it pulls toward either the positive or the
negative. A pull moves the mood marker on a bug's
mood track by one box, toward either the positive or
negative end.
Remember, bugs don't receive the benefits (or
drawbacks) of their positive or negative mood until
the mark reaches the final box on either the left or
the right of the track.
So what can actually change a bug's mood? Quite a lot,
really, but not so much that it’ll happen *too*  many times
within a single session. Some bugs gain a positive or
negative pull based on their parts or gear (a bug with a
Leafy Hat is immune to negative pulls from weather, for
example), and spending a task on recovery often pulls
mood in some way, but most of the time mood pulls are
handled by the Weaver. *See the box on the right for*
*more details, but remember that you can always ask*
*for a mood pull after an important achievement too!*
## MOOD PULLS
The Weaver might pull a bug's mood when...
### + THE WEATHER CHANGES
Often pulls toward the positive if it’s bright, pleasantly
sunny, or beautiful, the negative if the sky lets loose
rains, storms, or extreme heat.
### + YOU MAKE A GRAND DISCOVERY
Usually pulls positive for the achievement, but if solving
the mystery involves a sudden flourishing of terrible
knowledge, then that might pull negative.
### + YOU WIN A RUMBLE
Pulls positive if you bested the danger you were facing,
negative if you only barely escaped (or if you had to do
something nasty in order to win).
### + YOU FIND AN IMPRESSIVE LOCATION
Pulls positive if it’s full of wonder and tasty treats,
negative if it’s a place of tiny horrors and bitterness.
### + YOU FINISH A TINY STORY
Pulls positive for a satisfying conclusion, and negative
for lost threads and questions that will never be
answered.
### WE'RE ALRIGHT IN THE END
The other big mood-changer is a simple but useful one:
using a task to sleep can always pull mood toward
neutral if a player wishes, as great victories fade and
big sadnesses pass. In the end, unless anything else
happens to change a bug’s mood, they’ll always be
alright.
### BIG MOOD
It's rare, but you may be able to achieve giga impact
when pulling your mood to positive or negative. In these
cases, a bug's mood marker moves all the way from
wherever it is to either the extreme negative or the
extreme positive mood, whichever is appropriate. You
might get a bit of emotional whiplash, but it's a pretty
powerful move if you can pull it off! *Depending on the*
*situation, the Weaver might count the 'neutral mood' in*
*the middle of the track as a break, stopping the mood*
*change dead centre instead.*

## CHOOSING YOUR MOODS
When creating your bug, you choose one positive mood and one negative mood. Both give benefits of some kind,
but negative moods also give game-changing drawbacks for you to contend with.
Think hard before choosing your positive and negative moods - the special benefits and drawbacks they come with
can be a source of great power in the right circumstances.
... Or don't, and just go for whichever ones you think you'll be comfortable roleplaying!
*Example: Sasha is creating a bug for a game of PICO, a praying-mantis-inspired adventurer that she wants to be*
*cool under pressure and impressively wise. When choosing her negative mood, she wants something that plays*
*off of her original idea - she goes for Detached, a mood that disallows friending skills, but makes it likely she'll find*
*something really impressive once a scene. For her positive pick the choice is an easy one - the Focused mood allows*
*her bug to ignore a good amount of cut when attacking, a perfect representation of that cool-headedness she*
*wants him to embody.*

# BITS & BOBS
*"Hey! He's stealin' our bits and bobs!"*
Bugs are, for their size, pretty damn strong. Not as strong as the wind, or a particularly large cat, but strong
nonetheless.
But what does the average bug do with all this strength? Some climb mountains, some throw bottlecaps, but ALL of
them take part in one of the most important bugly rituals - picking up random stuff and carrying it around in case,
y'know, it might be useful. At some point. Maybe.
This is where bits and bobs come in, the catch-all term for random things that bugs might find along the road and
want to keep with them.
## WHAT ARE BITS? WHAT ARE BOBS?
There is a difference! Bits are organic in nature, and bobs are artificial. Find a dewdrop? That's a bit. A paperclip?
That's going to be a bob right there. On the next page, you'll find a list that gives some examples of each, but
essentially, the golden rule is that if it's small enough to pick up and carry with you, it's either a bit or a bob.
### WHY IS THE DISTINCTION IMPORTANT?
Good question! Both bits and bobs can be used to craft things, trade, throw at others, and help out when you're
performing an action (usually by giving 1d6 and then being consumed). But there are also some actions that specify
whether they need a bit OR a bob - healing parts is one, which usually requires consuming a bit. Hopefully a tasty or
medicinally potent one, but you never know.
### USEFUL, BUT TEMPORARY
The most important thing to remember about both bits and bobs is that they're transient - they get picked up and
put down, lost or thrown away, consumed, used to make other stuff, or fed to wild beasts. They help you out in a
pinch, but *they don't define your character*  - that's for your body parts, gear, and buddies to do, the things that stay
on your character sheet from game start to game end.
### CONSUMING BITS AND BOBS
Usually using a bit or bob will consume it, removing it from your character sheet. But PICO is a game about stories,
and fiction matters - if you're using a bit or bob to add dice to an action you're taking, but that action would logically
not consume the bit or bob, keep it instead. The Weaver might use the loss of this otherwise safe bit or bob as a
negative outcome of the roll if you end up with a conflict or disaster, just so you're aware.
Oh, and some aspects will ask you to consume a bit or bob, with that specific wording - in these cases, it's best not to
argue with the book. It's probably a balance thing of some kind.
## CHUNKS
So you know about bits and bobs now, but what's this? An apple? A whole apple? How could you *possibly*  carry that?
Well, you couldn't. That makes that pretty simple, doesn't it?
Things that you might want to pick up, but that are way too big to be a bit or bob, are called chunks. Chunks are used
to build features of your picotitan's titanden (page 184), and picotitans each have the ability to carry at least one
chunk at a time (in their mouths or paws, most likely), but they're way too heavy for bugs.
That said, if you don't have a picotitan and still want to take a nice-looking chunk with you, either...
	» Drag a single chunk along with you as a group, tripling all of the ticks you'd make on the ticking clock, or...
	» Break it into pieces! One chunk might give anywhere from four to six bits or bobs (and the chunk is destroyed).

## CARRY SLOTS
Now, carrying around a matchstick and a couple of
cool-looking petals might not be much to you, but to
the average bug, it's a herculean feat. It's all about
relative size, you know? And not only that, but there's
limited places you can balance things on a shell -
pockets are an invention still relatively new to the
scuttling domains.
To represent this, each bug has a number of carry
slots, which represent just how many bits and bobs
they can carry around. For most bugs, this starts
at six, which is actually mightily impressive, but it's
possible to have more (or fewer) thanks to the choices
you make as you build your bug.
Carry slots are simplified on purpose - yes, a matchstick
*is*  going to weigh more than a petal, but we're not
getting into precise weight here. Instead, bits and bobs
all take up one slot.
When all of your carry slots are full, you can't
pick up any other bits and bobs. You
could still search for them, but someone
else would have to pick them up.
Some aspects *might*  give you a bonus
for having slots empty, and some
hazards might even do more damage or
have more dangerous effects if you don't
have any slots free, but that kind of stuff will
come up pretty rarely - your character sheet, or
the Weaver, will point it out to you.

## TAGS
Particularly special bits or bobs have tags, words that
are written down next to them that clarify something
about them. You might have *Sweet Grass* , or a *Shiny*
*Penny.*  Sweet Grass is going to be sweeter than most
grass, and that penny is going to be shinier than
average!
Tags are most often gained when you receive a bit or
bob through a roll with a twist - both the Gathering and
Scrounging tables (pages 52 and 72) specifically call out
adding tags as a reward for rolling doubles.
*Example: Mekamus is attempting to scrounge for*
*something salty in the ruins of an old cupboard. They*
*succeed, with a triumph and a twist - they find a Potato*
*Chip, and with the added tag from their doubles they*
*turn it into a Delicious Potato Chip by adding a tag.*
Any bit or bob can have a tag, and some might even
have more than one!
### POSITIVE AND NEGATIVE TAGS
When a bug gains a bit or bob that has an associated
tag, that tag might be specified as positive or negative.
Positive tags, on the whole, make a bit or bob more
valuable or useful - negative tags tend to make them a
little bit worse.
But the concept of positive and negative tags in PICO
is a flexible one, as what might be a positive tag for one
bug may well be negative for another. A bug that wants
a sweet bit to chow down on to activate one of their
aspects might *love*  a Mouldering Applecore, arguing
that the mouldiness enhances the sweet flavour. So, as
a general rule, if the tag would make the bug receiving
the bit or bob go 'yay', it's positive. If it would make the
bug go 'boo', it's negative.
### EXPLICIT vs. IMPLICIT TAGS
There may be times when an aspect you choose for your
bug, or even the Weaver themselves, asks you to use a
particular kind of bit - crunchy, sweet, spicy etc. There'll
be other times, like when you're scrounging (page 72),
that you might want to search for a bit or bob with
certain properties - something hot, something flat etc.
You find a bit, but, shock horror - there's no tag! What
now? Well, that's where implicit tags come in.
Most tags are explicit - they're a word, sitting right
there on your sheet next to the bit or bob they add
to. But bits and bobs also have implicit tags, tags
that you can kind of just *assume*  that something has.
Sugar, for example, is going to be sweet whether it
explicitly has the sweet tag or not. *Implicit tags can be*
*decided whenever they become relevant, usually by*
*conversation at the table with both the Weaver and the*
*other players.*
In edge cases, feel free to ask the Weaver and anybody
else at the table that might have a helpful (or unhelpful)
opinion. An apple could easily count as Crunchy, and
you could probably argue that paper would too if you
scrunched it up. Silver foil? Yeah, why not! A bone? If
you bite it hard enough!
*Example: Franz wants to light a small fire to keep his*
*friends warm as they wait for the rains to pass. He*
*doesn't have matches, or any other bit of bob that*
*might immediately make one think of fire, but he does*
*have a good supply of twigs from the surrounding area.*
*All he needs now is something to light them with...*
*Luckily, one of his allies offers up a bit from their own*
*pack - a Voltaic Mushroom! This doesn't have any*
*explicit tags that would create fire either, but with a*
*metal pin and some quick thinking the pair manage to*
*create enough of a spark to set the kindling alight.*

## MYSTERY TRIANGLES {farbe=#3b413f}
A mystery triangle is a shape representing an unsolved
question about the world. A shape that looks... Well, a
lot like a triangle. Like this, in fact!
Every mystery triangle has three points and a circle
at the centre. Those are the black lines, you can't miss
them! But what it also has are some lighter, grey parts
- these connect the points together, and sit at the
middle of the circle.
As a bug investigates a mystery, every small discovery
they make fills in one of the grey lines - connecting
the triangle's points together, piece by piece.
Once all of the points are connected (so after three
small discoveries have been made), a bug can choose
to make a grand discovery - learning a truth about
the world and filling in that circle in the middle.
Congratulations! Mystery solved!
## SMALL DISCOVERIES {farbe=#3b413f}
Small discoveries are made by a player noticing
something about the world around them that's
relevant to their mystery, and calling that out to the
group - then they can fill in one of those grey lines. But
what kind of mysteries are we talking about here?
Early on, it's best to start with some simple questions
about the world. Here are a few examples...
	» Why are froglins so mean?
	» What is the best berry?
	» How does a tree fit inside an acorn?
As players get more used to the mystery system, they
might tackle bigger topics. Where does the sun go at
night? Just what exactly *is*  the Shining Mountain?

# MYSTERIES
*“Any sufficiently advanced mystery is indistinguishable from stuff we just make up on the spot.”*
The world of PICO is an unusual one, and not just because it's full of cute bugs. The backdrop of the world, the
Mysteriocalypse, is something so large and so strange that no single bug can ever hope to understand it. Not
completely, anyway.
But bugs are curious sorts, full of wonder and questions, and *who cares*  about knowing everything about *one*  big
event when there are so many smaller mysteries to solve? Especially when those smaller mysteries can make your
life better on a day-to-day basis and, in very real terms, let you better understand the world.
This philosophy sits at the heart of PICO's final basic bundle of rules - how to deal with mysteries, and how to
develop the world of PICO by creating new truths.

## TRUTHS {farbe=#3b413f}
Grand discoveries literally change the world of PICO,
giving the players and Weaver a new truth to fold
into their story. This means less time worldbuilding
before a game begins and more creativity at the table!
But how does that look?
*Example: Swift has always been fascinated by the*
*local trees. He knows they come from acorns, but how*
*do they fit in there in the first place? After three small*
*discoveries, he's finally ready to announce his grand*
*discovery to the group - acorns have tiny micro-trees in*
*them, and they burst into full trees when they're given*
*enough water! The group agrees this makes sense, and*
*the Weaver notes this new truth down.*
From that point on, this is true for the world. And it
might be beneficial, might be detrimental, might be
dangerous, might even create new mysteries that need
answers! Can enough water make *other*  things get
bigger? Who knows? Keep playing to find out!
All truths are recorded on the Roaming Sheet,
which is shared by all players at the table. This should
be a good reminder that truths can be used by any
player, and any bug - not just the one that discovered
them. Once a truth is out in the world, it's fair game for
everyone - even NPCs and hazards can use them!
### NEW MYSTERIES {farbe=#3b413f}
When you've made a grand discovery and added a new
truth to the world, erase your small discoveries and the
lines they made and choose a new mystery. You've
changed the world with your mind, but there's always
more to learn and explore!
### SENSIBLE LIMITS
With all that said, there are a few mechanical and
roleplay-based rules around mysteries, discoveries, and
truths. We'll list them here for reference...
	» Making a discovery is an action - you need the
spotlight. Don't just shout out a small discovery
when you notice it, or a grand discovery when
you've decided what it is. Make sure these things
flow with the rest of the game.
	» Truths need general approval. You're making this
world together, after all - posing a possible grand
discovery and seeing the reactions of the Weaver,
and the other players, is often better than just
blurting one out. Only write down the truth that a
grand discovery creates when everybody is on board
with it, and don't be afraid to workshop if necessary!
	» Discoveries and truths can't invalidate another
player's choices, or character. This is the big one
- you can't make a small discovery, grand discovery,
or truth that directly affects another player's
bug without their full permission. For example,
discovering that toffee is actually poison is great
flavour for the world, but if you've got a sweet-
toothed bug in your party who loves snacking on
toffee, you'll probably want to run that by them first.
	» Discoveries and truths should fit the tone of the
story. It should go without saying, but we're going
to say it anyway - don't make hideous discoveries
if you're aiming for a nice, genial tone, or super-
detailed discoveries if you're having a laissez-faire
wander through the world.
## GRAND DISCOVERIES {farbe=#3b413f}
Small discoveries come naturally through play, as bugs experience situations that relate to the mystery they're trying
to solve, but grand discoveries are entirely player-created - when you've made three small discoveries, it's time
for the grand discovery!
You can make your grand discovery known the next time you have the spotlight by announcing that you have big
news to share.
So, this is the answer to the mystery, right?
Eh... Maybe.
The world of PICO isn't one of scientific rigour. A grand discovery certainly *feels*  like an answer, but it doesn't actually
have to make sense.
But whether it makes sense or not doesn't matter - it still changes the world!

### OTHER CHANGES {farbe=#3b413f}
While the truths a mystery can lead to are useful in
terms of what they can do for a bug in the moment, they
often have wider reaching implications. You're changing
the very nature of the world here - there are bound to
be some knock-on effects.
These might come in the form of...
Changes to Hazards: Using the example on the left, if
all cats in PICO are found to be ghosts, feline hazards
should also gain the ghostly keyword in addition to the
beastly keyword.
Changes to Environments: Perhaps the bugs have
found out that everyone, no matter how small, can
have a measure of control over the way the wind blows.
The Weaver might choose to incorporate that into the
environment by adding an element that plays off of
it - something along the lines of silk-gliding spiders, or
a local weather system controlled by moving a rusted
weathervane.
Changes to the Story: If you're really ambitious, a
grand discovery and the associated truth might change
the course of a story arc the bugs are involved in... Or
maybe even one they thought they'd completed. After
all, if you establish 'No One Ever Really Dies' as a truth of
the world, you might have a very angry conquered foe
coming after you once more...
## USING TRUTHS {farbe=#3b413f}
We've already given a rundown of how truths work on page 44, but we'll give a shortened version here as well for ease
of reference. So how do they affect things? Well, you have a choice here - a truth, once established, can be used to...
### GAIN A 1D6 ENVIRONMENTAL ADVANTAGE {farbe=#5a3582}
If a truth might aid your bug in undertaking an action, you can call on it for a 1d6 advantage (advantage is limited to
2d6 per roll, as shown on page 34). *This counts as your environmental advantage, so you can't double up for 2d6 by*
*using the environment and a relevant truth.*
### GAIN A FREE TWIST, UNDER YOUR CONTROL, ONCE PER SESSION {farbe=#5a3582}
Though twists are usually only accessed through rolling doubles, each player can call on a truth once per session to
add a twist to the story as it unfolds (without needing to rely on luck). This truth-based twist is also unique in that the
player that invokes it has control over the effects, rather than the Weaver asking the rest of the table for suggestions.
These special twists must still be approved by the Weaver, however. *You'll find more information on twists on page 37.*

# NAMES
*"A bug by any other name is a rose. A very crunchy rose."*
All bugs have two names. The first is a common name, and used among friends. The second is complex,
pseudoscientific, and multifaceted - the kind of name that might have been used by a pre-Mysteriocalypse
scientist when classifying a whole group of scuttling critters, or that a bugly mother might call out after *someone*
broke her favourite vase. We'll tackle them in reverse order below - you'll see why soon enough.
## PSEUDOSCIENTIFIC {farbe=#3b413f}
A bug's pseudoscientific name is taken from a
combination of their aspects and augments. Each part
and buddy aspect, and the augments that improve
them, have a name segment. String enough of these
together, and you've got a name!
*Example: Garret is purchasing aspects and augments*
*as he makes his bug. He goes for the Hardshell aspect*
*(which comes with the segment 'athro'), along with*
*the Hollow and Spiky augments (with name segments*
*'don' and 'kiri' respectively). Putting those segments*
*together, he gets the final result 'Athrodonkiri' - the*
*perfect pseudoscientific name for a spiky hollow hard-*
*shelled bug!*
### SEGMENT ORDER AND LONGER NAMES
Almost all bugs will have two or more part/buddy
aspects they can draw name segments from. We
recommend using the first two of these for your
pseudoscientific name, along with any augments that
add to them, but don't feel like you have to put names
together in this way!
The lack of actual scientists in the world of PICO means
that other bugs aren't going to question you over the
accuracy of your pseudoscientific name. If you want
to put a segment from an augment before the aspect
it adds to, we're not going to stop you. Want to ignore
some parts entirely, or add a flourish of your own? The
pen, pencil, or keyboard is in your hand!
*Example: Garret's not sure about his bug's*
*pseudoscientific name, so he plays with it a little by*
*putting the augment segments before the aspect ones.*
*That gives him either 'Donkiriathro' or 'Kiridonathro'*
*and, liking the sound of the second one, that's what he*
*goes with. He also adds a second part taken from his*
*Razor-Edged Pinpointennae, giving him a final name of*
*'Kiridonathro Zioxei' - suitably pseudoscientific!*
## COMMON {farbe=#3b413f}
Now you've worked out your pseudoscientific name,
your common name might be easier to choose - you
could grab a segment of that name and use it, name
yourself after an action you often perform or a feature
of your body, or just choose a name you think sounds
appropriately bugly!
*Example: Garret has his bug's pseudoscientific*
*name, but  it's a bit of a mouthful. For a common*
*name he decides to take his favourite segment of the*
*aforementioned, 'Kiri', and add a second name of his*
*own devising - 'Katroza'. After sounding it out a few*
*times, he decides it's perfect, and Kiri Katroza is born!*
### ANYTHING CAN BE A NAME
We don't know if you know this, but all names are
ultimately made up if you go back far enough. A party of
bugs might contain a Tekino Wildfire, a Goose Gomono, a
Castiel the Swift, a Josephina, and a Dave. Don't feel like
you have to hew close to any particular naming process
with common names, just go with whatever feels *right* .
*Example: Heru and his friends have gathered to*
*play a game of PICO, but first they need to make their*
*characters. With all Nectar spent and choices made,*
*they start to tackle the last step of the process - finding*
*perfect names. For a few of them this comes easily, as*
*Freya thinks 'Galahad' would be an excellent name for*
*an armoured bug, and Laura names every character*
*she makes after her own cat. Heru is the last to decide,*
*and after toying around with the components of his*
*pseudoscientific name for a few minutes makes the*
*decision to ignore them entirely and call his little bug*
*'Book'. Why? Because he's holding one in his hand, and*
*he wants to start rolling the dice!*

### 169 {farbe=#494344}
# ... AND YOU'RE DONE!
*"Everything in its right place."*
You've now purchased aspects, augments, and skills for your bug, fleshed them out with a few moods, chosen bits
and bobs, a starting mystery and, perhaps most importantly, given them a pseudoscientific and a common name. So
what else is there left to do?
Play the game, that's what!
### CHANGE (IN THE HOUSE OF FLIES)
Bugs develop over time, by earning Nectar and spending it on more aspects, augments, and skills, but they're also
changed by the very nature of their adventuring. If one of the more central parts of your bug starts to feel like it
doesn't apply anymore (such as a mood, which wouldn't normally change), chat with the Weaver about swapping it
out for free next time you moult.
The most important thing about your character, at the end of the day, is that you have fun playing them.
