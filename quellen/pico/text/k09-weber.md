## WEAVING WORLDS
The Weaver is PICO's GM figure, a role that encompasses
playing the world as a whole rather than a single
character. If you've been the GM of another tabletop
game (such as the Keeper of a Call of Cthulhu game,
or the Firefly in a game of the Wildsea) then you'll have
a good idea of the roles a GM fills. And if you're new to
this, don't worry - it's not as tough as it might seem, we
promise.
Here's what the Weaver does in a game of PICO...
### PRESENT THE WORLD
The Weaver is a window to the world for the
other players at the table, presenting the post-
Mysteriocalypse world as a place to explore and engage
with. When the band of bugs stumbles over something
new, you're likely going to be the one describing it.
### EMBODY NPCs AND HAZARDS
PICO might be a small world, but it's a busy one - most
locations are chock full of NPCs to chat with and
hazards to scrap with and, as the Weaver, you give voice
and action to all of those. Just how does a grumpy
butterfly sound? Well, that one's up to you.
### GUIDE AND CHALLENGE PLAYERS
What's a story without obstacles to overcome? As the
Weaver, you don't make the story from scratch - it's a
narrative exercise where everyone at the table takes
part. But it is your job to offer options, to guide when
needed, to enforce rules and make judgment calls, and
to throw the occasional stumbling block down when the
narrative suggests a challenge.
### RESPECT THE PLAYERS (AND YOURSELF)
You're all in this together - respect what the rest of the
players want, the atmosphere you're all aiming for, and
expect to be respected in return. Safety tools (described
on page 193) and a solid session zero to set some
expectations really help here.
### ENJOY YOURSELF!
PICO isn't an adversarial game, at least not in the
relationship between players and GM. Challenge and
risks are part of the experience, but ultimately everyone
at the table should be both contributing to the fun and
enjoying the process of playing a game.

# SESSION ZERO
So what's a session zero? In its most basic form it's a short, introductory session where everyone makes pre-game
choices (like the character they're going to play) and ensures they're all on the same page in terms of tone and
content. In a session zero of PICO, we recommend...
	» Choosing (and Discussing) Your Bugs: The pre-gens are there to be used, so grab whichever bug looks right for
you and give the sheet a read. Once you've worked out how your chosen bug is likely going to play, tell the Weaver,
and those around you! Think your bug is going to be a fast-talking brawler? That's good information for everyone
to have.
	» Setting the Tone: Have a quick discussion, Weaver and players, about what kind of tone you're aiming for. PICO
is inherently a pretty cute experience, but it's also a weird one - both of these dials can be turned up or down by
presenting the world, and the characters, in various ways (each realm in chapter 11 actually has a bit of advice and
guidance for playing the game and world in high, normal, and low weirdness presentations).
	» Checking the Basic Rules: What's a track? How are we rolling? What is this strange cube, and what do these little
numbers mean on each side? You'll learn through playing, sure, but a solid base helps things run smoothly. Try a
few example rolls once you have your character sheet while you're still in a no-stakes scenario.
	» Going Over Safety Tools: Even an established group can benefit from reinforcing what they love and hate, what
they want and don't. The opposite page's lower section can be easily copied or printed, and acts as a quick-
reference sheet for the Weaver to keep in sight as they guide players through a game of PICO. Combine that with
the X-card (or some cool arm-crossing gestures) for use when a situation gets away from the established tone,
and you can be reasonably sure you're going to have a tonally consistent time.

## SAFETY TOOLS
*"When you stop having fun, it stops being a game." - Ric Heise*
When you've been playing with the same table for a while, things like introductory sessions and safety tools tend to
go out of the metaphorical window. You all know each other, right? And of course you know the tone you're aiming
for, the way you all play, the things to avoid or edge around during a game.
Well, maybe so - but don't discount the tone-setting, comfort-inviting power of a good set of safety tools and a
strong session zero. And if you're a newer group of humans that don't know each other so well, or a one-shot table
playing at a convention, or barbecue, or awful child's birthday party, well... This page is *especially*  for you.
### THE X-CARD
Most often a card placed in the centre of a shared table with a large and prominent 'X' printed on it. If something
happens in a game of PICO that you're not comfortable with, reach out and tap that sucker - it's a sign for the Weaver
to stop play, establish what's happened that probably shouldn't have, and then rewind the scene a little and take a
different path. Better to be open and communicative than to stew with resentment or discomfort, especially in the
middle of a game!
If you don't have a physical x-card, more usual when you're playing a game online, cross your arms over your chest or
hold up crossed fingers in lieu of tapping. It should get the message across, and is usually more visible than typing 'X'
in a chat window (though that can work too, depending on your set-up).
### TONE EXPECTATIONS
There are three important questions that a Weaver should ask before a game starts...
	» How cute are we being?
	» How weird are we being?
	» How dangerous is the world?
PICO is a cute game by default (you're tiny bugs, after all), but an innate level of cuteness doesn't have to dial down
the weird or the dangerous if that's how your group chooses to play.
So, before you start, it's best to agree on a general level of cuteness, weirdness, and danger: low, medium, or high
for each. A low-cuteness game might have bugs engaged in bitter battles over shifting territory, a high-weirdness
game might delve into issues of spirituality or bugly philosophy, or even the origins of the Mysteriocalypse itself and
the issue of the missing human race.
### LINES & VEILS
A simple one, this - if there's a topic you don't want to appear in the game at all, that's a line - a hard line that no
player should cross. If you don't mind a topic being there, but don't want it to be focused on in gameplay, that's a veil.
The list below covers some common topics that players may have a problem with if they crop up during play.
	» Bullying
	» Childhood Abandonment
	» Classism
	» Dental Horror
	» Drug Abuse
	» Epidemics
	» Eye Injuries
	» Graphic Gore
	» Harm to Animals
	» Harm to Children
	» Homophobia, Transphobia
	» Mind Control
	» Parasitism
	» PC Betrayal
	» Racism
	» Rioting
	» Sexual Content
	» Substance Abuse
	» Slavery
	» Spiders
	» Starvation
	» Suicide
	» Torture

# PLANNING
*"The best laid schemes o' mice often involve cheese." - Not Ric Heise*
So, you're the Weaver. You've got a whole session ahead of you, a group of players at your table waiting to feast on
bugly delights (though not literally, unless you're running a very specific type of game). You look down at your notes
and find a few circles, a name scribbled into one of the margins that might start with a p or a q, and a doodle of a
flower. Just how screwed *are*  you?
Luckily, not screwed at all!
PICO is a low- to no-prep game by default, with the intention being that you can throw a session together in a few
minutes if you need to. As long as your players all have a pre-gen character in front of them and a set of dice, and as
long as you have this very document that you're reading right now, you should be absolutely fine. If you want to get all
fancy you can have everyone make characters, decide on what the local hazards might be, load up some finely-tuned
NPC voices... But don't feel like you *have*  to.

## ... BUT DON'T OVERTHINK IT
Probably the best advice we can give you here. Wild Words games like PICO tend to fight *against*  extensive planning,
mostly due to the partial democratisation of traditional GM responsibilities (thanks, twists). They have a habit of
making lovely, unexpected narrative pathways, but also of throwing a wrench in the works of an established, pre-
written story.
So... *Don't write one.*
You're there to play, at the end of the day, not to tell. Let the stories emerge through action and environment, and
enjoy the ride.
## CHOOSE A STORY LENGTH (PIC-NIC OR PIC-ODYSSEY) {farbe=#008bd2}
An average session of PICO lasts for 2-3 hours (though this can easily be modified to suit different groups), but how
many sessions do you need to tell your story?
There are two default 'modes' when it comes to playing in the world of the Mysteriocalypse. the pic-nic and the
pic-odyssey. Pic-nics are usually either 1, 2, or 3 sessions long, encompassing one-shots and very short campaigns.
Pic-odysseys are far longer (as you may assume from the name), and may sprawl over ten, twenty, or maybe even
fifty sessions teling a consecutive story.
Though they work the same way in terms of rules, each of these modes of play has their own special version of the
character sheet; a single-sided sheet for the pic-nic, and a double-sided sheet for the pic-odyssey (the latter of
which is designed to give players more space for aspects, bits, bobs, and notes as their bugs develop).
Make sure to give your players the right kind of sheet for the game you're intending to play before you begin!
## MAKE SENSIBLE PREPARATIONS
But if you want to feel as prepared as possible, or if you're not that used to narrative-based gaming, you can boost
your chances of a successful session by...
	» Reading This Document: Should probably go without saying, but we're going to say it anyway. Make sure you're
familiar with the world and rules of PICO before you begin. You don't need to know *everything* , but the less you
have to check the book during play, the faster a session will move.
	» Familiarising Yourself With the Player Characters: Every bug that gets made for PICO will excel in a different
way, have a different mechanical or narrative focus to them, and will naturally take centre stage in different
types of encounters and situations. Make sure you have at least a passing knowledge of what the players at your
table have built their bugs to be good at; that way you'll be able to throw challenges at them with a rough idea of
whether they'll breeze through or struggle, helping you keep the game running at a good pace.
	» Choosing a Particular Place or Hazard: If you're using one of the pre-made realms, read through it thoroughly so
you have a good idea of situations, NPCs, or challenges you can throw at players when the time is right. Each has a
different feel, so choose one you think the table will enjoy.
It also helps to choose one or two hazards, challenging entities to put in the path of a band of bugs as they explore
and adventure. A hazard that plays to the strengths and weaknesses of the band really lets PICO's mechanics shine.
So choose a place for the bugs to start in, and a hazard you know that's nearby - they might go somewhere else and
they might never meet the thing you planned for, but it gives you a good solid foundation for the start of a session!

## HANDLING NPCS
So, you want some memorable NPCs, do you?
The locations in this book each come with a set of
single-sentence prompts for NPCs that you can easily
slot into a scene, but they're just words - you need to
bring these things to life.
When embodying an NPC, think about the following...
How do they sound? Do you use a unique voice for
them, or do you describe what they say without playing
the character yourself (switching between these
methods can help highlight particularly important or
noteworthy NPCs, and keep them distinct from less
detailed, incidental individuals).
What do they look like? You've got an easy in here,
as you can assign even an incidental NPC a very quick
appearance by telling the players what kind of bug they
are. A firefly is immediately distinct from a centipede, or
a bat. But you can go further than that - do they have
any other defining characteristics? An object they're
holding or interacting with, a particularly snazzy hat?
# PLAYING
You have a few plates to spin if you've taken on the role
of Weaver. Luckily, like spinning plates in the real world,
getting a good bit of momentum up with each keeps
them in the air for a surprising amount of time. Feel free
to unpick that metaphor however you like.
The plates you'll be spinning are...
## DESCRIPTION
Of the world, and of the NPC bugs that inhabit it -
the post-Mysteriocalypse is what you make of it,
both in terms of content and presentation. Using an
established realm can help here, but you should also be
adding extra flourishes and details where you can. Try to
focus on elements that resonate with the entire group
as well as those that might matter to a particular bug.
*Example: Dunf, one of the pre-gen bugs, has a special*
*ability that comes into play when he's on solid ground.*
*While traversing the rushing waterways of King's River*
*that ability will be far harder to use, something you*
*might have to remind Dunf's player about, or spotlight*
*with an extra bit of description or a POI demonstrating*
*how the muddy riverbanks make motion more difficult.*
## STORY
As mentioned on the previous page, stories are
something that evolve naturally through play in a
narrative system - but that doesn't mean you can't
give them a bit of help to get going. Have an NPC bug
request aid, or confront the group with a problem, and
keep track (literally) of how they're doing at solving it.
*Example: An aged snail within the Fringewood pleads*
*for the band of bugs to help him - the man's daughter*
*has run away into the Hoggish Depths to try to find a*
*legendary truffle of her own. Do the group have to help?*
*No, but it's a great start to a small adventure.*
## CHALLENGE
This comes from hazards, choices, and the very world
the characters inhabit - it's not easy being a small
bug in a big world. Challenge the players with things
both within and outside of their skillsets, and try to let
everyone at the table have some moments to shine.
*Example: The bugs have been unchallenged for a little*
*while, so the Weaver throws a band of miniature mercs*
*their way - either escaping them, defeating them, or*
*befriending them all serve as besting a challenge.*
What do they do? Talk, usually, but a particular gesture
or even facial expression can really help make an NPC
memorable. Does the ancient spider twirl her fingers as
she talks? Does the many-scarred croaklin boss stroke
an imaginary moustache?
What are they for? It's tempting to introduce NPCs
for a singular purpose - to give a quest, to relay some
useful information, or to act as a one-off hazard to be
overcome. But the world of PICO is a living one, and
once you've established an NPC in it, you can (unless
they get squashed by something) bring them back
again and again if the story calls for it. Why introduce
a new villain if the greedy stoat from an earlier session
slunk off in defeat, and has been plotting bugly downfall
ever since? Or perhaps an NPC that was originally simply
there to pass on a bit of information turns out to be a hit
with the players - maybe the NPC can accompany them
for a little while, let slip some facts about their own life
or the world, maybe even set up future adventures or
turn into a trusted source of gossip?

## THE 'PERFECT' PICO SESSION
... Probably doesn't exist, at least not in a form that will be a proper fit for every table. Some groups want more action,
others exploration, and some just want a huge cast of weird NPCs they can crack jokes with for an evening.
But, that said, there is a solid structure you can hew closely to if you're new to PICO, or to Wild Words games in
general. If you want a session with a good balance to it that shows off a lot of what the game can offer, you should
aim to include...
	» A Scene Where the Bugs Can Explore: It lets players get accustomed to the world, find a comfortable game
pace, and interact with an environment that's not perfectly scaled for them (some players have trouble
remembering just how small bugs really are).
	» A Journey, or Dramatic Change of Tone / Scenery: The world of the post-Mysteriocalypse is huge, and not just
in terms of relative scale. A journey, or a transition from the wild outside to the interior of an old structure, gives
you a chance to reinforce that size. And if that's not on the cards, a transition from day to night can help instead!
	» A Time for the Whole Band to Chat and Relax: Adventuring is hard work - let the players use a montage to rest,
get a bit of in-character conversation going, and ponder on both what they've done and what's left to do.
	» A Tough Challenge, With Clear Consequences: Throwing a hazard at an adventuring band is almost guaranteed
to make sparks fly, but try to do it so there's more at stake than just the pride of winning or losing.
	» A Chance to Interact With a Memorable NPC: Polish up those vocal cords and get ready to do some kind of
amusing voice - nothing builds player engagement like an NPC or two that make the world feel alive.

# REWARDS & RAMIFICATIONS
*"We do things not because they are easy... But because we thought they would be." - Ray Chou*
One of the benefits of being the Weaver is that you get to dole out rewards from time to time - for good roleplay,
for a creative and well-put-together plan, for a brilliant roll in a dire circumstance. You get to do the same with
ramifications too, the fallout from bad calls and poor rolls. That power is in your human-sized hands!
But wait - this is important. Rewards and ramifications can be in the moment or lasting, narrative or mechanical.
Giving them out is one of the biggest jobs you have, as they help the other players feel that sense of change and
progress that all good stories should have. Make rewards cool, make ramifications memorable, and take this little bit
of power seriously.
Hoo, that got a bit heavy there, didn't it? But we're not going to throw you in at the deep end without a life raft of
advice to cling onto, so without further ado...
## REWARDS
Things are looking up! The dice fall on sixes and plans go off without a hitch. When you want to award good rolls, good
teamwork, good ideas, or good play, you might...
	» Increase Impact: It's pretty rare that a bug gets to use giga impact with any regularity, but it makes a great
reward for just the right idea, executed perfectly. Boost a bug's skills up to giga for a single action every now and
then, and let them make some real impact on the story (see what we did there?)*. Giga impact is an excellent*
*reward for when a band of bugs are working as a team to face a tough foe - consider setting a track leading*
*up to a moment of giga impact, like a kind of building combo meter, with a box marked every time one of them*
*successfully performs an action as part of an overall strategy or plan.*
	» Reduce Cut: A lot of the tougher hazards have ingrained cut as part of their statblocks, and bugs tend to want to
do things that are a little bit beyond them (a perfect time to add cut as a ramification). But other times, a brilliant
idea or a novel approach might make you think 'wait, that *would*  make that way easier'. In those situations, reduce
the amount of cut on a roll, or even allow a bug to ignore cut completely for a single action. It'll help the triumphs
start rolling in. *Don't do it too often, though - cut is the easiest way of adding and modulating difficulty in PICO.*
	» Give Secret Information: This might not work for every group, but some absolutely love a veil of secrecy between
characters. Maybe call one player over to whisper in their ear, pass them a note, or send them a private message
with some useful (or useless, but interesting) revelation. You could even tie it to their mystery if you want, or the
mystery of another bug at the table!
	» Give a Bit or Bob: ... or add a positive tag to an existing one. This is a classic reward, and works particularly well at
the end of a tough scene or encounter. Everyone loves loot at the end of the day!
	» Treat X as Y: Best given just before a roll when a bug is taking a long shot, or is going to cut significantly, but their
intended action really is a stellar one. Treating a conflict as a triumph is a cool boost for a single roll, and treating a
disaster as a conflict works as an excellent safety net too.
	» Reduce Immunity or Resistance: A great option when the bugs have dealt a serious blow to a hazard, removing
an existing resistance (or downgrading an immunity to a resistance) tends to make players feel like they've made
tangible process, and that the scales of an encounter just tipped in their favour.
	» A Change in Situation, for the Better: That NPC that they thought was down for the count? They still have
a shred of life in them! A change to the narrative that works for the players rather than against them is a really
good reward in situations where the odds are against them. Which, given that they're tiny bugs in a massive and
dangerous world, is likely going to be most situations!
	» Free Healing: Especially if a bug is looking rather banged-up, the removal of a mark (or maybe even a burn, if
you're feeling *really*  generous) can make their life a tad easier.

## RAMIFICATIONS
When things go wrong, they *really*  go wrong.
The Weaver has quite a few options when it comes to
handing out negative consequences, whether it's for a
bad decision, a wilfully dangerous course of action, or
simply an unlucky roll. You might...
	» Deal Damage: As a general rule, 1 or 2 marks is light
damage, 3 or 4 medium damage, and 5 or 6 heavy
damage. You could also deal giga damage, if you just
want to hit a bug hard and ignore their resistances.
*Remember to specify the type of damage you're*
*dealing by giving it an associated skill, like Sizzle*
*Damage or Dig Damage, and to match that type to*
*the situation.*
	» Impose Cut: Either in the moment
(for a less than stellar action
choice) or in the future (as
fallout from a conflict or
disaster). Specify the
type of cut (Tough Cut,
Armour Cut, Speed Cut, or
Camo Cut) and the amount
of results the bug should be
cutting (usually 1, rarely 2).
*Remember to add a condition*
*of when the cut is applied, or*
*how it might be avoided.*
	» Affect Bits or Bobs:
Specifically by adding negative
tags to them, like *Mouldy*  or *Rusted* . You might
even completely destroy a bit or bob, usually as a
consequence of an unlucky roll.
	» Treat Y as X: Either conflicts as disasters, or
triumphs as conflicts. Like imposing cut, this might
be for an impending roll or as an ongoing condition in
a certain situation.
	» Give an Immunity or Resistance: Usually this works
well for a hazard, but it could be added to a more
general track instead.
	» A Change in Situation, for the Worse: Not
everything has to be mechanical! Sometimes a
fictional or narrative-based change for the worse can
be a decent enough ramification in itself, especially if
it has consequences for the wider story of the game.
