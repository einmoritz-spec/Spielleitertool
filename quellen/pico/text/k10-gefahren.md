### 201 {farbe=#494344}
## WHAT IS A HAZARD?
 A hazard is anything that might be a potential source
of danger to the gang as they travel, something that'll
take some quick thinking, teamwork, and exertion to
overcome. Most are pretty obvious, like a hungry frog or
an angry mouse, but some do get a bit... *esoteric* .
All hazards have a statblock of useful information and
potential moves for the Weaver to draw from when
they're added to a scene. These are, in order...
	» Name: Letting you know what to call the thing.
	» Keywords: Special tags that some aspects might
interact with, or that give the Weaver some info.
	» Hazard Track: Just like the kinds of tracks you'd
find on a bug's aspects, a series of hearts with some
break points thrown in there for good measure. Can
easily be shortened or lengthened to suit the kind of
encounter that a Weaver wants to run.
	» Overview: A brief description of the hazard.
	» Signs: What the bugs might see or notice as the
hazard approaches, or is in the general area - used to
clue players in that a challenge is coming.
	» Behaviour: A quick list of things a hazard might do
once introduced, to help set up an encounter.
	» Defences: Any resistances, weaknesses, or
immunities a hazard has to particular skills.
	» Breaks: Short examples of what might change when
a hazard's track breaks.
	» Stuff to Grab: Bits and bobs that might be gained
from fighting, outwitting, stealing from, or otherwise
defeating a hazard.
	» Parts & Moves: The bits that make a hazard special,
containing the damage it can deal and any cool
unique stuff it can do.
### CUTE OR SCARY?
PICO, intentionally, straddles the line between cute and
scary in various aspects of world design, and one of the
most important things to keep in mind when running a
hazard is your group's stated lines and veils, and their
expectations of tone.
You can play PICO with little to no violence by avoiding
rumbling skills, treating encounters as hijinks that
little bugs get into. You could also play with cut-away
violence, where a mouse *might*  get attacked with a
scissor-sword, but it'll just end up with an oversized
bandage on after a brief cutaway and a cartoon sound
effect. Or you can play with fists flying and teeth bared;
if it works for your table, it works for the game.

### 202 {farbe=#494344}
## HAZARD OVERVIEW
Hazards are under the Weaver's control, just like non-threatening NPCs - a hazard might even start out in a scene as
a normal NPC, only becoming an active danger when someone steps on their toe, steals their lunch, or accidentally
insults their mother.
These next few pages go into detail on how to run scene with a hazard: introducing them, establishing their
intentions, using their moves to dish out damage and effects, and handling their tracks and breaks when they take
damage themselves. There's also advice later in this section on how to handle titans, the largest and most fearsome
of post-Mysteriocalypse foes, and some suggestions on how to help newer players that might not be used to
cinematic challenges or flexible, non-violent ways of handling encounters.
So, let's get started!
## INTRODUCTIONS ARE IMPORTANT
A hazard's overview, signs, and behaviour (found in the hazard entries later in this chapter) should all give you a good
idea of how you can introduce a potential foe into a scene, but there are a few additional ways you can ratchet up the
drama and tension as danger threatens...
	» Make the Hazard Track Open: Draw it out where the players can see. This works particularly well for groups that
like to know how tough what they're facing is, or how close they are to victory.
	» Show NPCs Reacting: Do they flee, take cover, wail, or call for help? Maybe some NPC bugs will even stand with
the gang against a hazard, helping out from time to time. It makes the world feel more alive!
	» Start with a Bang: Use one of the hazard's moves to start an encounter off, forcing an immediate reaction from
one or all of the players as the threat crashes in.
	» Give a Glimpse of Rewards: An old video game trick, this one - draw the
players' attention to something shiny tucked into a hazard's pocket or
shell, or a particularly impressive weapon that the creature might
drop if beaten or forced to retreat. Or, a real classic, have the hazard
swallow something that the bugs desperately need, only to spit it
out when defeated.
	» Bring in the Environment: Is there a conspicuous chandelier
above that might be dropped on a marauder's bonce? A cliff-
edge the hazard is dangerously close to? Making sure the
environment has some points of interest or additional
threats encourages tactical play, and often gets
players excited to try out their cool strategies - and
maybe get some gigadamage as a reward!
	» Imply Immunities and Resistances: Simple,
but effective in informing players of what
might or might not work against a particular
hazard. Maybe a heavily armoured bug
enters the scene only to be immediately
crushed (perhaps by some kind of
chandelier, who knows?), but then
heaves it off themselves and stands
up unharmed. Bash might not be so
effective here...

## INTENTIONS MATTER
It's good to have an idea of a hazard's intentions when you introduce one to a scene, and to think of some way you
might communicate those intentions to the players (even if not immediately, nothing wrong with a bit of mystery). In
other words, avoid just plonking every hazard down and expecting a fight.
*Example: An adventuring band of bugs is passing through a derelict trainyard when they hear scuffling from a heap*
*of trash up ahead. A few moments later, with a rattling hiss and a scraping of limbs, a predatory shape pulls itself*
*free - a binwolf!*
Binwolves are lupine trash-constructs, hunters by nature, so this scene could very well turn violent - but that doesn't
mean it has to. Here are a few examples of what might happen, based on how the Weaver communicates a hazard's
situation and intentions.
*Example: The binwolf charges forward, pin-like limbs*
*pumping like pistons. It'll be on you in moments - what*
*do you do?*
This is a direct call for action, and some obvious
aggression on the part of the binwolf - a scuffle is likely!
*Example: The binwolf watches you warily, but doesn't*
*approach. Just behind it you catch sight of a few*
*binwolf pups, each no larger than a grub. And, judging*
*by their yelps, they're pretty hungry...*
What do binwolves eat, apart from bugs? Electricity,
probably. This might be the start of a scene where
players try to 'charge' the cubs up to earn the
binwolf's temporary trust.
*Example: The binwolf scrapes a screw-shaped paw in*
*the dirt, regarding your band with challenging eyes for*
*a moment before turning away and readying itself. In*
*the distance, a set of immense signal posts are shifting*
*colour, the glare reflected on the binwolf's shining hide.*
*First red, then orange, and the beast tenses up. Green is*
*moments away - is it expecting some sort of race?*
This kind of hazard entrance clearly sets a non-violent
option for the hazard's 'defeat', perfect for adventuring
bands that prefer to solve problems without violence.
*Example: The binwolf yelps, once, then collapses into*
*its component pieces. They roll down the trash pile,*
*stopping at your feet.*
An example of ambiguity - this might be a ruse to get
bugs to drop their guard or a creature in genuine need
of help. Maybe this encounter has bugs healing marks
on a hazard's Heart Track, rather than making them?
Each one of the examples above shows the Weaver signposting a different type of encounter, suggesting a way
that the players might want to engage with this particular threat. And this isn't just true of binwolves, either - every
hazard contained in this chapter can be presented in a variety of ways to suit the tone of the table, the lines and
veils in play, and the flow of the narrative.
### ... AND INTENTIONS CAN CHANGE
Something else to keep in mind. Just as a player can have their character change tactic, attitudes, or approach
at any point, a hazard can change its intentions and behaviour midway through a scene. That last example of the
binwolf up there? Maybe the bugs partially reassemble the Clanky threat, and it rewards their efforts by immediately
turning on them.
Switch things up, keep players guessing, and don't be afraid to explore various avenues of challenge when you can.

## MAKING MOVES
As soon as a hazard is an active threat in a scene, it can
start taking the spotlight to make moves. But what's
a move? Here's an example, from the munchmeleon's
'Parts & Moves' section on page 249.
Sticky Tongue (Bash 1-2): Normally curled up within
the munchmeleon's maw, this gloopy danger can shoot
out with incredible accuracy. Reactions take a Speed
Cut of 1, and the tongue can *push*  or *grab*  a bug as well
as dealing damage.
	» The first bit of bolded text, (Sticky Tongue), lets you
know the part of the hazard that's being used, or the
particular name of the move. *The munchmeleon uses*
*its tongue to make this move.*
	» The second bit of bolded text, (Bash 1-2), references
the skill that the move used to do damage, and how
much damage it does. *The munchmeleon is hitting*
*with one or two marks of Bash Damage, depending*
*on what the Weaver chooses.*
	» The rest of the text gives both a fictive overview
and a rundown of any special rules that the move
follows (such as inherent effects other than damage,
for example, or whether it imposes cut on a bug's
reaction to it). *The munchmeleon's sticky tongue is*
*good at grabbing and pushing (page 64), and it's fast*
*as a whip, imposing Speed Cut.*
### DAMAGE GRANULARITY
When bugs take an action, they're usually dealing either
one mark (for most actions), or gigadamage (enough
marks to fill the hazard's track up to the next break,
often unlocked by filling a Combo Track or targeting a
hazard's weaknesses).
But hazards work differently - they can usually deal
anywhere between 1 and 6 marks of damage to a
bug's aspect track with a single move.
It's still always tied to a particular skill and, just like
with bugs, 'damage' doesn't always need to be violent
(Swagger Damage from a hazard might intimidate
a bug, Dig Damage might trap their little feet in the
ground so they have to shake themselves loose).
This asymmetrical design is to give the Weaver a few
more levers to pull in terms of a hazard's power level.
### WHEN TO MAKE MOVES
With even the most tense scenes in PICO running off
cinematic spotlight-flow rather than a strict initiative
system, as the Weaver it's your choice of when a hazard
makes a move. As a good rule of thumb, hazards
should act about half as much as the players do - so
for every two player actions, force a reaction by making
a hazard take a move of its own.
If there's more than one hazard in a scene, increase this
(or maybe have all hazards act in a row). If a hazard is
bigger and slower, it might only act once every player
has taken an action, but the move it makes might
involve multiple players rolling to react.
Also remember that a hazard making a move can force
a reaction from a player that's been out of the spotlight,
bringing them back into the narrative. Hazard moves
are just as much a Weaver tool as rewards or NPCs.
### EXTRA EFFECTS
Most moves can have an extra effect of some kind,
usually more narrative than mechanical as suits the
moment. But some hazard moves have specific extra
effects, like changes to the way an Action Roll is read. If
this is the case, these extra effects will be noted in the
'Parts & Moves' section of a hazard's statblock.
It's probably a good idea to brush up on the general
effects you can throw on a move as well - take a look
at the Rewards and Ramifications section on page 198,
and the Effects section on page 64.
### GOING BEYOND THE STATBLOCK
... Is something we heavily recommend, especially once
you're more familiar with running the game.
When you're comfortable with it, think of the moves in
a hazard's statblock as examples to be added on top of,
with numbers and damage types changing based on
the flow of a scene. Maybe a hazard uses a nearby POI
to deal an unexpected sort of damage, or perhaps one
of the bugbarians the band are facing is *really*  into cap-
golf, and can throw out long-ranged attacks with ease.

### MOVES & REACTIONS
One of the most important things to remember when running a hazard is that you don't need to be rolling dice -
hazard moves always hit, by default, unless the target manages to shield, dodge, or otherwise evade in some way.
How do they do this? With a reaction! When a hazard grabs the spotlight and makes a move, ask the target of that
move for a reaction. They can then put a Defend Roll together using the rules on page 59 to see how the exchange
plays out. *As the Weaver, you don't need to roll a reaction when a hazard is hit by a player character, just like you*
*don't roll when using a move to attack.*
If a bug can't react to a move (due to being stunned or asleep, for example), the character treats it as if they had
rolled a disaster.
### AUTOMATIC DAMAGE, AND OTHER ODDITIES
Every now and then you'll find a move that *doesn't*  allow reactions, that deals automatic damage or has a guaranteed
effect. These are rare, not just because they're powerful, but because they take the reins away from players in terms
of agency. Use them sparingly, and if they're having a disproportionate effect on an encounter, kick things down a
notch to remove them (you'll find more about adjusting a hazard's difficulty on the fly on page 208).
*Example: The constant thumping music of the drummingbirds deals automatic damage to any character that takes*
*the spotlight. This is great thematically, but if an encounter goes on for longer than expected, it may wear both*
*characters and players down over time. In situations like these, either use a track break to halt the effect, or let the*
*bugs do something to ignore the effect from that point on, like trading an immunity to it for a lost resource, or an*
*instance of burn.*

## HAZARD TRACKS
Every hazard in this chapter has a suggested track in
its entry, a measure of how much damage it can take
before it's defeated. Here's an example, a track of nine
boxes with a break after every three...
Hazard tracks don't tell you how *tough*  a creature is;
that's mostly down to their parts and moves. Instead,
think of hazards as an indicator of *dramatic weight*  - the
longer the track (and the more breaks it has), the more
complex the fight is expected to be.
You can always make an encounter shorter and
punchier by reducing the track length, or more
important and potentially time-consuming by... Well,
lengthening it. They are just examples, after all - I'm not
going to tell you what to do!
### WHAT MARKS A HAZARD'S TRACK?
Let's bullet-point this one, for ease of reference!
	» Any action that negatively affects a hazard can
mark their track, dealing damage.
	» Damage is always tied to the skill used to roll an
action. By default, an action with normal impact will
deal one mark of damage to a hazard's track.
	» If the hazard is resistant to the skill being used, drop
the action's impact by one step.
	» If the hazard is weak to the skill being used, increase
the action's impact by one step.
	» If the hazard is immune to the skill being used, no
marks are ever made.
	» If an action deals gigadamage, it fills boxes until it
hits a track break.
### WHAT CAN MARK A HAZARD'S TRACK?
The more offensive rumbling skills (Bash, Bite, Slash, and Sizzle) are the obvious go-to for when a bug wants to
damage a hazard's track, but they're far from the only option. We've talked about it on page 147, in the Skills section,
but we'll say it here too - damage doesn't have to be physical, and any skill can mark boxes on a hazard's track if
used in a narratively appropriate way. If it negatively affects a hazard, or brings the encounter closer to some kind
of end, it can deal damage.
*Example: A band of bugs are facing off against a crew of miniature mercs, who are demanding a bob-based toll for*
*passing through a valley. Some bugs are using Yap, Scare, and Swagger to damage the hazard track, attempting to*
*talk them round or intimidate them. Another is using Slink and Climb to jockey for position on the nearby cliffside, as*
*one of the mercs tries to scramble after them and pull them down. These skills aren't doing physical damage to the*
*mercs, but they're certainly getting close to giving up and letting the band pass just to end the annoyance.*
### DAMAGE AND TRACK BREAKS
Hazard tracks have breaks, just like a bug's own aspect tracks. They work in pretty much the same way too, giving the
hazard some survivability when hit with a large amount of damage in one go.
*Example: Heironymoth is using Chart to confuse a swarm of cephalocopters, sending them careening into each*
*other. With the benefit of an aspect she treats it as Gigachart Damage, but the action only deals two marks of Chart*
*Damage before hitting the first break on the cephalocopter track.*
But that's not all they do! Once we've dealt with titan tracks (on the next page), we'll delve a little deeper.

## ... AND TITAN TRACKS
Titans still have tracks to mark, like every other hazard,
but they work a little differently - and look a little
different too!
These are TITAN HEARTS, easily identified by the swirl
in the middle. The track above has only two titan hearts,
so it should be easy enough to fill!
... Eh, well, not exactly.
Each titan heart can only be marked by gigadamage,
and each instance of gigadamage can only mark a
single titan heart.
### GIGADAMAGE VS TITANS
A pretty simple one, this. If a bug (or the environment)
hits a titan with an action dealing giga impact, a single
mark is made on the titan's track. Filling a Combo
Track (page 46) is a reliable source of gigadamage,
extremely useful for titan encounters, but some
aspects allow a bug to deal gigadamage too.
*Example: Klay releases a spurt of acid from their*
*scorpionish tail, aiming some Gigasizzle Damage at the*
*titan they're throwing down with. Klay's player rolls a*
*triumph, so the attack hits home - the Weaver marks a*
*single titan heart.*

### EXPLOITING TITAN WEAKNESSES
Just like normal-sized hazards, titans also come with
one or two weaknesses to specific skills (and sometimes
to other things, like environmental conditions).
Successfully exploiting a titan's weaknesses is
another reliable way of gaining gigadamage, enough
impact to mark a titan heart.
*Example: March, a dung beetle chef, is taking on the*
*titan Brimstocchio (a living clay puppet that just wants*
*to play with fire). While his cooking skills aren't going to*
*do any damage here, his Dig skill can - Brimstocchio*
*has a weakness to Dig, and March exploits it by wiggling*
*inside the puppet's cracked pottery skin and making*
*a general nuisance of himself. March's player rolls a*
*conflict, so March takes some Sizzle Damage as he*
*does so (it's hot in there!), but the beneficial part of the*
*conflict is still enough for the Weaver to mark one of the*
*titan's hearts.*
### BUILT-IN TRACK BREAKS
It might look, at first, like titans have more simple tracks
than most hazards thanks to the lack of visible track
breaks. Well, let me disabuse you of that notion right
now - whenever a titan heart is marked, that counts
as a track break.
Why does this matter? Because when a hazard's track
breaks, whether it's a titan or not, the Weaver has a
chance to kick things up or down a notch.

### 208 {farbe=#494344}
## HITTING THE BREAKS
The breaks on a hazard's track (and the ones built into every titan heart) do more than just provide a defence against
gigadamage - when bugs hit them, that's a signal to the Weaver that it's time for something to change. When a
hazard's track breaks...
	» The Weaver can kick things *up a notch*  or*down a notch* , to represent the hazard being damaged,
desperate, or determined.
	» The hazard immediately *steals the spotlight*  for a move of its own, showing off the change as they do so.
These on-the-fly changes are a rules-approved way of making a hazard or encounter easier or harder, lessening
the frustration players may feel if their foe is unexpectedly strong or time-wastingly weak. As the Weaver, the actual
mechanical and narrative outcome of this change is completely in your hands; we have a few suggestions below, but
there's nothing stopping you from creating your own difficulty changes in the moment, perhaps bringing in elements
of a grand discovery that the bugs have made, or involving an NPC or previous plotline they've been a part of.
### KICKING THINGS UP A NOTCH
Things feeling too easy? Want the bugs to face more of
a challenge, more of a threat? Let's kick things up a
notch! As the Weaver, you might...
	» Power up one of the hazard's existing moves with
more damage or an additional effect.
	» Unlock an entirely new move for the hazard to use for
the rest of the encounter.
	» Add a new immunity or resistance.
	» Impose a situational cut on certain types of actions
that the bugs have been relying on.
	» Have the hazard redouble its efforts, making two
moves at a time for the rest of the encounter.
	» If the foe is a boss, bring in a lackey to support them.
	» If the foe is a lackey, start a track to summon a boss.
### KICKING THINGS DOWN A NOTCH
If the encounter feels like a slog, or the bugs are
struggling, it's probably better to kick things down a
notch. As the Weaver, you might...
	» Disable one of the hazard's moves or parts.
	» Remove an immunity or resistance, or add an entirely
new weakness.
	» Remove a source of cut that the bugs are struggling
to work with.
	» Have the hazard change up its tactics or attitude,
maybe beginning to calm or retreat.
	» Make an extra mark on the hazard's track each time it
makes a move, hurrying the encounter to a close.
	» Remove one of the hazard's future track breaks,
making gigadamage more effective.
### STEALING THE SPOTLIGHT FOR A MOVE
As the Weaver, you don't have to immediately explain the mechanical change that kicking things up or down a notch
makes (though you can, if your group enjoys transparency). But what you *do*  have to do is give the hazard a dramatic
moment to show off this change in a narrative fashion, by stealing the spotlight and having the hazard make an
appropriate move.
Each hazard entry gives a few thematic suggestions on what a break might bring, to help the Weaver decide. The
*Froglins* entry, for example, suggests things like 'Hop to New Positions' and 'a Tangle of Long Legs'. That first one
might be kicking things up a notch, with the froglins finding more advantageous positions. The second is likely
kicking things down a notch, and might be represented by removing a move that relies on the power of the froglins'
well-muscled legs, or removing the usual Speed Cut bugs take when targeting them with rumbling skills.
### END OF THE LINE
When all boxes on a hazard's track are marked, the encounter is over and the bugs have won. What does defeat look
like? That's up to the narrative. What are the rewards? Well, we have a whole 'Stuff to Grab' section in each hazard
entry to answer that!

## HAZARD KEYWORDS {farbe=#f3a477}
All hazard entries have a set of keywords nestled in between the hazard's name and the Heart Track. Let's take
bugbarians as an easy example - they have the keywords Small/Bugly/Lackey, with each of those words telling you
something about the hazard's presence, appearance, or behaviour. Some are a little more complex - a quetzalcroakl,
for example, is a Large or Huge/Feathery/Slimy/Elite.
But what do these mean? Oh, if only there were some kind of handy breakdown of what each keyword implies...
### SIZE KEYWORDS {farbe=#f3a477}
A simple descriptor of the hazard's size. Narratively, this helps the Weaver give players an idea of how large, or small,
the thing they're facing is (in relation to both their own bugs and the surrounding world). Mechanically, size only really
matters for a few special aspect rules and effects (like grabs, on page 64). The sizes presented are the same as those
on page 12, with...
	» NANO foes being about the size of a sugar granule,
	» SMALL foes being about the size of a raspberry,
	» LARGE foes being almost the size of an aluminium can,
	» HUGE foes being about the size of a generously portioned takeaway noodle container, and...
	» TITAN foes being as big as, or bigger than, a housecat.
### TYPE KEYWORDS {farbe=#f3a477}
These put hazards into particular categories based on their physical aspects, splitting things like bugs, beasts, and
plants into different groups. Many aspects give special bonuses against hazards with a particular type keyword.
The types you'll find within the rest of this chapter are...
	» BUGLY, for antagonists with insectish properties,
	» BEASTLY, for warm-blooded mammalian types,
	» SCALY, for anything resembling a fish, snake, or reptile,
	» SLIMY, for slippery customers like frogs and eels,
	» FEATHERY, for those with... well, feathers (whether they can fly or not),
	» LEAFY, for fruit, vegetables, and bug-eating plants,
	» SHROOMY, for the fungal and the slow-spreading,
	» CLANKY, for things crafted artificially, often made of plastic or metal, and...
	» GHOSTLY, for those that crossed beyond the veil, didn't like it much, and came back for a good old haunt.
### THREAT KEYWORDS {farbe=#f3a477}
The final set of keywords relates to how this hazard presents itself and how their Heart Tracks work (as well as giving
a few clues as to how they should probably act). For threat keywords, we have...
	» SWARM, to represent multiple creatures working as one. Swarms usually have a single long Heart Track with a
generous amount of breaks, and rarely retreat from a rumble before being beaten.
	» LACKEY, used for weaker threats that band together, but lack any real sort of leadership. An encounter with
lackeys should have multiple separate Heart Tracks, all on the shorter side, and without a boss present, lackeys will
often flee if a band of bugs proves too tough for them.
	» ELITE, for individual hazards that can take on an entire band toe-to-toe. Elites may appear alone, in pairs, or even
in trios, and each elite should have their own Heart Track. When an elite gets low on hearts, it may retreat, bargain,
or dramatically change up its tactics.
	» BOSS, a keyword reserved for the biggest and baddest hazards around. Bosses have a single, long Heart Track
with a lot of breaks, and can be accompanied by lackeys or elites for extra muscle. Bosses very rarely stick around
if their hearts get low, preferring to retreat and then strike back at a later time with additional forces at their
command.

### OVERVIEW
Most mice, even the ones that don't get along with
bugs too well, are pretty cute. Not so the beastmice,
barbarian rodents with grizzled fur and gnashing teeth
and altogether too much in the way of arms and armour.
Why are they like this? You can probably blame
carbonated beverages before bedtime for that one, and
some of them drink so much of the stuff that they get
*really*  weird.
### SIGNS
	» Footprints, deep with weight despite their small size.
	» Glistening smears, a strong scent of cloves, and the
smell of congealed soda and old fur on the wind.
	» Other bugs fleeing, shouting something along the
lines of 'noooooo, beastmiiiiiiice!'.
### BEHAVIOUR
	» Rampage around and knock stuff over.
	» Shout poorly constructed insults at foes that look too
powerful to actually fight.
	» Get drunk on off-brand soda.
	» Leave sticky marks everywhere they go.
	» Go wild for soda, even the smallest drops.
### PARTS & MOVES {farbe=#3b413f}
Bottleglass Weaponry (Bash 3 / Slash 3): Simple and
to the point, often literally.
Sticky Paws (Rummage 2): Bugs suffer a Tough Cut of
1 whenever they try to break free of a mouse's grab.
Overpowering Scent (Sense 1-2): Clove soda never
really caught on pre-M, but it's in high demand now. Any
bug with a Feelers aspect takes 1 mark of automatic
Sense Damage whenever a beastmouse draws near.
Bottlecap Shielding (Shield 2): Can be used for a quick
and dirty shield bash, but is more useful for defence.
Bugs targeting a beastmouse with a physical attack
suffer an Armour Cut of 1.
Grizzled Fur (-): Have you ever tried to clean soda out
of *your*  fur? You can't really hold this one against them.
Any bits or bobs used to add dice to an attack targeting
a beastmouse are lost, ending up stuck to their fur.
Faster Than You'd Think (-): Even while weighed
down and unpleasantly adhesive, a beastmouse can
pull off an impressively swift scamper. Whenever one
beastmouse takes damage, another can steal the
spotlight to reposition for free.
Clove Cannon (Sizzle 5): Now *this*  is war. A rare
high-pressure cannon that can only be fired once per
encounter. Can hit and push multiple nearby targets.
### DEFENCES
	» Resistant to Bash, Tame, and Yap, from a combination of good defences and poor manners.
	» Weak to Sizzle - singed fur smells terrible.
### BREAKS
	» Scamper to the high ground! / Screech, point, and bite / Loss of shield / Knock a tooth free
### STUFF TO GRAB
	» Bits: Scrappy Fur, Tensile Whisker, Clove Soda Droplet
	» Bobs: Thin Metal Shield, Big Clove Club, Colourful Wrappings, Bottleglass Axe

### UNDERMICE
After consuming a truly *unhealthy*  amount of clove soda, a beastmouse might find their mammalian minds crowding
with dark, sticky thoughts that are impossible to escape. This is the Curse of the Clove, an affliction that changes
a beastmouse first mentally, and then physically. The resulting creatures (known as 'undermice') abandon their
barbarian ways, instead turning to a kind of syrup-based wizardry that few can comprehend.
Confusing Anatomy (-): It's still just a mouse... right? Attacks targeting an undermouse suffer a Complex Cut of 2
instead of the usual beastmouse's Armour Cut of 1.
Bleak Prognostication (Think 3): A jab of a spindly paw and a few words about dark futures are all it takes to strike
fear into the heart of even the bravest bug.
Chittering Hex (Scare X): A string of curses and pronouncements, sometimes shocking, sometimes insightful.
Deals Scare Damage equal to double the number of small discoveries a bug has for their current mystery.
Crashdown Aura (-): Why suffer a sugar crash when you can force others to endure it instead? When an
undermouse joins an encounter, all bugs pull their mood to the negative.

### OVERVIEW
We've waxed lyrical about cats at various points
throughout this book, and it's not without good reason;
to a bug, a cat is the pinnacle of titandom. It's not just
about their size (for there are many titans that dwarf
them), or even their skills - at the end of the day, what
makes a cat so impressive to insect kind is the way it
moves through the world as if everything it surveys is its
domain, and always has been. That kind of unabashed
confidence is hard won, and cats have it in abundance.
It also, unsurprisingly, makes them extremely dangerous
foes when you get on their bad side. Earning the ire
of a cat will likely gift you an enemy for life, a furred
interloper that will turn up to harass you at unexpected
junctures (or, somehow, at the precise moments when
the addition of a feline force into the mix would be most
inconvenient).
That's not to say they can't be countered or outwitted,
and no broken bond is beyond repair for a bug... But it
is to say that one should tread carefully unless they're
very, *very*  confident.
*Titans follow special rules when it comes to damage,*
*and besting one is an almost impossible challenge for*
*most bands of bugs without planning and forethought.*
### SIGNS
	» Glowing eyes in a distant shadow, eyes that follow
you as you move.
	» Fur and dust on just about every surface, especially if
that surface is fabric.
	» The stench of ammonia, a scent of marked territory.
### BEHAVIOUR
	» Preen, lounge, and hunt for sport.
	» Reveal the mysteries of the universe (laconically).
	» Engage in a cruel play and unexpected chases with
smaller creatures.
### TITAN DEFENCES
	» Immune to all except gigadamage and
weaknesses stated below - should you really be
doing this?
	» Weak to Swim. Cats may act all high and mighty,
but trick them into a body of water and they quickly
lose both regal posture and a good measure of self-
confidence.
	» Weak to Scare and Dazzle. It might not be the
kindest approach, but you can definitely shave a few
lives off of a cat's absurd counter by giving them a
mighty surprise.
	» Immune to Slink. You're trying to out-stealth a cat?
Good luck, dude.
	» Immune to Tame - even when a cat seems like it's
been completely disarmed and befriended, there's
still the heart of a hunter beating somewhere deep
within.
	» Immune to permanent defeat of any kind, though
it can be beaten back, outwitted, or encouraged to
slink off and think about what it's done. Those 99
lives aren't just for show.
### BREAKS
	» Arch back and hiss / A reactive swipe / Tail
thwacking intensifies / Hunker down and get
ready to leap / Puff up like a dandelion / Curse in a
language of spits and hisses

## HELPING PLAYERS OUT
It's a trope as old as TRPGs - part of the GM's job is to teach the game as they run it.
Well, Weavers are no different - PICO may be a pretty collaborative experience as tabletop games go, but you're still
likely the one with a copy of the book and the ability to push your glasses up your nose and start a sentence with the
words 'Umm, actually...'.
And we're here to help you out with that, with this short section dedicated to general advice for Weavers and a few
tips on dealing with common player hang-ups and mistakes, especially when danger enters into the mix.
### IF A HAZARD FEELS TOO TOUGH...
Maybe it is! That can happen, and it shouldn't be seen as a roadblock either; just a problem to be solved. You could...
	» Kick things down a notch when the bugs reach the hazard's next track break.
	» Have NPC aid arrive to help in an encounter, or have the weather/environment begin to work in the band's favour.
	» Have the hazard retreat, and set a track for its return so the players have some time to prepare.
### IF A HAZARD FEELS TOO WEAK...
Hey, that can happen too - there are definitely some aspect and augment combinations that can give a bug a real
edge in certain situations. You could...
	» Kick things up a notch when the bugs reach the hazard's next track break.
	» Have a hazard's lackeys show up to support their boss, or have an entirely new (and more dangerous) hazard
appear, eat the old one, and become the new main threat.
	» Have an element of the environment or weather turn against the bugs mid-scene.
	» Have the hazard retreat in fear or frustration, vowing revenge, then return scenes later after beefing itself up.
### IF A PLAYER SEEMS UNSURE...
Then that's pretty natural, actually. Not everyone's going to have a great idea for every situation. To help, you could...
	» Have the hazard target them with a move, giving them an immediate, concrete situation to react to.
	» Ask if they have an idea or approach they want to share. Sometimes they might just need a little encouragement!
	» Engineer a situation that you know mechanically benefits them, and gently spur them to take advantage of it.
### IF AN ENCOUNTER RUNS TOO LONG...
It's surprising how often this one gets overlooked. You should...
	» End it! It's not a game if people aren't having fun. Have the hazard break away, slip unexpectedly down a hole, or
remember that it left the oven on. It can return later (still damaged), or it might never be seen again.
### IF A PLAYER FEELS USELESS...
We guarantee that they're not.
This most often happens with players that have a lot of experience in crunchier games, or games that have less
flexible skill and action systems. They'll likely worry that they don't have the 'right skill' to do something useful, or that
there's 'nothing they can do' in the face of a hazard's cut or resistances/immunities. To counter, you might...
	» Teach as you play. Ask them what skills they have access to, and give a few pointers or hints as to what they might
do with those same skills (especially non-rumbling skills that could still have a cool effect).
	» Remind them that there are lots of things they can do in an encounter that aren't attacking, but that can still mark
a hazard track. Scare a hazard? That's a mark. Throw spices in its face? That's a mark. Climb up it and cover its
eyes? Hey, that could be a mark. Distract it with something really shiny? You know, that's probably a mark too.
	» Remind them that death and defeat are *very*  different things. It helps to look for survival or gain, not conquest.
	» Remind them that they can ask the rest of the table for ideas too - tap every resource you can!

## GENERAL ADVICE
Given their power and toughness, a titan might at first seem like an insurmountable challenge to a group. If needed,
remind them that...
### ALL ACTIONS ARE VALUABLE
"I can't hit this thing's weaknesses, and I don't have any gigaskills, am I useless now?" Definitely not!
Actions without giga impact can still be *extremely*  *useful*  in a titan encounter. They might not have the ability to make
marks on a titan's track, but they can still have effects! Here's an example...
*Jules and her band are facing off against the specimachine, a Clanky titan that's trying to snatch them up to add to*
*its personal collection of scurrying sorts. Jules has the spotlight, and she uses an action to throw a series of bursting*
*berries at the titan. It has boosted impact, but that's still not enough to deal gigadamage, so no marks are made*
*on the specimachine's titan hearts, but there's still a narrative effect from the action. Jules might have the berries*
*smear over the machine's lenses, impeding its vision, or have juice from the berries gum up some meshing cogs to*
*slow it down a little.*
### THERE's STRENGTH IN TEAMWORK
It's spelled out on page 46, but the players might need a reminder. Combos and finishers allow all bugs access to
giga impact with a bit of teamwork and some good rolls, so even if a group lacks the specific skills to target a titan's
natural weaknesses, they can still muscle through if they need to. It might even be worth setting a Combo Track at
the start of every titan encounter, just as a visual reminder that combos and finishers are particularly useful in these
situations (see page 47 for more details on Combo Tracks).
### IT DOESN'T HAVE TO BE A FIGHT
Yes, we admit, it's pretty cool to stand toe to toe with a beast a hundred times your size and take it down with a rusty
nail and some cool acrobatics. But have you, maybe, considered making it a nice cup of tea instead?
Just like regular hazard encounters, tangling with a titan as you attempt to mark its track might take wildly different
forms depending on how your group solves problems. *Each titan page has a few suggestions on how bugs might*
*engage with the foe in question, usually ranging from full-frontal assaults to entirely non-violent approaches, which*
*are there to help Weavers set up these encounters effectively and hint at possible solutions.*
### THE 'BIG BAD' RETURNS!
As the Weaver, throwing a titan at the players isn't something you should do lightly. Consider...
	» Having a titan teased early on, so the bugs can start planning their strategies in advance and maybe recruit some
outside help.
	» Have other events in the world or story affect the titan, whether positively or negatively, even when it's 'off-screen'.
	» Have the titan as a reoccuring challenge, where damage the bugs manage to deal stays on the titan track
between scenes after it retreats. It's not a buffet, you don't have to eat it all in one go! *On reflection, I may not*
*understand how buffets work.*
