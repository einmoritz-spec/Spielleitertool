AUSTRO-HUNGA RI A N EMPIRE
Czechs, Slovaks, Lemkos, and some Ukrainians and
Poles lived in the Austrian and later Austro-­Hungarian
Empire. The Austrians also pursued a policy that
was intended to be “assimilationist,” but in practice,
amounted to adopting their culture. Unlike the two
empires mentioned above, the Slavs had much more
freedom under the Austrian yoke. They could publish
in their national languages, subject only to light censorship. German, however, was the official language,
and all were obliged to obey, including paying taxes and
performing military service. The northeastern areas
of the Empire were provinces with very little investment, resulting in relatively high poverty. There was
freedom of religion under Austrian rule, mainly due to
the great religious diversity and the resulting conflicts.

OTTOM A N EMPIRE
Some of the Slavs, mainly those living in the Balkans, found themselves in the territory of the Ottoman Empire, where they were repeatedly persecuted
as infidels or Islamized. Their contact with their
neighbors to the north was severely restricted, and
the intense tensions between the many nations concentrated in a small area resulted in the so-­called
“Balkan Cauldron.”

SPRING OF NATIONS
One of the key moments in the 19th century was
the year 1848, which marked the beginning of the
“Spring of Nations.” This revolution was quite violent
among the Slavs, with numerous anti-­feudal movements, lynchings of the nobility and landowners, and
national uprisings of differing scopes. It ended the era
of duty to the feudal lords, known since the Middle
Ages, but it also created a large group of unemployed
and unskilled people who fed into the urban poor.
The 19th century was also the heyday of Romanticism, a trend in art and culture that had a substantial
impact on national consciousness – among Czechs,
Bozena Nemcova was the mother of the “national
revival”, among Ukrainians, Taras Shevchenko
was very popular, and among Poles, Adam Mickiewicz and Juliusz Słowacki were equally appreciated.
Romanticism resulted in numerous ethnographic
studies and an interest in strengthening the autonomy of nations and their right to self-­determination.
This idea was further developed in later years, but it
was in the 19th century that research was conducted
which made it possible to reconstruct the national
language in the case of Czech, to systematize it in
the case of Polish, or to modernize it in the case of
Ukrainian or Belarusian. Less popular languages,
such as Lemko and the extinct Old Slavonic, were
also studied. An auxiliary interlanguage was worked
on but did not come into being then; work was not
completed until far later.
