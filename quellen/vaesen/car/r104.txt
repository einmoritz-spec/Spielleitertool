BACKGROUND
Brygidki, the oldest prison in Lemberg (Lviv), has a
long and complicated history.
The first building erected at the location was a
monastery of the Bridgettine order, founded in 1614
and intended for girls from noble families. In 1782,
after the Partition of Poland, the Austrian administration decided to secularize the convent. The
monastery, which at the time housed 30 nuns, was
closed, and the Brygidki building was later turned
into a prison.
One of the nuns who lived here, Olha Kobzar,
joined the monastery against her will as a young girl.
Her parents, influential nobles, chose a husband for
her whom she refused to marry. They renounced the
girl and sent her to the convent with a hefty donation.
Despite this, she eventually found comfort in her
new life and became very attached to the monastery.
When the monastery was closed, a tragedy happened – Olha was accidentally killed by an Austrian
officer, Josef Strasser, the person responsible for
the operation. While they were having an argument
in the bell tower about moving the monastery, he
accidentally pushed her too hard, and Olha fell to
her death. The officer left the crime scene in a panic,
deeply regretting his actions, but he did not tell anyone about what happened.
Olha was branded as a suicide victim (everyone
thought that she had willingly jumped from the tower)
and thus buried separately from her sisters. Her spirit
did not find peace – as a renounced child who died
violently, she returned as a vykhrovyk (page 69).
She stayed in the prison, endlessly wandering its
halls. During this time, her spirit was dormant, and
the prisoners felt her presence only as a gust of wind
in unexpected places.
Three decades years later, the situation changed
when Josef Strasser returned to Brygidki to accept
the position of warden. Olha slowly awoke from her
semiconscious slumber. The vykhrovyk felt that the
unpunished murderer was there, and began restlessly searching for him among the prisoners. When
the vaesen passed through the prisoners, her touch

infected them with chickenpox. The unnatural disease started spreading, and then two of the prisoners
who caught the disease were found brutally killed in
their cells by unknown forces.
Josef fears that his past is about to catch up with
him. He is no stranger to the occult, as during his
time in the military, he had a close friend who was
a Thursday’s Child. He pored over the monastery’s
books, looking for something that might protect him
against the powers of the vykhrovyk, and fashioned
himself a new elder wood stick. Meanwhile, t vykhrovyk
realized that the murderer was hidden from her sight.
She returned to kill the previously infected prisoners
and collected their souls, leaving messages on the walls
written in blood. She aims to either force her murderer to come out of hiding or collect enough souls
to eventually break through his protection.
Josef tried to handle the situation himself but
understood that he was out of his depth. When three
more people became infected, he decided that it was
time to seek the Society’s aid.

CONFLICTS
The primary conflict of this mystery is between the
vykhrovyk Olha and the prison’s warden, Josef Strasser.
He regrets killing the nun and tries to atone for his
sins by caring for prisoners. Olha is full of raw emotion and thinks that the only sufficient punishment
for Josef’s crime would be his death.
The secondary conflict is between Josef Strasser and
the other prison guards (including Eugene Shvets,
see page 103). The warden believes that even the
worst of men can change, and he has deep empathy
for prisoners. He thinks that he can alter the prisoners’ ways of life with effort and compassion. The
guards are of the opinion that murderers who are
locked there deserve worse punishment, and thus
they mistreat them. The chaplain Father Hryhorii
(page 109) believes in God’s judgment and only
treats people who have faith and who confess as being
worthy of salvation.
