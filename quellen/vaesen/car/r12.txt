BOHEMIA
This section focuses on the situation in Bohemia,
which in the 19th century does not exist as a state,
only as a nation. In this and the following chapters,
the words “Czech” and “Bohemia” are used interchangeably. Without getting into too much detail,
these two words mean the same. Both relate to one
of the historical Czech territories, but the first is in
the Czech language, and the other is in Latin. Feel
free to use them both in your game.

CZECHS REBORN
In 1809, Josef Dobrovský published a textbook on
Czech grammar, the first publication in almost 200
years written to save the Czech language. Dobrovský’s
work was very well received by educated Czechs, who
started a wave of national movements to try and regain
statehood. This movement was called the “Czech
National Revival” (České národní obrození) and was crucial
for building national consciousness and Czech patriotism. A discovery of the manuscripts of Dvůr Králové
and Zelena Hora, allegedly medieval works written in
Old Bohemian, was a sensation. Although skepticism
about the originality of the works “discovered” in 1817
emerged rather quickly, they were not recognized as
undisputed forgeries until the 1880s. The poems
were soon translated into German and English, which
became extremely important for the awareness of
Czech culture among the European aristocracy. The
consequence of the above was the flourishing of literature in the Czech language and interest in Czech
culture. The Czech language was used by the elites
again, which significantly strengthened the national
liberation aspirations of the Czechs.

Politically, the situation evolved somewhat and was
only seemingly stable – after all, the Czechs of the 19th
century were under Austrian rule. The year 1805 saw
the Austrian defeat at Austerlitz in Moravia, where
Napoleon’s army virtually smashed Emperor Francis’
army and his allies, the Russian military. The following
decade of political instability ended with the Congress
of Vienna in 1815. It re-­established Habsburg rule in
the territories through which Napoleon’s army had
swept like a hurricane, including the Czech lands.
During the reign of Francis I, permission was
granted for the construction of the Czech National
Museum in Prague (1818), albeit without any state
funding and in a location that was hardly representative at the time. After Francis died in 1835, his son
Ferdinand took over. The ruler’s ineptitude gave
Bohemia a great decade, contributing to a massive
development of culture, literature, and art. Efforts
for a national theater began but did not come to
fruition until four decades later (it opened in 1881).

THE SPRING OF NATIONS
Another landmark date was 1848 – the peak of the
Spring of Nations. Nationalist and national liberation riots of various significance had been occurring
for several years at that point. Ferdinand was forced
to abdicate in favor of the 18-year-­old Franz Joseph.
The new Emperor soon embroiled the country in
two losing wars, resulting in an economic crisis and
further national liberation movements. Thanks to its
strong political party, geographical remoteness from
Vienna, and thus difficulties in logistics and administration, the Hungarian side gained autonomy, and
1867 brought the “Austro-­Hungarian Compromise.”
The Emperor remained the formal head of both states,
but Hungary gained much more freedom – both in
terms of self-­determination and local administration.
All major offices and the army, however, remained
under the rule of Franz Joseph until he died in 1916.
