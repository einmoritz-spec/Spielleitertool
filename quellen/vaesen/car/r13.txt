FOLKLORE AND
LOCAL TRADITIONS
The vast scope of the terrain under the rule of
the three empires is reflected in the variety of
mythical creatures and other local folklore. People, mostly Slavs, try to cultivate their traditions
despite the laws of any oppressing rulers. In the
wild and remote areas, they live a simple life, trying to accommodate the unnatural into their day-­
to-­day lives. It is not uncommon to meet the elder
women of knowledge, whom some call witches.
They are respected by common folk who ask them
for remedies and help with getting rid of particularly dangerous or annoying creatures. Myths are
incorporated into daily routines. For example,
people might leave some food behind to feed local
spirits or gnomes, leave herbs on their doorframes,

·9·

or carve strange symbols into their barns to protect
livestock. Myths are very alive here, and anyone
messing with local customs might find themselves
dealing with trouble from both the weird creatures
and the people.
Cities are another story. There, it is all too common for people to consider myths and even some
traditions nonsense. They live in vast, multinational
cities where traditions and religions are mixed into
an amalgam. Old traditions are chosen carefully
to carve the foundations of national movements,
and anything that doesn’t fit is thrown aside. In
the Austro-­Hungarian Empire, traditions from
all over the region are mixed. It is not uncommon
to hear Jewish legends mixed with stories of creatures from Transylvania. Finding truth in these
intertwined stories is a challenge for the Society
members. Library and technology access might be
insufficient when knowledge of some obscure tradition or folklore is needed.
Some mythical creatures are easier to encounter
in different regions of the three Empires. In the East,
myths of Baba Yaga are common, while in Romania,
one might find dhampirs almost on every corner.
Further south, even some creatures from the Muslim
world might be found. In Silesia, some Nachzehrer
might roam, and the legend of the artificial man made
of clay might spread in the Jewish districts. Wherever
Society members go, they might find something different and new.

RENEW ED INTEREST
The rising popularity of Romanticism resulted in
some unexpected changes for local vaesen and other
mythic creatures. As poets and writers began to study
folklore, bringing old and often forgotten traditions
into the light, some creatures woke up from long
slumber. That led to conflict with locals, especially
in the rural areas. For poets from great cities, it was
just folklore, maybe a scary and dangerous adventure, but life became unbearable for people living
with angry creatures.
