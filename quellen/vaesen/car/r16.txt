THE CLERGY

Former settlers – German-­speaking Christians,
mainly Protestants, resettled here in the mid-17th
century. Most of them were peasants or small craftsmen. In the 19th century, their descendants are several
generations removed from those settlers, but many
retain a strong old national identity, forming groups
such as “Sudeten Germans.” They are distrustful of
strangers, especially of the non-­Germanized Czechs,
Moravians, and Slovaks.

As with most places in Europe, the clergy are a constant presence. Some scholars say the Reformation in
Bohemia began a century before Luther, with the Hussite Wars. However, due to the Counter-­Reformation,
social changes (including the separation of state and
church), and the multiculturalism of the country,
the position of the clergy weakened from decade to
decade, more so the further west a parish was. Despite
this, several factions of Protestants as well as Roman
Catholics, Jews, and in the east of the country even
some Muslims can be found. The end of the century
also saw a fascination with “ancestral beliefs,” i.e.,
those of the Slavs.

Czech-­speaking Czechs are a large group constituting
the majority of the lowest social stratum. The policy
of Germanization meant that the Czech language
survived mainly in the provincial areas, among the
peasantry and the inhabitants of small towns. In the
19th century, this situation slowly begins to change,
and increasingly, the Czech language appears “in the
salons” and on the streets – as villagers emigrate to the

Many Jews in the 19th century were highly regarded
artisans, artists, or merchants. Yet Jews were often
banned from certain professions across Europe
and therefore lived on the sidelines – often in their
semi-­enclosed neighborhoods. In some cities, they
were forbidden to settle or, in extreme cases, even
stay within the city limits. Usually, these were regulations dating back to the end of the Middle Ages,
and as the 19th century progressed, these restrictions were gradually abolished.
