MILITA RY
There was compulsory conscription into the Austrian
and later Austro-­Hungarian army, which included
more than half of all adult males (it could be avoided
for health or family reasons, university education, or
with the use of bribes). Service lasted three years and
was followed by transfer to the reserves for at least
seven years. That led to the erection of many garrisons with permanent officer posts and whole garrison
towns geared to serve soldiers. Thanks to the army
and orders from the military, a whole host of innkeepers, shoemakers, tailors, hairdressers, doctors,
photographers, but also prostitutes, petty thieves,
card sharks, and usurers made a living.

IMMIGR A NTS
In the 19th century, the process of industrialization
was progressing, which also strongly affected Bohemia – both in terms of heavy industry and mining, and
the textile industry. Engineers were often brought in
from distant places to run factories – usually within
the Empire, but there were also occasional contracts
with the English or French.

THE STATE
OF INDUSTRI A LIZATION
Life in the Czech lands was much like life in similar
areas of Europe. Due to the mountainous terrain,
there were very few large farms, but in the east this
was often compensated for by the high quality of the
land. In Moravia, grapevines were also grown from
which high-­quality sweet wine was produced. As the
standard of living increased, life expectancy grew, and
child mortality fell significantly. That could have led
to the area’s agriculture becoming inefficient. The
demographic catastrophe was avoided by investment
in industry – numerous steelworks were established
in the Bohemian area, giving rise to heavy industry
and mechanical engineering.
Bicycles and, towards the end of the century,
motorbikes and the first automobiles were produced

in Bohemia. Precision machining was perfected,
which eventually led to a robust arms industry. In
the 19th century, there were many ingenious gunsmiths in the area who took care of the weapons for
the officers and hunters, and quite a few imperial
gunsmiths working on military weapons.
From the mid-19th century onwards, light industry
– mainly textile – was significantly developed, with many
women also finding employment there. The Austro-­
Hungarian manufactories and factories quickly gained
recognition in the region and later throughout Europe.
It was not without reason that Brno was compared to
Manchester, which, at the time, was probably the most
modern industrial city in the world.
Living conditions were, therefore, not bad – the
closer the area was to Vienna, the better. The situation was much worse in the underdeveloped regions
of Slovakia or Galicia (south-­eastern Poland and
north-­western Ukraine), where mass starvation was
not unheard of. That led to emigration, sometimes
within the Empire and sometimes outside its borders
or even to another continent. Many Galicians, for
example, emigrated to the United States at the end
of the 19th century.
The Austrian emperors strongly insisted on the
mono-­national character of the state, forcibly Germanizing the Slavs. After the Spring of Nations
and the subsequent transformation of the Empire
into Austria-­Hungary, this pressure eased considerably, and the idea of a “multinational” state with
a dominant position for Austrians (and collaborating Hungarians) was recognized. Publishing in
other languages and limited education in them were
already possible.
Still, to get a state job or a permit for an activity regulated by law (for example, the practice of medicine),
Germanization was still welcome or even compulsory.
All national liberation movements were distrusted,
and disobedience to the crown was a serious offense.
Despite this, the situation of the Slavs under Austrian
rule did not look terrible – the Prussians (and later
the Germans) and the Russian Tsar were much more
oppressive towards their minorities.
