HISTORY OF PRAGUE
The first Slavs arrived in Prague in the early 6th century, displacing Celtic settlers. In the 8th century, the
Přemyslid dynasty took over. It was started by the legendary Prince Přemysl “Orač” (Přemysl the Ploughman) and his wife Libuše, whose dream visions were
said to have contributed directly to the establishment
of a castle on that particular spot. In the 9th century
the Church of St. Clement and later the Church of the
Blessed Virgin Mary was founded, built on the right
bank of the Vltava River on a hill called Žiži, a pagan
site of worship and a place of tribal assembly.
Right next to the church, rises the Prague Castle
– according to some, the largest castle in the world in
terms of surface area. On the other side of the river
was a castle that had already been the seat of the legendary Přemyslids – both of these fortresses gave rise
to Prague. The name was said to have originated simply from the word “threshold” (prag). Settlements on
both sides of the Vltava River developed in parallel.

Unification was only formalized in 1784, but even
before that, the individual towns already existed in
close symbiosis, each providing services specific to
that particular town or having an associated atypical
social structure. The 18th-­century “Royal” Prague
formally consisted of four towns, but Josefov was
undoubtedly another one, even though it wasn’t officially incorporated until 1850.

STA RÉ M Ě STO

The authorities of the Old Town (and later Prague)
hold offices in the Town Hall located in the main
square. This medieval building was reconstructed
several times. The most distinctive feature of the
Town Hall is the astronomical clock – the Prague
Clock (Pražský orloj). It was designed in 1410 by the
mathematics and astronomy professor Jan Sindl

The Church of Our Lady before Týn (Chrám Matky
Boží před Týnem), one of Prague’s largest churches, was
rebuilt several times and after the 1679 fire, almost
entirely raised from the rubble.
The State Theater (Královské Stavovské Divadlo) was
built just before the unification of the towns (the
construction was completed in 1783) and funded by
Count František Antonín Nostic-­Rieneck. It is one
of the most important cultural institutions in 19th-­
century Prague. The theater was rebuilt several times
and is well-­funded, so it hosts many foreign artists
(especially at the turn of the 18th and 19th centuries).
The Gothic St Agnes Convent was built in the
13th century, dissolved in 1782, and never returned
to its sacred function. In the 19th century, the building served several purposes – it housed the poor, had
some rooms rented out to craftsmen, and the church
itself was used as a warehouse.
