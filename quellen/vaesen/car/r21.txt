The Smallest House in Prague: A noteworthy curiosity is a house at 4 Anežská Street, commonly known
as “the smallest house in Prague.” The building, erected
in 1853, is only 2.25 meters wide and originally consisted of two connecting rooms without access to any
amenities. Nine years later, a third room was added to
the rear, and over time, the first room was converted
into a corridor/vestibule. From the street, the house
looks like no more than a gate to an alley. It shows how
difficult living conditions could be for the less-­than-­
wealthy inhabitants of the city in the 19th century.

Due to the less permanent nature of the buildings
in Nové Město, there are far fewer old structures there
– houses and workshops were treated as very utilitarian,
and when they no longer met the requirements, they
were simply demolished and replaced with new ones.
Armies approaching the city in subsequent wars also
repeatedly destroyed this area. That, combined with
planned reconstructions, meant that in the 19th century,
Nové Město, already a district of Prague, was built anew.

The “U Fleku” Brewery was founded in 1499 but
acquired its current name in 1762 when it was purchased by Jakub Flekovský. It is widely known as the
oldest beer hall in the city and the oldest operating
brewery. It brews dark yet not strong beer, which it
serves with local cuisine.
The National Theater had been under construction since 1868, was completed in 1881, and then
caught fire during the finishing work, resulting in
a two-­year renovation. The building is one of the
most important structures for Czech culture and the
drive for statehood revival. One of the largest and
most modern buildings in 19th-­century Prague, it is
a place to be for well-­respected citizens.
The National Museum: For most of the 19th century, the National Museum did not have its own premises. The impressive new building, completed in 1891,
visibly towers over Wenceslas Square. The National
Museum is a gathering place for Czech patriots and educators who teach about the Slavic past of the Czech lands.

Over time, the housing situation improved somewhat – the proportion of brick buildings increased,
but the city managed to preserve the district’s unique
feature: main streets that were astonishingly wide for
their time and turned into market squares on market
days. One of the most famous was Wenceslas Square –
the horse market. In addition, there were three other
squares in Nové Město – the cattle market, the hay
market, and the general-­purpose market. Thanks
to large distances between the frontages, fires were
much less likely to spread.
