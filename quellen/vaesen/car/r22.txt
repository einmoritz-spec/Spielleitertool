Žofín Palace was founded between 1836 and 1837
on an artificial island in the Vltava River. It was built in
the Neo-­Renaissance style and was an important meeting place for Prague’s elite, particularly the Slavs. The

JOSEFOV
Josefov is the smallest of the towns that make up Prague.
It lies in a bend of the Vltava River and is surrounded
by the Old Town, which makes it an enclave. Since
the Middle Ages, it served as a Jewish town (ghetto).
Because of this, despite its proximity to the Old Town,
it was completely excluded from its privileges. Due to
its small area (less than 9 hectares), the buildings here
were extremely cramped – many workshops, shops,
and even flats had windows, and the buildings were
expanded with all kinds of uncontrolled extensions,
mezzanine floors, and superstructures. The buildings were thus inconsistent and unstable – sometimes
minor building disasters happened. Every meter of
land was highly coveted and valuable.
For most of the city’s history, Jews were only
allowed to settle in Josefov (and were still expelled
from there sometimes). A special permit had to be
obtained to settle outside the ghetto. This generally
cost a great deal, required friends among influential
people, and could still be revoked at any time. This
situation remained virtually unchanged until the 19th
century – people were occasionally allowed to settle
outside the ghetto, permits were revoked, pogroms
took place, and fires broke out, which, due to the
dense housing and many wooden elements, spread
rapidly through the ghetto.
There were synagogues within the town walls –
including the Old Synagogue from the 13th century,
the High Synagogue and the Maisel Synagogue – as
well as small cemeteries. Josefov had its own town hall,
which was the seat of the council. It too was relatively

small, and efforts were made to combine it with neighboring buildings, but as Josefov was only annexed to
Prague in the mid-19th century, it served its purpose for
a long time. Despite being overlooked in the creation
of “Royal Prague,” ­Josefov was not entirely forgotten
– during the reign of Joseph II, the obligation to live
within the walls was abolished. The Jewish population began to conduct their business in various places
in Prague, and the poor started to move into Josefov
instead, tempted by very low rental prices. However, the
percentage of the Jewish population was still very high.
After the merger with the rest of Prague, the town
council was replaced by a Jewish council – responsible
for assimilation and social matters within the district.

M A L Á STR A NA
Malá Strana, or “Little Town,” was established near
Prague Castle. Its inhabitants lived in direct symbiosis with the fortress – they provided various services
to the castle’s staff, and those working in the castle
whose round-­the-­clock presence was not necessary
lived there. Malá Strana was also home to all those
who did not have room in the castle or could not find
a decent space (or space that would satisfy their needs,
for example, for a workshop). The district includes
Kampa, an island in the Vltava nicknamed “Prague’s
Venice” because of its dense housing and the boats
mooring at tiny piers of the waterfront townhouses.
Povídky malostranské, written by Jan Neruda, who
lived in the mid-19th century, is an extraordinarily
colorful account of life in Malá Strana. The story is
told from the perspective of the bourgeoisie and their
everyday life in this district, which makes this item
truly unique. It was published in English under the
