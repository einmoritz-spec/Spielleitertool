HR A D Č A N Y (PR AGUE CASTLE)

After unification, most of the Prague 1 district was
occupied by Prague Castle, the largest castle complex
in the world. In addition, Hradčany (Hradschin in German) included the Strachov Monastery, famous for
having one of the best libraries in Europe. On the
castle grounds lies the famous Nový Svět (New World)
street, whose tiny buildings have not changed much
in centuries, but unlike the crumbling buildings of
Josefov, are well maintained. The second famous
street is Zlatá (Golden) Street – for centuries, it was
inhabited by artisans working exclusively for the castle, including master masons and decorators who
actively participated in numerous reconstructions.
The town itself, however, was not of impressive
size – its density and small size were intended to
help with potential defense. The option of settling
in Hradčany was available only to those who were
directly needed for the day-­to-­day running of the
castle or monasteries, but did not require palaces or
tall tenement houses, for which there was physically
no room in this area
In the 19th century, much of Hradčany features the
redeveloped architecture from the previous century,
with a few remnants of earlier eras, such as the Nový
Svět and Zlatá streets. St. Vitus Cathedral towers over
the district, still formally unfinished.
On the other side of the dry moat are the royal
gardens, which include the historic orangery, a favorite place for meetings and strolls for Prague’s elite.

PRAGUE TODAY
This section describes the “newly created” Prague –
the city formed by the merger of five smaller towns.
During the 19th century, efforts were made to combine
the parts into a single urban organism, with the differences between them becoming smaller and smaller.
With the gradual unification of law throughout the
Empire, urban privileges lost much of their importance. Once again, the size of the city was crucial – it
determined the cost of labor and gave access to a vast
market for goods and services. With the loss of their
substantial privileges, small towns with centuries of
tradition ran into serious trouble – because of depopulation, lack of industrialization, and having no assets,
they were doomed to become marginalized. In some
cases, like Prague, neighboring towns merged now that
there was no longer a conflict of interests resulting
from unequal privileges (under the feudal system it
was impossible to merge towns on a partnership basis
– one would always take over the other and the privileges of the town being taken over would be lost). The
merger gave them all means to develop more intensely.

in a close symbiosis long before their unification in
1784. This intensified in the middle of the 19th century,
