Of course, after municipal privileges disappeared,
minority regulations were also relaxed, and Jews
were allowed to settle outside Josefov, which they
took advantage of where possible. It is important to
remember that we are talking about a society where
people were still “subjects of the emperor” and not
“citizens.” Legally, class differences blurred over time,
but the influential and wealthy could always do more
– they could afford better defenders and often bribes.
Prague was in a unique position because, in addition
to class differences, it was plagued by ethnic divisions.
The Czechs were trying to revive their statehood after
200 years, and the city was home to many Austrian
settlers who had assimilated into the surrounding
population over the centuries. On the other hand, a
sizable garrison was stationed in the city, and the most
important posts were filled by envoys from Vienna.
During any social unrest, the authorities feared an
armed uprising. This should be considered when
planning a mystery – actions of the player characters
may require particular caution.
Nineteenth-­century Prague began to industrialize rapidly – industrial plants of various sizes started
to emerge on the city’s outskirts and in the heavily
urbanized suburbs over the century. As light and
heavy industry and mining developed in the Czech
lands, plants of various sizes popped up in every district, producing an extensive range of products. Foreign investors – from England and Belgium, less frequently from France – played a major part, too. Often,
engineers from these countries were consultants in
establishing factories and, in times of shortages of
domestically produced machinery, acted as sales representatives for factories from their homeland. Player

The city’s architecture underwent significant
changes – many old buildings, sometimes even hundreds of years old, were demolished and replaced by
classicist and then Art Nouveau townhouses. The Old
Town and Josefov were also being prepared for major
razing – most buildings were to be demolished, and
the narrow medieval streets replaced by wide boulevards similar to those in Vienna.
The city developed very quickly. In the first half of
the century, street lighting in the form of gas lanterns
appeared in Malá Strana, and work began on the city’s
water supply and sewer systems –gradually, more and
more streets were being connected. After 1870, the
first private power stations began to appear – they
each supplied electricity to up to a dozen buildings.
The stability of these power supplies was mediocre,
but electric lighting and the first electrical household
appliances were a big sensation. Electrification proceeded very quickly (as did the whole industrialization
of the city), and by the end of the 19th century, most
of the city center already had electric street lighting.
Another invention related to electricity was the
tramway. The first permission to build the new transport system was granted to an English-­owned company in 1873, but due to financial problems, the
project didn’t come into fruition. Two years later,
however, a horse-­drawn tramway owned by a private
company took to the streets. The network grew quite
rapidly – while still in private hands, it exceeded 12
miles (or more than 15 kilometers) in length.
The railways came to the Czech territory very
quickly – the České Budejovice–Linz line was established as early as 1832. The wagons were pulled by
horses. It was the first permanent line of its kind in
Europe. However, in the same decade the horses were
replaced by steam locomotives. Initially, passenger
lines were of secondary importance to the Empire –
priority was given to industrial lines, especially those
leading to towns with mines or large industrial plants.
Private institutions were allowed to establish their
own connections.
