At the time, Pravda had several branches – in addition to Prague, they were also located in the cities of
Grodno, Lviv, and Osijek. These were equivalent
“head offices” that could host a council of representatives if necessary. Envoys were also active in other
cities – Vilnius, Kyiv, Krakow, Dresden, Belgrade,
Riga, Smolensk, and Buda – as permanent residents,
and in many others on an ad hoc basis.
Pravda’s ranks began to decline during the wars
and unrest sweeping through Europe in the 15th and
16th centuries. By the end of the 16th century, Prague
was the only seat of Pravda remaining, sending residents to various places in need. It was much easier
to explain the inexplicable as the actions of foreign
armies or the plague than by the activity of beings
other than humans.
HUSSITE TIMES

In 1415, a religious movement began in the Czech
lands with the burning at the stake of the religious
reformer Jan Hus. The movement gained strength
in 1417, and from 1419 onwards it became a proper
religious war. With the support of the papacy, the
German King Sigismund of Luxembourg organized
a series of crusades to restore religious order in the
lands of Bohemia and Moravia. By 1436, there had
been several such expeditions. Initially, the Hussites
achieved spectacular victories, leading raids far in
all directions. On many occasions, they defeated
an enemy two or even three times their size, a feat
considered almost impossible according to the art of
war. Catholics reporting on these clashes often saw
the Devil himself in the Hussite ranks. The members
of Pravda believed, however, that the victories were
not due to the Devil but to other mythical beings who
appeared among the ranks of the Hussites to prevent
the Inquisition from developing in Europe. Pravda
members also traveled around Europe then, making
contacts in Upsala and Basel, among other places.

On November 8, 1620, the Battle of White Mountain
took place near Prague. It was part of the Thirty Years’
War. The Bohemians, who were mostly Protestants,
clashed with the reigning Habsburgs and their Catholic allies, who systematically tried to force them back
to Catholicism. After a series of skirmishes, the decisive battle took place near Prague. On the surface, the
disparity did not look that serious – 28,000 Habsburg
troops (including 6,000 French soldiers) versus 22,000
Czech insurgents. Qualitatively, however, it was a gulf
– the army of the German Empire consisted entirely of
soldiers, including veterans and mercenaries. Most
of the Bohemian army consisted of standard, poorly
commanded, armed, and organized troops.
The decisive battle ended in total defeat for the
insurgents, with more than 5,000 dead on the battlefield, but far worse was what happened next.
Immediately after entering Prague, an ad hoc court
sentenced 27 of the most prominent Czech citizens
to death. Shortly afterward, 600 more shared their
fate. All properties belonging to Protestants were
confiscated. Czechs began to leave their lands en masse,
fleeing to other countries. Their largest diaspora was
in Leszno, located in Greater Poland (later western
Poland). Wherever they fled, they formed communities of “Czech brothers.” Within two years, about three
quarters of all Czechs left the Czech lands, including
about 95% of those who could read and write (most of
the nobility had died in battle or during persecution).
That led to the near extinction of culture, tradition,
and learning in the Czech language.
It was almost the end of the Pravda Society as well,
which for nearly 200 years lost its importance completely. It moved its activities to the Jizera Mountains,
which were considered an uninteresting destination
without much oversight from the Habsburgs, and at
the same time were not far from Prague, some 100
kilometers away. These lands had been inhabited for
centuries by Sorbian people, among others, so it was
no problem for any Slav to blend in among them.
