Door 3:
THE DA NCE OF DEATH

THE GHOST APPEARS
If the player characters have passed through
two of the doors and visited two of Linnaeus’s
memories but not yet figured out how to use
the floor relief and solve the puzzle, the ghost
can reveal itself and help them along.
A shimmering figure appears to the char­
acters. It is an older man wearing 18th cen­
tury clothes and wig. See the adjacent image
of Linnaeus’s ghost. The sight of him provokes
a Fear test against Fear 1 (lower than normal
as the ghost is not trying to scare them). He
apologizes for startling them, saying that he is
only trying to keep the curious away, but that
they have shown good intentions.
“To prove yourselves worthy to enter my
secret library, you must pass through the three
doors and face the challenges that await within.
Each challenge provides a clue to a symbol in
the circle on the floor in front of you. Three
symbols must be pressed simultaneously to open
the passage; one for each challenge.”
The ghost of Linnaeus can also appear and
admonish characters who are trying to fig­
ure out the right combination without going
through all three doors – after all, once they
have two symbols, there are only seven left to
choose from. Note that the mechanism locks if
they press the wrong combination of symbols,
and stays locked for an hour.

When the player characters pass through this door,
they are engulfed in darkness, as before, but soon
find themselves treading through dense bushes. They
arrive at a tall linden tree near a small village church
and its cemetery, situated on a slope down to a dark
river surrounded by deep woods.
The characters are in the same guise as in the previous memory, but much more practically dressed.
They carry simple camping gear but no weapons,
except for the character with the highest CLOSE COMBAT , who has a knife. Linnaeus himself is standing a
bit ahead of them. Crouching behind some bushes,
he once again greets the characters as his apostles.
Hedda von der Burg is here as well.
It is night, and the intense sound of violins echoes
in the darkness. Down by the riverbank is a clearing
where people are dancing in pairs, swirling around
four musicians in the moonlight. Several graves in
the cemetery appear to have been vandalized. The
earth has been dug up and the tombstones overturned.
Linnaeus signals for the characters to move closer
to the crowd down by the river. As they approach,
they see that the faces of the dancers are contorted
with terror and pain. The ground beneath them is
completely trampled, their feet bleeding or broken
at unnatural angles.
A man swings around a woman hanging limply
in his arms, seemingly lifeless. Farther away, they
see the rotting corpse of a man leading a terrified,
bridal-­clad woman in dance. Two small skeletons
of children in decaying clothes are standing in
front of the musicians, listening to the melody as
if entranced.
The faces of three of the musicians are filled with
fear. They play the stirring polka with bleeding fingers on their violins. Behind them, closest to the
river, stands a tall, dark-­haired man with piercing
eyes and a hat on his head. He smiles enigmatically
and carries a beautifully carved lyre.
“My apostles, we must save these poor souls, but be careful,”
warns Linnaeus.
